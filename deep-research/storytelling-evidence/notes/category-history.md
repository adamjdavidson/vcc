# Category Deep History as a Sales Story (Wine primary; Cheese and Bread secondary)

Note on sourcing: facts from Paul Kindstedt's *Cheese and Culture* come from the copy in the user's "Cheese Narrative" project files (cited as "Kindstedt, project file"). Everything else has a web URL. Where only an abstract or press summary was available, that is noted.

## 1. How do producers use deep history? (ancient origins, historic methods, varieties, regions, archaeology)

### Takeaway
Deep history is used in a few repeatable ways. The commonest is the archaeological "oldest" claim, as with Georgia's 8,000 years. Producers also invoke a historic vessel or method (qvevri, clothbound cheddar, mother dough), an institution (Cistercian monks, Alpine fruitières), or a legal and heritage stamp (UNESCO, PDO, Slow Food Presidium). The best-documented cases tie one physical, still-used object or practice to the long history. A bare date rarely carries the story on its own.

### Cited Findings
**Wine: Georgia (archaeology plus method plus UNESCO)**
- McGovern et al. (PNAS, 2017) found chemical residues of grape wine on pottery from two Georgian Neolithic sites, dated ca. 6,000–5,800 BC: "the earliest biomolecular archaeological evidence for grape wine and viniculture from the Near East." — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1714728114)
- The same paper adds caveats that the marketing usually drops. There is "no evidence" of how those Neolithic jars were positioned or whether they were buried. The earliest archaeological evidence for qvevri winemaking itself is Iron Age (8th–7th c. BC). So "8,000 years" refers to wine in Georgia, not to qvevri. — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1714728114)
- The research was not neutral: "The National Wine Agency of Georgia was the main supporter of this research." In 2014 the agency started a 3-year international project on Georgian grapes and wine culture. — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1714728114?doi=10.1073%2Fpnas.1714728114)
- McGovern to the NYT: "Here we have an 8,000-year-old vintage that we've identified." A scientist's quotable line like this becomes reusable marketing copy. — [New York Times, 2017](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/11/13/science/georgia-oldest-wine.html)
- Georgia layered on formal heritage status. Qvevri winemaking was put on Georgia's national intangible heritage registry in 2011 and on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2013. — [Wikipedia: Kvevri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kvevri). In 2021 the qvevri vessel itself got a Protected Geographical Indication, which the agency says was the first PGI for a non-consumable. — [PR Newswire / National Wine Agency, 2021](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/georgian-wine-exports-to-the-us-continue-to-post-gains-35-above-2020-301361395.html)
- The 2025 US campaign "Georgia, Naturally" "highlighted Georgia's heritage as the birthplace of wine." Georgian wine exports to the US grew at a 15.5% CAGR from 2021 to 2024 (figures from Wines of Georgia, an industry body, so self-reported). — [PR Newswire, Dec 2025](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/growing-2025-exports-to-the-us-underpinning-georgias-strong-sales-momentum-302636966.html)
- Earlier figures, also from the agency: US imports averaged about 30% year-on-year growth over five years to 2020, with 2019 up 46%. An importer credited qvevri wines, "most often natural wines from family wineries," with giving Georgia "their foothold in the U.S. market." — [PR Newswire, 2020](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/imports-of-georgian-wine-continue-to-climb-during-2020-301164023.html)

**Wine: Armenia (competing "cradle" claim)**
- Armenia markets itself as a "cradle of wine" too. It points to the Areni-1 cave, about 6,100 years old, the earliest *complete wine production* site (press, vats), plus Noah and Mount Ararat in Genesis and Urartian records. — [Smithsonian Folklife](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/smithsonian-center-folklife-cultural-heritage/2021/02/04/wine-expert-diary-roads-vayots-dzor); [BKWine](https://www.bkwine.com/features/wine-regions/explore-armenian-wines)
- Drinks Business (Aug 2026): "The karas has become the marketing emblem of Armenian wine's ancient credentials at precisely the moment when authentic vessels are running out." The supply comes from old rural families, abandoned farmyards and archaeological salvage. — [The Drinks Business, Aug 2026](https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2026/08/origin-story-armenian-wine-makes-its-long-awaited-comeback)
- Armenian producer Vahe Keushguerian sorts the "origin" case into "two routes. One is the mythological... Noah," and the other archaeological. — [Grape Collective, 2023](https://www.grapecollective.com/vahe-keushguerian-on-armenias-ancient-wine-history)

**Wine: Burgundy (monks, climats, UNESCO)**
- The Clos de Vougeot vineyard was built up by Cistercian monks of Cîteaux from donations made from 1109 to the early 14th century. — [Wikipedia: Clos de Vougeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clos_de_Vougeot)
- The regional tourism route sells the château as "built by Cistercian monks," on a parcel "planted with Pinot Noir since the 11th century," and now part of UNESCO's Climats listing (2015). — [Route des Grands Crus de Bourgogne](https://www.routedesgrandscrusdebourgogne.com/en/the-history-of-the-route-heritage-villages/unmissable-treasures/chateau-du-clos-de-vougeot)
- The Climats were inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List on 4 July 2015 as a cultural landscape "shaped by 2000 years of history." The trade body says the listing "will not just help promote the regional wine trade, but will also boost... wine tourism." — [Beaune Tourism](https://www.beaune-tourism.com/explore/surprising-burgundy-wines/the-climats-of-the-burgundy-vineyards); [Bourgogne Wines (BIVB)](https://www.bourgogne-wines.com/wine-and-terroir/our-climats-and-lieux-dits/world-heritage-status/the-climats-win-their-place-on-the-world-heritage-list,2466,9261.html)

**Cheese: Alpine, Roquefort, cheddar (institutions and methods)**
- Kindstedt: monastic orders (Benedictines, Cistercians, Templars) controlled grazing and salt routes for Roquefort. That influence "probably helped to elevate Roquefort," and the town was granted the first *appellation d'origine* in 1411. — Kindstedt, project file
- Kindstedt documents Swiss transhumant cheesemaking under the Abbey of Muri in the 11th century. Twelve households pooled cows under a master cowman, and cheese tithes fell due on St Andrew's Day. — Kindstedt, project file
- Comté uses its cooperative *fruitière* system as its heritage story. Official history dates it to 1253 at Déservillers. About 1,800 fruitières operated in the late 1800s; 134 make Comté today, each drawing on an average of 17 farms. — project kit file `alpine-cheese-history.md`, citing [European Commission Comté PDO](https://agriculture.ec.europa.eu/farming/geographical-indications-and-quality-schemes/geographical-indications-food-and-drink/comte-pdo_es) and [Comté USA](https://comte-usa.com/about-comte/in-the-cheesemaking-facility/)
- Somerset cheddar's heritage story is a decline-and-rescue narrative. Slow Food: "Barely 5% of the 400 producers who made Cheddar in... Somerset... a half-century ago remain in business." It blames postwar centralization, moisture rules, rindless blocks and pasteurization. — [Slow Food Foundation, Artisan Somerset Cheddar Presidium](https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/en/slow-food-presidia/artisan-somerset-cheddar)
- The Presidium was "formed by a few Somerset cheesemakers" to protect the product. Makers used a heritage designation as a collective branding tool. — [The Cheese Professor, 2024](https://www.cheeseprofessor.com/blog/somerset-cheddar-cheesemakers)

**Bread: lineage starters and heritage grains**
- Boudin Bakery (San Francisco) builds its brand on a living object: "We bake a slice of history into every loaf... using a portion of the original mother dough we've nurtured for over 166 years." It also claims "It's believed that Isidore Boudin received the mother dough from one of these 49ers." That is hedged language on its own timeline. — [Boudin: Our Bread](https://boudinbakery.com/our-story/our-bread/); [Boudin: Our History](https://boudinbakery.com/our-story/our-history)
- Boudin trademarks "The Original San Francisco Sourdough™" and claims to be "San Francisco's oldest continuously operating business." The flagship is designed as a place to "learn the full story." — [Boudin: Our Story](https://boudinbakery.com/our-story); [SF Legacy Business Registry report](https://www.sf.gov/sites/default/files/2024-11/item_3b._lbr-2016-17-066_boudin_bakery.pdf)
- Heritage-grain bakers tell a regional rather than ancient story. Maine Grains (founded 2012) cites Somerset County as "the bread basket of New England in the 19th century," producing 239,000 bushels of wheat in 1837. — [Maine Grains: Our Story](https://mainegrains.com/pages/our-story)
- Tucson baker Don Guerra (Barrio Bread) ties White Sonora wheat to Spanish introduction in the mid-16th century and to the Jesuit Father Kino in the 1690s. He names a loaf "Pan de Kino" after him. — [AFAR, 2024](https://www.afar.com/magazine/how-baker-don-guerra-is-preserving-sonoran-heritage-one-bread-loaf-at-a-time)
- White Sonora is on Slow Food USA's Ark of Taste. Its revival is told as a network story of farmers, millers, bakers and brewers. — [Slow Food USA](https://slowfoodusa.org/white-sonora-wheat-adding-heritage-grains-into-the-local-foods-mix)
- An industry R&D executive: "There's a perception that [ancient grains] haven't been tampered with... Ancient grains remain true to their past." He calls it a "perception," not a fact. — [Baking Business, 2023](https://www.bakingbusiness.com/articles/58913-ancient-and-local-grains-driving-artisan-baking)

### Inferences
- The strongest deep-history stories hang on a tangible, still-used artifact: a qvevri, a karas, a mother dough, a clothbound wheel, a copper vat. The artifact links the distant past to the product in hand. Dates alone are weaker. This matches Beverland's "method of production" attribute (see section 3).
- Scientific and institutional validation (PNAS, UNESCO, PDO, PGI, Slow Food) acts as third-party proof. In Georgia's case the state funded the science. That did not visibly hurt the claim in market, but it is a vulnerability.
- The "oldest" claim invites rival claims (Armenia vs Georgia). It works as a door-opener, not as differentiation within a region.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found that isolates the sales effect of an "8,000 years" claim from the natural-wine tailwind. The export growth figures are correlational and come from interested industry bodies.
- No hard data was found on Boudin's mother-dough claim. The bakery itself hedges its 49er origin.

## 2. How do new producers (e.g., US Alpine-style cheese, Vermont hybrid wine) connect to deep history credibly? What works vs. borrowed authenticity?

### Takeaway
The credible pattern is to borrow method and inspiration explicitly while claiming your own place. Uplands says it is "not trying to copy an old-world cheese"; Thistle Hill picked Tarentaise because the climate matched. New producers who have no inherited tradition do better framing themselves as pioneers of a new terroir, as Paxson describes in "reverse engineering terroir," or as reviving a *local* lost history (New England grain), than claiming lineage. Hybrid-grape winemakers mostly lean on resilience, place and "American" identity, and use genetic pedigree (Marquette as Pinot's grandchild) as a secondary hook.

### Cited Findings
- Uplands Cheese (Wisconsin) says Pleasant Ridge Reserve "is made in the tradition of Alpine cheeses like Gruyere and Beaufort. Like the Alpage versions... we only make Pleasant Ridge Reserve from May through October when our cows are eating fresh pasture." When pasture conditions are not ideal, "we sell the milk." — [Uplands: Our Cheese](https://uplandscheese.com/our-cheese)
- Andy Hatch of Uplands: "'alpine' is sort of like saying a beer is a lager, it just kind of identifies the techniques... We gave it our own name because the point is to try to make a cheese that tastes like our farm, so we didn't want to call it Beaufort, we weren't trying to copy an old-world cheese. But that is the inspiration... the techniques are borrowed from those traditions." — [Zingerman's Deli, 2023](https://www.zingermansdeli.com/2023/10/pleasant-ridge-reserve)
- Pleasant Ridge Reserve has won ACS Best of Show three times (2001, 2005, 2010) and the 2003 US Championship. Uplands calls it "the most-awarded cheese in American history." Awards serve as the new producer's substitute for centuries of reputation. — [Uplands product page](https://uplandscheese.com/product/pleasant-ridge-reserve)
- culture magazine roots Uplands' practice in Vermont's late-1980s rotational grazing rather than in Europe. That is a short, local, true history. — [culture](https://culturecheesemag.com/stories/art-and-craft)
- Thistle Hill Farm (Vermont) toured Alpine cheesemakers in 2000. It chose the Tarentaise style because the Alpine climate is the closest match to Vermont's, and it uses a Swiss-built copper vat. — project kit file `alpine-cheese-history.md`, citing [NPR 2007](https://www.npr.org/2007/08/30/13981929/cheesemakers-taste-a-change-in-the-weather) and [Thistle Hill](https://www.thistlehillfarm.com/about)
- Paxson (*American Anthropologist*, 2010): US artisan cheesemakers adapt terroir "to reveal the range of values—agrarian, environmental, social, and gastronomic" behind their cheese. "Some see themselves as reverse engineering terroir": they ask what cheese would best express a landscape rather than inheriting one. This "may work to naturalize entrepreneurial innovation, making it seem a part" of the land. — [Paxson 2010 PDF](https://files.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/13216/files/2020/10/Paxson-2010-Locating-Value-in-Artisan-Cheese-Reverse-Engineering-Terroir-for-New-World-Landscapes.pdf)
- In the same paper a skeptic at the American Cheese Society says that unless "people off the street can tell the difference" between, say, Vermont and Wisconsin cheddar, terroir talk "crosses the line into marketing." — [Paxson 2010](https://files.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/13216/files/2020/10/Paxson-2010-Locating-Value-in-Artisan-Cheese-Reverse-Engineering-Terroir-for-New-World-Landscapes.pdf)
- Paxson's book (*The Life of Cheese*, 2012) has a chapter titled "Traditions of Invention." She calls handmade cheeses "unfinished" commodities that "embody a mix of new and old ideas about taste and value." Reviewer Amy Trubek calls her stance a "post-pastoral" food ethos. — [UC Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-life-of-cheese/paper)
- Kindstedt contrasts Europe, where traditional cheeses leave "deep cultural imprints... embedded in the human landscape," with the US, "where cheese history spans fewer centuries." He notes the American revival relies on buyers paying "two, three, five, even ten times" commodity prices. — Kindstedt, project file
- La Garagista (Vermont) frames itself as "The Outer Edge of American Wine... growing resilient American vines in Vermont and making homespun bottles." The site uses no deep-history claim. — [La Garagista](https://www.lagaragista.com/)
- Deirdre Heekin describes "a lot of skepticism" toward hybrids, especially in Vermont, and says they have become "exciting and intriguing" over the past decade. Peers frame hybrids around identity ("Why wouldn't we try and use our own grapes and really try to define what American wine can be like?") and climate resilience. Modern Farmer had to correct an earlier version that called hybrids "indigenous" and "native." Overreaching on heritage language gets caught. — [Modern Farmer, 2021](https://modernfarmer.com/2021/05/where-did-americas-native-grapes-go/)
- A Grape Collective interview with Heekin quotes a critic who argued that places like the Hudson Valley "should not be making wine... they should be focusing on apples," because "in France and Italy, there's a climate and a tradition for grape growing." This is the authenticity objection new regions face. — [Grape Collective, 2021](https://www.grapecollective.com/deirdre-heekin-of-la-garagista-and-the-unlikely-tale-of-vermont-wine)
- Marquette (Univ. of Minnesota, crossed 1989, released 2006) is promoted with the pedigree hook "Marquette Wine Has Pinot In Its Blood": Ravat 262, one parent, is a Pinot Noir offspring. The grape is patented and licensed. — [Midwest Wine Press](https://midwestwinepress.com/?p=19191)
- Sonoma's Caleb Leisure, described as the only American fermenting in genuine Georgian qvevri, says "Qvevri really jump-started my whole interest in wine." On Georgian advice: "I don't think there is a best way." He shows humility rather than claiming mastery of the tradition. — [SF Chronicle](https://preview-prod.w.sfchronicle.com/food/wine/article/In-Sonoma-a-natural-winemaker-looks-to-the-14307011.php)
- Oregon's Jeff Vejr (Golden Cluster) "makes biannual pilgrimages to the Republic of Georgia" to study its techniques. He ferments outdoors, Georgian style. — [SevenFifty Daily](https://daily.sevenfifty.com/why-we-should-be-talking-about-open-air-winemaking)
- Beverland (2006): heritage claims "were both real and stylized versions of the truth," and consumers accepted them. — [Journal of Business Research](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296305000603)
- Beverland & Farrelly (consumer authenticity study): one informant saw through large breweries that "bought the rights of old Abbeys or even invented Abbeys to give their beers an aura of authenticity... because the cues do not reinforce a sense of continued tradition." — [Cardiff ORCA PDF](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/19883/1/Article%2010.pdf)

### Inferences
- Credibility patterns for new producers:
  - Name the tradition you learned from, and say where your version departs from it.
  - Show the *practice* (seasonal milk, copper vat, buried qvevri, pilgrimages), not just the label.
  - Claim a local, verifiable history where one exists: Vermont grazing, New England's grain past, Wisconsin's Swiss immigrants.
  - Use third-party validation (awards, Slow Food) in place of age.
- Borrowed authenticity is detected through a break in continuity: a cue that does not connect to an ongoing practice (Beverland & Farrelly's invented abbeys), or category language that overreaches (hybrids called "native").
- Hybrid-grape wine has no deep vinifera history to borrow. Its strongest stories so far are about place, resilience and pioneering, with genetic ancestry as a light hook.

### Gaps
- No consumer experiment was found that directly compares "made in the tradition of X" against no-heritage framing for a New World artisan product.
- No source was found on how hybrid-wine buyers respond to pre-Prohibition American wine history (Norton, 19th-century American viticulture) as a story. Worth a targeted search if needed.

## 3. What does research say about heritage, tradition, nostalgia and brand-heritage appeals?

### Takeaway
There is solid experimental and survey evidence that heritage cues, including founding dates, raise perceived quality, credibility and willingness to pay, especially for *less familiar* brands. Nostalgic labels raise purchase intent for foods that are already nostalgic. There are documented limits. Heritage tied to specific product features can penalize innovation (the "curse of the original"), consumers accept innovation in traditional foods only if it does not erode the traditional character, and GI premiums are smaller for wine than for basic commodities.

### Cited Findings
**Foundational concept**
- Urde, Greyser & Balmer (2007, *Journal of Brand Management* 15(1):4–19) define a heritage brand as "one with a positioning and value proposition based on its heritage." Their "heritage quotient" elements are track record, longevity, core values, use of symbols, and history important to identity. — [Greyser/Urde, HBS PDF](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Greyser_Nobel_Prize_JPBM_94a1b94a-6997-4f55-92fc-3cc9872f122d.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267834420_Corporate_brands_with_a_heritage)
- "Although all companies have histories and many even have heritage, only some set heritage at the core of their brand identities." — [Journal of Brand Management, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-021-00243-9)
- The same 2021 semiotic study found consumers want "traditional quality, modern at the same time." They assume "the standard of the past is somehow updated currently." — [Journal of Brand Management, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-021-00243-9)

**Heritage as a quality signal (experimental)**
- Pecot et al., "Cognitive outcomes of brand heritage: a signaling perspective" (*Journal of Business Research*, 2018). Brand heritage "enhances perceived brand quality and commands a price premium for established companies (Study 1) as well as companies penetrating a new market (Study 2)." Familiarity and consumers' past-time orientation moderate the effect. The heritage symbols tested included font, founding date, headquarters and signature. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S014829631830016X)
- Pizzi & Scarpi (2019, *Journal of Consumer Marketing* 36(6)): showing the year of establishment in a logo raises perceived heritage, which fully mediates better brand attitudes. Older dates work better, and the effect is stronger for *less-known* brands. The study used 12 university brands (n=250) and 12 beer brands (n=200). The authors flag that in categories like hi-tech an old date could read as "outdated." — [Univ. of Bologna repository abstract](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/690786)
- Baumert & de Obesso (2021, *Journal of Business Research* 123:241–254): in an experiment, brand antiquity communicated in the logo ("Since…") had a significant effect on the prices consumers would pay. The proposed mechanism is that survival signals quality. Only the abstract was available. — [Complutense repository](https://docta.ucm.es/entities/publication/70adfe40-095f-4f82-a2d2-7b478fc86c69/full)
- Wiedmann et al. (2011, structural model): heritage adds "depth, authenticity and credibility" and can lead to loyalty and "willingness to accept higher prices." — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-8349-3722-3_27)

**Limits: the "curse of the original"**
- Han, Newman, Smith & Dhar (*Journal of Consumer Research*). With a brand framed as founded in 1917, an *enhanced* hand cream was rated significantly worse than the original; with a 2017 founding there was no difference (400+ participants). For a fictional tomato sauce (n=602), the penalty grew when the new sauce was framed as a "bold departure" and nearly disappeared when it was framed as a "return to the brand's origins." Heritage tied to values (e.g., family ownership) did not trigger the backlash; heritage tied to specific product features did. — [UGA Terry summary](https://www.terry.uga.edu/how-heritage-brands-can-sidestep-curse-original); [Illinois Experts](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/the-curse-of-the-original-how-and-when-heritage-branding-reduces-/)

**Authenticity in wine specifically (qualitative)**
- Beverland (2005, *Journal of Management Studies*; 26 luxury wine firms): authenticity required "a sincere story" built from "public avowal of hand crafted techniques, uniqueness, relationship to place, passion," while disavowing "commercial motives, rational production methods, and the use of modern marketing techniques." Firms deliberately decoupled "their technical core from their espoused communications." — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-6486.2005.00530.x)
- Beverland (2006, *Journal of Business Research*; 20 ultra-premium wineries, 30 consumers) names six attributes: "heritage and pedigree, stylistic consistency, quality commitments, relationship to place, method of production, and downplaying commercial motives." They "resonated with consumers." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296305000603)

**Tradition claims in food (consumer research)**
- Guerrero et al. (2009, *Appetite*; Belgium, France, Italy, Norway, Poland, Spain): consumers see traditional foods as "simple products, with low complexity... basic, natural, and pure." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0195666308006065)
- Guerrero et al. (2010; 721 consumers, free word association): "traditional" in food has ten dimensions, including sensory, health, elaboration, heritage and variety. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0950329309000858)
- Vanhonacker et al. (2013; n=4,828, six countries): innovations in traditional foods are accepted only if they bring tangible benefits without "harming the intrinsic traditional character." Such innovations "would not likely attract new consumers," though existing traditional-food consumers accepted them. — [Food Research International](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0963996913005759)
- Balogh et al. (2016): traditional foods "can command a substantial premium, albeit contingent on effective quality certification, authentic product..." (snippet only). — [Food Policy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0306919216300161)
- Deselnicu et al. (2013, meta-analysis of GI valuation studies): GI premiums are largest for short-supply-chain, low-value-added commodities and smaller for wine and olive oil, "where other differentiation tools, such as branding, are available." Strict PDO rules earn bigger premiums than the looser PGI. — [AgEcon Search](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/158285)

**Nostalgia**
- Zhou et al. (2019, *Appetite*; four experiments, China and US): nostalgic food labels raise purchase intent "when such items are intrinsically nostalgic" (e.g., tied to childhood), through a sense of "food comfort." Restaurant dishes with nostalgic labels were more likely to be ordered. — [Southampton PDF](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/~crsi/Zhou,%20X.,%20Van%20Tilburg,%20W.%20A.%20P.,%20Mei,%20D.,%20Wildschut,%20T.,%20&%20Sedikides,%20C.%20(2019)..pdf); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0195666318317070)
- A 2025 study (*Journal of Global Marketing*, n=132) found nostalgia raised Gen Z's willingness to pay by about 13% for non-nostalgic brands and about 25% for nostalgic brands. Gen X showed higher purchase intent but no clear WTP link. The sample is small. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08911762.2025.2493239)

**Critique of tradition romance**
- Rachel Laudan, "A Plea for Culinary Modernism" (*Gastronomica*, 2001): the "sunlit past" of traditional food "never existed." The backlash against processed food is "based not on history but on a fairy tale." Rural people historically ate "thin gruels and gritty flatbreads." — [Mother Jones summary, 2015](https://www.motherjones.com/food/2015/05/romanticizing-grandparents-food/)

### Inferences
- For small, unknown producers, the research points one way: heritage cues help most when the buyer does not already know the brand (Pizzi & Scarpi; Pecot et al.). A small producer with no founding-date heritage can still borrow *category* heritage, which may serve the same signaling role. This is my extrapolation; no study tested category heritage specifically.
- The curse-of-the-original finding implies that heritage anchored in values and practice, rather than in a fixed recipe, leaves room to innovate. It also suggests "return to origins" framing makes novelty palatable, which is exactly how amphora and natural wine are framed (section 6).
- Nostalgia evidence covers *personal* nostalgia (childhood foods), not historical nostalgia for eras the buyer never lived through. Those are different mechanisms and should not be conflated.
- Most of this research uses large or fictional brands. Applying it to artisanal food is plausible, but direct tests are thin.

### Gaps
- No experimental study was found that isolates "thousands-of-years" *category* history (vs. company founding date) as a cue for an individual artisan product.
- The Hakala, Lätti & Sandberg (2011) Finnish food-brand heritage paper could not be accessed; its abstract was blocked.
- The "Discovering the 'Since Effect'" paper is cited in Pecot et al. but its findings were not retrieved.

## 4. Heritage as tourism: wine routes, cheese trails, museums, and history-telling at a place

### Takeaway
Regions turn deep history into places people can visit: the UNESCO Climats with the Château du Clos de Vougeot, Boudin's flagship museum-bakery, the Vermont Cheese Trail. Research shows cellar-door visits are followed by purchases for roughly half of visitors. Taste, memory and service drive those purchases, and no study found isolates history-telling as the driver.

### Cited Findings
- Château du Clos de Vougeot: classified a Historic Monument and open to the public year-round, with guided tours through "the history of the château over the centuries," framed by Cistercian origins and the UNESCO listing. — [Route des Grands Crus](https://www.routedesgrandscrusdebourgogne.com/en/the-history-of-the-route-heritage-villages/unmissable-treasures/chateau-du-clos-de-vougeot)
- The UNESCO Climats management plan includes a commission on "mediation & tourism." The trade body expects the listing to "boost... wine tourism." — [BIVB](https://www.bourgogne-wines.com/wine-and-terroir/our-climats-and-lieux-dits/world-heritage-status/the-climats-win-their-place-on-the-world-heritage-list,2466,9261.html)
- Boudin's Fisherman's Wharf flagship is described as the "Home of our mother dough" and the place to "learn the full story of our famous sourdough bread and the city that made it possible." — [Boudin](https://boudinbakery.com/our-story/our-bread/)
- Vermont's tourism board promotes 45+ cheesemakers making 150+ varieties along the Vermont Cheese Trail. — [Vermont Tourism](https://vermontvacation.com/things-to-do/food-drink/cheese)
- Mitchell (Otago PhD; 33 New Zealand wineries; follow-up 6–8 months later): "almost half of the respondents made a post-visit purchase." The drivers were mainly taste, then sharing the experience, memory of the visit and service. Wine education and interpretation mattered to many visitors. He coins the term "touristic terroir." — [Otago PDF](https://www.otago.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0025/263662/mitchell-richard-064449.pdf)
- O'Mahony et al. (2006; 207 cellar-door visitors in Australia, 73 re-interviewed five months later) studied cellar-door influence on later purchase. Only the methods were retrievable, not the results. — [ADU repository](https://repository.adu.ac.ae/items/1efc58a0-d8d7-485e-be96-80c7bbc7ac93/full)
- A Georgian study of family wine tourism ties the sector's appeal to the UNESCO qvevri listing, alongside export growth (43.4M litres to 56 countries in H1 2023, +27%). — [Georgian academic journal, 2025](https://journals.4science.ge/index.php/GGJ/article/download/4128/4204/6223)
- Burgundy's Confrérie des Chevaliers du Tastevin, founded in 1934 amid unsold stock during the Depression, has met since 1945 at the Clos de Vougeot. It ran ritual banquets and inducted diplomats and celebrities, and opened chapters in Paris, London, New York and Tokyo. A French write-up says that "before marketing became a science, the Confrérie had understood the importance of the story" (translated). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confr%C3%A9rie_des_Chevaliers_du_Tastevin); [Vu d'ailleurs, 2026](https://www.vudailleurs.com/confrerie-chevaliers-tastevin-bourgogne-histoire)

### Inferences
- Visiting a heritage site turns abstract history into personal memory. Mitchell's finding that "memory of the visit" drives later purchase suggests that place-based storytelling works through memory, not only through information.
- Small North American producers cannot build a Clos de Vougeot. They can share regional infrastructure (cheese trails, wine routes), and one tangible artifact on site, such as a vat, a cave or a starter, can anchor the history-telling.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures whether *historical* interpretation, specifically, increases purchase at a tasting room or creamery compared with tasting alone.
- No published data was found on Vermont Cheese Trail visitation or its sales effects.

## 5. Concrete examples: effective uses, and ones criticized as kitsch, false or invented

### Takeaway
Many of the most successful "ancient tradition" stories were partly constructed in the modern era: Burgundy's 1930s folklorization, the monk-tasting-soil legend, Dom Pérignon's "tasting stars." That has not stopped them working commercially. Outright fabrication of *specific provenance* (Tesco's fictional farms) draws public backlash and legal threats. The line seems to fall between stylized history of a real tradition, which buyers tolerate, and false claims about where *this product* comes from, which they punish.

### Cited Findings
**Effective (commercially)**
- Georgia: the qvevri and 8,000-year story gave the country "an identity the market could immediately grasp." US shipments reached 1.07 million liters in 2024 (+58% by volume), and the US pays about $6 per liter, "among the highest premiums for Georgian wine globally." — [SevenFifty Daily, Apr 2026](https://daily.sevenfifty.com/georgian-wine-looks-beyond-its-amber-identity)
- Simon J. Woolf (*Amber Revolution*) on qvevri as Georgia's emblem: "We all need something to hang a country on a peg." He compares it to Tokaj for Hungary. — [SevenFifty Daily, 2026](https://daily.sevenfifty.com/georgian-wine-looks-beyond-its-amber-identity)
- Burgundy's Confrérie du Tastevin (1934) was set up as a promotional response to a sales collapse. It revived "traditional Bacchic confraternities to stimulate market interest and sales." Its robes, banners and rituals are a deliberately staged heritage. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confr%C3%A9rie_des_Chevaliers_du_Tastevin); [Vu d'ailleurs](https://www.vudailleurs.com/confrerie-chevaliers-tastevin-bourgogne-histoire)

**Constructed or contested history that still works**
- Historian Gilles Laferté describes 1930s Burgundy as "commercial regionalist folklore." Republican elites "invented a marketing strategy for their wines based upon the traditional images of wine-grower, terroir and authenticity," shifting from a bourgeois image to a "folkloric and peasant based regional marketing." — [H-France review by Demossier, 2009](https://www.h-france.net/vol9reviews/vol9no18demossier.pdf); [Laferté, JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40990595)
- Historian Marion Foucher (ARTéHis, Dijon) calls the 19th-century postcard image of habited monks working vines "completely false." Lay brothers and, from the 14th century, hired laborers did the work. Monks "tasting the soil" is a modern invention, and the Cistercians never made a "Clos de Vougeot" wine; they blended estate-wide as *vin de Cîteaux*. — [Wine Scholar Guild](https://www.winescholarguild.com/blog/wine-fundamentals/researcher-sheds-light-on-true-role-of-monks-in-burgundy)
- Anthropologist Marion Demossier (*Burgundy: The Global Story of Terroir*, 2018) argues for anthropology as "a science of critique and not a contributor to myth-making." She shows authenticity in Burgundy being constructed "through encounters with the site of production." — [EuropeNow review](https://www.europenowjournal.org/2018/11/07/burgundy-global-anthropology-of-taste-and-place-by-marion-demossier)
- The Dom Pérignon legend: the "come quickly, I am tasting stars!" line probably comes from a late-1800s print ad. Many myths trace to an 1821 account by a later Hautvillers cellarer, Dom Groussard. Moët & Chandon bought the name in 1937 for its prestige cuvée. — [History Hit](https://www.historyhit.com/dom-perignon-the-champagne-monk/)
- The ploughman's lunch is a food "tradition" with a marketing origin. The term is believed to date from the 1950s, when the Cheese Bureau promoted it in pubs to boost cheese sales after rationing ended, and the Milk Marketing Board pushed it nationally in the 1960s. Wikipedia calls the marketing-origin claim "debatable" and cites a 1956 pub reference to a "Ploughboy's Lunch." — [Wikipedia: Ploughman's lunch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ploughman's_lunch)
- Hobsbawm's "invented tradition" framework: movements create "an ancient past beyond effective historical continuity... by semi-fiction." — [Hobsbawm, Inventing Traditions PDF](https://staff.washington.edu/ellingsn/Hobsbawm_Inventing_Traditiions.pdf)

**Fell flat / backlash**
- Tesco's 2016 "fictional farms" range (Woodside, Boswell, Rosedene and others) used British-sounding farm names. A Farmers Weekly spot check found Rosedene products from Belgium, Spain and Chile. Farmers' groups called the names misleading. — [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-35889282); [The Independent](https://prod-mw.independent.co.uk/news/business/news/tesco-willow-boswell-rosedene-redmere-made-up-fictional-farms-a6949801.html)
- Michelin-starred chef JP McMahon called it an "insult to real farmers." Aldi, M&S (Oakham chicken, not from Oakham) and SuperValu used similar invented-provenance brands. — [Irish Farmers Journal](https://www.farmersjournal.ie/news/news/tesco-under-fire-for-fake-farms-but-they-are-nothing-new-205027)
- In 2017 a food charity threatened legal action over UK supermarkets' "fake farm" branding as misleading. — [The Guardian, 2017](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2017/dec/13/tesco-faces-legal-threat-over-marketing-its-food-with-fake-farm-names)
- Consumers spot invented abbey branding on beers "because the cues do not reinforce a sense of continued tradition." — [Beverland & Farrelly, Cardiff PDF](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/19883/1/Article%2010.pdf)

**Success that became a cage**
- SevenFifty (2026): qvevri wines are "barely five percent of Georgia's commercial production," yet in the US they "threaten to dictate the entire narrative." Winemakers call this the "amber ceiling." Patrick Honnef of Chateau Mukhrani: "Georgia is qvevri. That's it. And it hurts my heart." He likens it to selling German wine "with Bavarian lederhosen and bratwurst": "not the image of a serious wine culture. It is the image of an emotional experience." — [SevenFifty Daily, Apr 2026](https://daily.sevenfifty.com/georgian-wine-looks-beyond-its-amber-identity)
- The same article worries about commercial wineries "chase[ing] the amber trend" and diluting the category "until consumers lose all sense of what authentic amber wine actually is." Saveur notes conventional wineries "cashing in on the kvevri craze with European varieties and industrial yeasts." — [SevenFifty](https://daily.sevenfifty.com/georgian-wine-looks-beyond-its-amber-identity); [Saveur, 2025](https://www.saveur.com/wine/georgian-wine-tradition-new-generation)

### Inferences
- Commercially, a story need not be historically pure. Burgundy, Champagne and the ploughman's lunch show that partly invented traditions can last for generations. The risk is reputational when debunked, not necessarily commercial.
- What triggers backlash is a false claim about *this* product's provenance (a farm that doesn't exist, beef from elsewhere) or a cue with no living practice behind it. A romanticized story about a real, continuing practice gets far more latitude.
- A powerful deep-history story can over-define a category (the amber ceiling), which crowds out a producer's other products.

### Gaps
- No documented case was found of a *small artisan* producer facing public backlash specifically for overclaiming ancient lineage. Most backlash cases involve large retailers.
- The quantified commercial effect of debunkings (e.g., the Burgundy monk myths) is unknown.

## 6. How long history interacts with novelty: "ancient method, new place" (amphora in California, natural wine as return to pre-industrial practice)

### Takeaway
The ancient story is most often used to package *novelty* for today's buyers. Amber/orange and amphora wine is new to most drinkers but sold as the oldest method, and natural wine is framed as undoing industrial intervention. This matches the research finding that a "return to origins" framing softens resistance to change. The pioneers paid an early price: Gravner lost critical favor before a market formed.

### Cited Findings
- Josko Gravner (Friuli) turned away from high-tech winemaking after a 1987 Napa visit, then after hail destroyed 95% of his 1996 crop. He resolved to "go to the source" in Georgia, buying qvevri in 2000; 2001 was his first full amphora vintage. "We don't control the temperature, and we don't add anything." — [Grape Collective](https://www.grapecollective.com/another-way-is-possible-josko-gravner-and-the-never-ending-quest-for-improvement/)
- The market reaction was harsh at first. His wines had held top Italian ratings every year, but after the switch "the reviews were really terrible." Sommeliers used them in classes as examples of problems, before a segment of the market embraced them. — [Grape Collective](https://www.grapecollective.com/another-way-is-possible-josko-gravner-and-the-never-ending-quest-for-improvement/)
- Whetstone: Georgian wine "is very of the moment in that it's based on ancient techniques, focuses on unique varietals and natural winemaking, and it offers profoundly new characteristics." For many people, "drinking white wine with structure and tannins didn't happen until they tried Georgian wine." — [Whetstone Magazine, 2021](https://www.whetstonemagazine.com/journal/the-story-of-georgian-wine)
- Buyers credit the natural-wine movement and interest in clay-fermented and skin-contact wines with providing "tailwinds" for Georgia's offerings, conventional wines included. — [SevenFifty Daily, 2021](https://daily.sevenfifty.com/understanding-the-georgian-wine-boom)
- SevenFifty frames US open-air and Georgian-style fermentation: "As winemakers return to traditional methods, they're thinking outside the building." — [SevenFifty Daily](https://daily.sevenfifty.com/why-we-should-be-talking-about-open-air-winemaking)
- Critics of natural wine "question why anyone would willfully ignore modern winemaking techniques." They cite faster spoilage and bottle variation; some detractors "prefer the words scam and cult." — [Portland Mercury, 2018](https://www.portlandmercury.com/lush-life/2018/01/24/19626323/respect-my-authority-the-debate-over-natural-wines)
- Laudan's broader critique applies: pre-industrial food was often worse, and the romantic past is "a fairy tale." — [Mother Jones summary](https://www.motherjones.com/food/2015/05/romanticizing-grandparents-food/)
- The research mechanism: framing a new product as "a return to the brand's origins" nearly eliminated the heritage penalty on innovation. — [UGA Terry summary of JCR study](https://www.terry.uga.edu/how-heritage-brands-can-sidestep-curse-original)
- Heritage grains follow the same pattern. White Sonora is described as having "suddenly become a game-changer... for stimulating innovation across the entire food supply chain." Ancient grain sold as new. — [Slow Food USA](https://slowfoodusa.org/white-sonora-wheat-adding-heritage-grains-into-the-local-foods-mix)
- Uplands' Rush Creek Reserve (in the style of Vacherin Mont d'Or, from fall milk, wrapped in spruce bark) applies an old seasonal method to a new product, in a place without that tradition. — [Cheese Professor](https://www.cheeseprofessor.com/blog/pleasant-ridge-reserve); [culture](https://culturecheesemag.com/stories/art-and-craft)

### Inferences
- "Ancient method, new place" works because it lets novelty-seekers try something new while getting the reassurance of tradition, and it lets the producer frame departures from current industrial norms as continuity.
- For North American producers this is probably the most usable frame. They claim the method's deep history, not the place's, and make the place the new chapter.
- A first mover with an ancient-method story may face a period of critical rejection (Gravner) before the market catches up, and the story may then become a category cliché (the amber ceiling).

### Gaps
- No consumer data was found on whether US buyers of amphora or orange wine know or care about the Georgian history, as opposed to the style itself.
- No research was found that specifically tests the natural-wine "pre-industrial" narrative on purchase decisions.
- Only a thin evidence trail was found on California amphora producers beyond Leisure. Wider sampling of producer websites would help.
