# Vermont cheese, 1985–2026: how a small state became a world cheese place

*Background for any Vermont maker who wants to place their cheese in the larger story of Vermont's last 40 years: the artisan revival, the institutions, the awards, and the hard economics underneath. Sources are listed at the end. Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012) supplies the national context.*

---

## The short version

In 1985 Vermont was a dairy state in long decline, and American cheese was almost entirely a factory product. Over the next 40 years a few dozen makers, most of them on small farms, turned Vermont into the place people in the cheese world name first when they talk about American artisan cheese. That reputation runs as far as international juries and Swiss award halls.

The Vermont story has three parts that pull against each other:
- **The farms kept disappearing.** Vermont had about 2,770 dairy farms in 1987 and 439 in 2025.
- **The cheese kept getting better and more celebrated.**
- **It was done collectively:** a council, a university institute, a shared aging cellar, cooperatives large and small.

---

## The backdrop: what the revival grew out of

- **The factory century.** Kindstedt describes 20th-century American cheese as a race to the bottom on cost. Cheese came from bigger plants, wetter cheese, shorter aging and blander flavor. Cheeses that couldn't scale disappeared. By the 1970s Vermont's historic cheesemaking survived in a handful of places: Crowley, Plymouth, Grafton, Cabot.
- **The dairy collapse.**

  | Year | Vermont dairy farms |
  |---|---|
  | 1947 | 11,206 |
  | 1967 | 4,729 |
  | 1987 | 2,771 |
  | 2007 | 1,168 |
  | 2025 | 439 |

  - Vermont's Secretary of Agriculture noted the state lost half its dairy farms in the ten years to 2025.
  - In 2026 four milk plants closed or idled, including DFA's St. Albans creamery. The secretary's summary: "We essentially need more cows."
- **The national revival.** Kindstedt dates the artisan comeback to the 1970s and early 1980s. Small makers committed to handmade methods, raw milk and European styles that had vanished from America. They founded the American Cheese Society (ACS) in 1983. Big industry saw them as a food-safety disaster waiting to happen.
- **A friendly state.** Kindstedt has credited Vermont's Agency of Agriculture with being friendly to small cheesemakers since the early 1980s. The Agency also figures directly in the founding of Vermont Creamery.

## 1980s: the first makers

- **Orb Weaver Farm, New Haven (1982).** Marjorie Susman and Marian Pollack started making farmhouse cheese a year after founding their farm. *Edible Vermont* calls them the first artisanal cheesemakers in Vermont.
- **Vermont Butter & Cheese (1984), now Vermont Creamery.**
  - Bob Reese at the Agency of Agriculture needed a goat cheese for a state restaurateurs' dinner at the Topnotch in Stowe and asked Allison Hooper, who had learned cheesemaking in France, to make it. The chefs wanted more.
  - The two started with a few thousand dollars in a Brookfield milk house.
  - The company went on to make America's first commercial mascarpone (1991) and its first French-style cultured butter (1998). Both are company claims.
- **Lazy Lady Farm, Westfield (1987).** Laini Fondiller, off the grid in the Northeast Kingdom, aging her first goat cheeses in a closet lined with plastic.
- **Vermont Shepherd, Westminster West.**
  - The Major family's sheep dairy dates to 1987; it calls itself the oldest sheep dairy in the US.
  - Cindy Major called their first cheese the worst cheese ever, like eating a hockey puck. David Major then learned from cheesemakers in the Pyrenees and built a hillside cave in 1995.
  - Vermont Shepherd won its category at the ACS in 1993 and Best of Show in 2000.
- **Cabot comes back from the brink.** The 1919 farmer cooperative merged with Agri-Mark in 1992 after nearly going bankrupt. Its cheddar became one of the best-known Vermont products in the country.

## 1990s: getting organized

- **The ACS came to Vermont.** The American Cheese Society held its 1993 conference at Shelburne Farms, which had been making raw-milk cheddar from its Brown Swiss herd since about 1980.
- **The Vermont Cheese Council (1996–97).**
  - Hooper and Reese founded it with 19 cheesemakers. Hooper's later description of the industry then: "we were invisible."
  - It grew to 50 member makers by 2015 and lists about 40 today.
  - It runs the Vermont Cheese Trail, the Vermont Cheesemakers Festival and Vermont Cheese Week. It also helps pay members' entry fees for national competitions, one reason Vermont shows up so heavily in the results.

## 2000s: the infrastructure years

- **New makers arrived with a clear idea of what they were doing.** Thistle Hill's Tarentaise (2002) was chosen because the Alps' climate resembles Vermont's.
- **Jasper Hill Farm, Greensboro.**
  - Brothers Mateo and Andy Kehler bought a farm in 1998, the year about a third of Greensboro's dairy farms sold their cows. They sold their first cheese in 2003.
  - Their stated founding idea was value-added agriculture that supports Vermont's working landscape.
  - The same year Cabot asked them to grow a natural rind on its cheddar. The result, **Cabot Clothbound**, won ACS Best of Show in 2006: a cooperative giant and a tiny farm making one cheese together.
- **The Cellars at Jasper Hill (2008).** A 22,000-square-foot underground aging facility with seven vaults, built to age, market and distribute other farms' cheeses as well as Jasper Hill's own. Jasper Hill says ripening is more than 70% of the labor in a natural-rind cheese. Sharing the cellar lets small farms make cheese without each building its own cave. It changed what was economically possible for a small Vermont creamery.
- **Vermont Institute for Artisan Cheese, UVM (2004).**
  - Paul Kindstedt and Catherine Donnelly brought cheese science into the university and trained more than 1,500 people from 48 states and 13 countries.
  - Its funding dried up in 2013.
  - Its legacy includes Kindstedt's *American Farmstead Cheese* (2005, written with the Vermont Cheese Council) and *Cheese and Culture* (2012), and Donnelly's *Oxford Companion to Cheese* (James Beard Award, 2017).
- **More makers:**
  - Consider Bardwell (2004), reviving the name of Vermont's first cheese-factory builder
  - Twig Farm (2005)
  - Vermont Farmstead (2008)
  - Spring Brook Farm (2008), with its Farms for City Kids program
  - von Trapp Farmstead (2009)
  - Mt. Mansfield Creamery (2009)
  - Boston Post Dairy (2010)
- **The Vermont Cheesemakers Festival (2009)** at Shelburne Farms.

## 2010s: the awards decade

- **ACS Best of Show (top cheese in America) won by Vermont cheeses:**

  | Year | Cheese | Maker |
  |---|---|---|
  | 2000 | Vermont Shepherd | Vermont Shepherd |
  | 2006 | Cabot Clothbound | Cabot, aged at Jasper Hill |
  | 2013 | Winnimere | Jasper Hill (Grafton's Bear Hill was second) |
  | 2014 | Tarentaise Reserve | Spring Brook Farm |
  | 2018 | Harbison | Jasper Hill |
  | 2022 | Whitney | Jasper Hill |
  | 2026 | Winnimere | Jasper Hill |

  A state assessment found Vermont placed in ACS Best of Show nine times between 2013 and 2018, as many as the next three states combined.
- **World Cheese Awards (Guild of Fine Food):**
  - Bayley Hazen Blue was named World's Best Unpasteurized Cheese in 2014.
  - Harbison was named Best American Cheese in 2015, 2016, 2018 and 2023.
  - In 2024 Bayley Hazen Blue was named Best American Cheese and finished **5th in the world out of about 5,000 entries**.
  - In 2025 the awards were held in Bern, Switzerland, and Vermont makers won 33 awards in the heart of Alpine cheese country.
- **A state dinner.** Jasper Hill's Bayley Hazen Blue was served at the White House state dinner for French President François Hollande in February 2014. An American blue was served to the president of France.
- **The fights over rules.**
  - In June 2014 an FDA statement suggested that aging cheese on wooden boards was a problem. Wood boards are standard in traditional cellars, and the industry protested loudly. The FDA backed off within days.
  - In 2015–16 makers pushed back on FDA testing standards for raw-milk cheese, and in 2016 the FDA paused and revisited them. Vermont makers, Jasper Hill's Mateo Kehler prominent among them, were central voices.
  - Kindstedt traces these fights to the deep split between American and European food cultures.
- **The density claim.** The Vermont Cheese Council's own claim, published in the state's Food System Plan: Vermont has more state-inspected cheesemakers per capita than any other state, about one per 13,000 people. Counts run from about 40 to 60+ makers and 150 to 225+ cheeses, depending on who is counting. (Treat it as the industry's claim.)

## 2017–2026: consolidation, loss, and renewal

- **Pioneers sell.**
  - Vermont Creamery sold to Land O'Lakes in 2017. It then had about 100 employees and bought goat milk from family farms in Vermont and Ontario.
  - Grafton Village Cheese sold in 2025 to Vermont Farmstead Cheese after failing to recover lost pandemic-era distribution.
- **Closures and second lives.** Consider Bardwell shut down after a voluntary Listeria recall in 2019. Its Pawlet, Rupert and Dorset recipes now live on at Woodlawn Creamery, a seventh-generation Pawlet farm. Woodlawn's Brimstone won Best in Show at the 2026 Northeast Gold Medal Cheese Competition.
- **Ripening goes home.** Von Trapp's Oma, one of the first cheeses aged in the Cellars at Jasper Hill, moved back to the von Trapp farm in 2024.
- **Still winning.**
  - Vermont makers won 44 ACS ribbons in both 2019 and 2023, and in 2026 the Vermont Cheese Council counted 54 from 15 makers.
  - Jasper Hill's Alpha Tolman was the highest-scoring raw-milk cheese at the 2025 ACS awards.
  - Winnimere took Best of Show again in July 2026, Jasper Hill's fifth.
- **The squeeze.** In 2026 milk plants closed and farm numbers kept falling. The state now talks openly about needing more cows. Artisan cheese is one of the few parts of Vermont dairy that grew, and a state assessment estimated that expanding artisan-cheese milk demand could support "15–20 or more" small farms.

## Why Vermont? The threads that explain it

- **Old dairy country, cleared land.** The hills were cleared for sheep in the 1800s and grazed by cows for 150 years. The grass, the barns and the dairy know-how were all already there.
- **A climate like the Alps and the Pyrenees.** Makers chose European mountain styles because they fit Vermont, not to copy Europe.
- **Collective infrastructure.** The council, UVM's institute, the Cellars at Jasper Hill, Cabot's cooperative and the Agency of Agriculture all played a part. Few Vermont makers built their success alone.
- **The working landscape.** For many Vermont makers, keeping farms open and land working is part of the purpose of the cheese, not just a backdrop. Jasper Hill and Vermont Creamery both used the phrase.
- **Craft, not recipe.** The Swiss cheesemaker Walter Räss, maker of prize-winning Appenzeller and Chällerhocker, described the difference to Adam Davidson this way: in Switzerland we follow recipes; Vermont has cheesemakers. Vermont's reputation rests on invention: new cheeses, collaborations, the clothbound cheddar, the shared cellar.
- **Vermont was early, and stayed.** Vermont was there at the founding of American goat cheese (1984), the sheep-dairy revival (late 1980s) and the clothbound revival (2003–06). It has kept winning top awards for a quarter century.

## Story seeds

- **The inverse curve.** Farms went from thousands to hundreds while Vermont cheese went from invisible to world-ranked. A maker's own farm usually sits right on that line.
- **"We were invisible."** In 1997, 19 makers, no recognition. Thirty years later, Vermont is a byword for American cheese.
- **Shared caves.** One farm built a cellar for everyone. It's a modern version of the Alpine village herd and the 1800s cheese cooperative.
- **Small and big together.** Cabot Clothbound joins a 1919 farmer co-op and a farm that started in 1998, using a 19th-century American technique, and it beat every cheese in America.
- **A Vermont blue for the French president.** Or Vermont cheese winning in Bern, Switzerland.
- **A second life.** Recipes, names and farms that came back: Consider Bardwell's 1864 name revived in 2004 and passed on to Woodlawn; Plymouth's 1890 factory making cheese again; Grafton's 1892 co-op rebuilt in the 1960s.

## Careful

- **Vermont Shepherd's 1993 ACS award was a category win.** Its Best of Show came in 2000.
- **Founding years differ by source:**
  - Vermont Cheese Council: 1996 vs. 1997
  - Shelburne Farms cheese: 1980 vs. 1981
  - Jasper Hill's purchase: 1998 vs. 1999
- **The ACS Best of Show list above was checked for 2000 onward.** Earlier years were not fully checked.
- **The per-capita claim comes from the industry itself**, published in a state plan. It has no independent verification.
- **Award counts for 2026 vary** (54 vs. 64 ribbons) depending on the source.
- **Vermont is not the largest cheese producer.** Wisconsin and California make far more. Vermont's distinction is quality, density and reputation, not volume.
