# Washed rind: the history, from the monastery cellar to Vermont

*Background for makers of washed-rind cheeses: Munster, Époisses, Livarot, Pont-l'Évêque, Maroilles, Taleggio, Limburger, the Trappist and monastery cheeses, and the Vermont washed rinds. It covers where these cheeses came from, why they exist, and how the tradition reached Vermont. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012), plus the sources listed at the end.*

---

## The short version

Washed-rind cheeses are what happens when fresh, sweet milk is made straight into a soft cheese and kept in a very damp cellar. Yeasts grow on the surface first, then orange bacteria. Someone, at first by accident and later on purpose, rubbed that orange film over the whole cheese with a hand wet with salt water. The result is a sticky orange rind, a strong smell and a paste that is often milder than the smell. Kindstedt places their birth in medieval northern Europe, on peasant farms with a few cows and in monasteries with big herds, stone cellars and a daily schedule of manual work. That's why they are called "monastery cheeses." The Trappists carried the tradition into the 1800s and as far as Quebec. German and Belgian immigrants brought Limburger to America, where it thrived in Wisconsin and then shrank to one factory. Vermont makers took up washed rinds again in the 2000s, many of them ripened in the Cellars at Jasper Hill.

- **The problem:** a wet, low-acid cheese that wants to rot.
- **The answer:** steer the rot. Salt brine, high humidity and constant handling favor one friendly surface community over the molds and spoilers.

---

## Why washed rinds exist: the problem they solved

Kindstedt's reconstruction (he is clear it's conjectural) starts with milk.

- **Enough milk to use it fresh.** A household with a few cows, or a monastery with a herd, could make cheese right after milking. Fresh milk carries few acid-making bacteria. Set quickly with strong rennet, it gives a cheese that is **high in moisture and low in acid**. (Compare bloomy rinds, which came from older, sourer pooled milk.)
- **That cheese is vulnerable.** Wet and low in acid, it is an easy meal for microbes. In the warm Mediterranean it would have spoiled in days.
- **A cold, very damp cellar changes who wins.** In a root cellar or stone cave at high humidity, yeasts colonize the surface first. They eat the lactic acid on the rind and raise its pH. That opens the door to salt-tolerant, acid-sensitive, **orange-pigmented coryneform bacteria**. The best known is *Brevibacterium linens*, though modern studies find a mixed crowd.
- **The hand that spreads it.** The orange bacteria first appear as pinpoint colonies. Kindstedt describes cheesemakers smearing them across the whole surface with their hands, ideally wetted with weak brine, until an orange-red "lawn" covers the cheese. That's the origin of the names: smear-ripened, washed-rind.

Why washing works:

- **Salt picks the winners.** The brine favors salt-tolerant yeasts and bacteria and discourages molds. Taleggio makers, for example, wash once a week partly to keep mold off.
- **Moisture keeps the surface alive.** Washing stops a skin from drying and keeps the sticky surface where the bacteria live.
- **Spreading is inoculation.** Each wash carries the community from older cheeses to younger ones and from one spot to the whole rind. At the Chalet Cheese Co-op in Wisconsin, makers scrape rind off old pine boards into fresh brine to keep their Limburger cultures going, and say those cultures have been in use since 1911.
- **The rind softens the cheese.** The surface microbes break down protein and fat, so the paste softens from the outside in, and the rind can grow sticky and thick.
- **Flavor is a result.** Meaty, brothy, barnyard and "feet" aromas come from the surface microbes breaking down protein and fat. Wikipedia notes that *B. linens* is also found on human skin, where it contributes to body odor; hence the feet. The paste underneath is often sweet, milky and mild. That gap between smell and taste is the central drama of the style.

Kindstedt's wider frame: the same simple soft-cheese recipe split three ways. Pooled sour milk made bloomy rinds. Very slow, acid sets made the lactic goat cheeses. Fresh sweet milk made washed rinds. He calls the whole family "controlled rotting." He also notes the orange bacteria show up on some alpine cheeses (heavily on Gruyère, hardly at all on Emmental), depending on how the maker washes or scrapes. Washing is a technique, not just a family of soft cheeses.

## The monastery cheeses (600s–1500s)

- **Benedict's rule.** In the 500s, Benedict of Nursia wrote a rule for monks that made manual labor holy ("to labor is to pray") and asked monasteries to be self-sufficient. After about 600, the popes promoted it across Europe, and kings gave monasteries huge landholdings. By the 900s many Benedictine houses were enormously rich. The Cistercians, founded in 1098 as a back-to-basics reform, built about 500 abbeys within a century and became the great farmers of medieval Europe.
- **Why monks were good at washed rinds.** Kindstedt gives four reasons:
  - They had **plenty of fresh milk** from their own herds.
  - They had **stone cellars** inside the walls, cool and evenly damp.
  - The rule set aside about **four hours of manual work** each morning and afternoon. Daily washing and turning fit the schedule.
  - The cheese was **eaten on site**, so a fragile cheese never had to survive a trip to market.
- **The meatless diet.** Benedict's rule told monks to "abstain entirely from eating the flesh of four-footed animals," except the very sick. That made cheese, along with fish, eggs and beans, a main source of protein. Lay Christians also kept many meatless days. Kindstedt quotes a ninth-century story in which Charlemagne, on a Friday, refuses meat. His host, a bishop who can't find fish, serves him a rich, creamy cheese instead, and the emperor learns to eat the rind. Kindstedt suggests it may have been a bloomy or washed rind.
- **But not always cheese.** The strict Cistercian rule in England banned milk, butter, cheese and eggs through Advent and Lent, with set exceptions, according to the University of Sheffield's history of Yorkshire Cistercians. Rules relaxed over the centuries, and by the late 1400s many monks ate meat several times a week. "Monks made cheese because they couldn't eat meat" is part of the story, not all of it.
- **Cheeses linked to monasteries.**
  - **Maroilles** (northern France): often said to have been first made by a monk of the Abbey of Maroilles in 962. Kindstedt, citing the British cheese writer Patrick Rance, says Maroilles and Munster are believed to have begun in monasteries in the early Middle Ages.
  - **Munster** (Alsace): the name comes from the town of Munster, which grew around an abbey. The Munster Valley tourist office dates the cheese to monks in the 600s. Mountain herders called *marcaires* used the high pastures by at least 1371. AOC (French protected-name status) in 1969.
  - **Époisses** (Burgundy): said, by oral tradition, to have begun with a community of Cistercians in the village in the early 1500s, with farmers taking it over after the monks left. It is washed in water and marc, the local grape-pomace brandy. Production nearly died out after the World Wars. Robert and Simone Berthaut revived it in 1956. AOC in 1991.
  - **Pont-l'Évêque** (Normandy): made since at least the 1100s, perhaps first by Cistercian monks, according to Wikipedia. It was first called *angelot*. AOC in 1972.
- **The peasant side.** Kindstedt is careful here. Pont-l'Évêque and Livarot were also peasant cheeses, and nobody knows whether peasants worked it out alone or learned from neighboring monks. Rance says some monasteries taught their methods to the lay cheesemakers on their lands. Livarot, wrapped in strips of dried reedmace that earned it the nickname "the colonel," got its AOC in 1975.
- **Italy.** Taleggio comes from the Val Taleggio in the Alps above Bergamo. It was traditionally ripened in caves and washed weekly. It was made only in the valley until the late 1800s, when production spread to the plain.

## The Trappists and the 1800s

- **Exile and return.** The Trappists are a strict branch of the Cistercians. Many monks fled France during the Revolution, learned cheesemaking abroad, and came back after 1815.
- **Port-Salut.** At the abbey of Port-du-Salut in Entrammes, Trappist monks developed a mild, orange-rinded cheese in the 1800s. In 1873 the abbey gave a Paris cheese seller exclusive distribution rights. In 1950 it sold the rights to the Bel Group. Today's Port-Salut is a factory cheese with a plastic-coated rind. It's a clear example of a monastery recipe passing into industry.
- **Oka, Quebec.** French Trappists who had settled at Oka, Quebec, began making a cheese in 1892, using a recipe Brother Alphonse Juin brought from Port-du-Salut. Less than a year later it won first place at the 1893 Montreal Exhibition. The monks sold the rights in 1981, and Agropur now owns them. Oka is the nearest Trappist washed-rind lineage to Vermont.
- **Limburger.** Herve cheese from the old Duchy of Limburg (now eastern Belgium) is said to date back to the 1400s. The Limburger style spread to Germany, which made it famous, and then to America.

## America: Limburger and Muenster

- **Immigrant cheese.** Kindstedt lists Limburger and Muenster among the cheeses German immigrants brought. Limburger was first made in the US at a factory in Antwerp, New York, in 1854, and soon there were dozens of factories.
- **Wisconsin's Limburger country.** Late-1800s immigrants made Green County, Wisconsin, its center. In 1926 the county made 98% of Wisconsin's Limburger. By 1935 Wisconsin made almost 7 million pounds.
- **The smell as a social issue.** Mark Twain called the aroma "most evil." In 1902, Louisville, Kentucky, banned Limburger as "unwholesome," over local Germans' protests. It was a stock joke in vaudeville, the Three Stooges and Charlie Chaplin.
- **The collapse.** Kindstedt's general rule applies: cheeses that couldn't be scaled up "largely disappeared" from America. Limburger production began falling in the mid-1940s. Between 1950 and 1960 the number of US Limburger plants dropped by two-thirds. By the early 1980s, one was left: **Chalet Cheese Cooperative** in Monroe, Wisconsin, founded in 1885. Its master Limburger maker, Myron Olson, started in 1970. In 1947, when Chalet moved to a new plant, the first month's cheese came out green and moldy. A Swiss-born manager brought over the old factory's wooden boards and culture, and the Limburger came right. A good Living Thing story: the microbes lived in the wood.
- **American Muenster went another way.** Under the FDA's standard of identity, "muenster" is a generic name for a mild, standardized American cheese. It shares a name with Alsace Munster, not much else.
- **The revival.** Kindstedt says the artisan makers who began in the 1970s and 80s (the American Cheese Society was founded in 1983) were especially drawn to washed, bloomy and natural rinds, the cheeses the big industry saw as riskiest. US rules require raw-milk cheese to age at least 60 days, which shapes which washed rinds can be made raw.

## Vermont

Many Vermont washed rinds are tied to the Cellars at Jasper Hill in Greensboro (operational in 2008; 22,000 square feet built into a hillside), which ripens cheese for partner farms. That has made the Northeast Kingdom a center for the style.

- **Jasper Hill Farm, Greensboro: Winnimere.** Raw milk from the farm's own Ayrshire cows, made **only in winter** (November to April) from hay-fed milk. It's wrapped in spruce cambium from the farm's woods and rubbed with a cultured salt brine. The farm calls it a modern take on Jura cheeses like Vacherin Mont d'Or. It's named for the corner of Caspian Lake where the Kehlers' grandfather had an ice-fishing shack. It won Best of Show at the American Cheese Society in 2013. It combines This Wheel, This Season with a name tied to a place.
- **Jasper Hill: Willoughby.** A small, soft washed rind created by Marisa Mauro at Ploughgate Creamery in Albany, Vermont. A fire in 2011 stopped production. Jasper Hill revived it, in its own words "with Marisa's blessing," and has kept refining the recipe. A Second Life story, and a Nobody Does It Alone story.
- **von Trapp Farmstead, Waitsfield: Oma.** A washed-rind tomme from the family's certified-organic cows (Jersey, Normande, Ayrshire and Montbéliarde), named for Sebastian von Trapp's grandmother Erika, who ran the dairy from 1959. Jasper Hill says Oma debuted in early 2009 as one of the first cheeses ripened in its Cellars, and in 2024 moved back to the von Trapp collection. An Inheritance story through a name.
- **Spring Brook Farm, Reading: Reading.** A raw cow's-milk washed rind modeled on French raclette, aged about three months. Sales support the Farms for City Kids Foundation, which brings city kids to the farm for a week of farm work.
- **Twig Farm, West Cornwall: Washed Wheel.** Michael Lee and Emily Sunderman's farm, in business since 2005. *culture* describes Washed Wheel as raw goat's milk, sometimes with a little raw Jersey milk from a neighboring organic dairy, washed with a whey brine. A goat's-milk washed rind is rarer than a cow's.
- **Woodcock Farm, Weston.** Mark and Gari Fischer list a Taleggio-style washed-rind cow's-milk cheese alongside their sheep's-milk cheeses.
- **Vermont Creamery, Websterville: Hooper.** Released in 2024 for the company's 40th anniversary. It's a doughnut-shaped blend of goat's and cow's milk, washed in a French gray sea salt brine, and named for co-founder Allison Hooper. The company calls it its first washed-rind cheese. The shape, by its account, is meant to maximize rind.

## Story seeds

- **The cheese that needs you every day.** A washed rind has to be wiped, brushed or washed, often several times a week, for weeks. Put a number on it: how many washes, by whose hands, how early. (The Hands.)
- **Tame the rot.** This style began as a way to steer a cheese that wanted to spoil. The maker isn't fighting nature; they're choosing which microbes win. (The Living Thing; Why It Tastes Like This.)
- **Smells like this, tastes like that.** The gap between aroma and flavor is the style's best For the Table line, as long as it's true of this cheese. Pair a broad word with a specific one: "smells like a barn, tastes like warm milk and roasted garlic." (For the Table.)
- **What's in the wash.** Époisses uses marc; Twig Farm uses whey; Hooper uses gray sea salt. If the maker washes with local cider, beer or brandy, that's a Taste of Place link. Ask what and why.
- **Labor as prayer.** The monastery line works for a maker whose routine is close to devotion, as long as it's framed as kinship, not lineage. (Chosen Ancestors.)
- **The board, the culture, the room.** Chalet's Limburger came back when the old wooden boards came over. If a maker's rind comes from their own room, shelves or a saved brine, that's a story nobody else can tell. (The Living Thing.)
- **Winter cheese.** Winnimere is made only from winter hay milk. A washed rind tied to a season, or made because summer milk goes elsewhere, is a seasonal story told in advance. (This Wheel, This Season.)
- **A recipe passed on.** Willoughby moved from Ploughgate to Jasper Hill after a fire; Oma moved back home. Washed rinds in Vermont have often traveled between makers. (Nobody Does It Alone; Second Life.)
- **The joke cheese.** Limburger was a punchline in America for a century. A maker who wins over a skeptic has a small, true story. (The Upstart.)

## Careful

- **Monastery origins are mostly tradition.** Kindstedt says Maroilles and Munster are "believed to" have monastic origins, citing Rance. The 962 date for Maroilles, the 600s for Munster and the Cistercian start of Époisses are traditions, not documented facts. Say "said to have begun."
- **"Monks made cheese because they couldn't eat meat"** is a simplification. Cheese was a key protein on meatless days, but strict Cistercian rules banned dairy too during Lent and Advent, and meat rules relaxed over the centuries.
- **Kindstedt's peasant/monastery reconstruction is conjectural** by his own account. Peasants and monks may both have developed washed rinds.
- **Charlemagne's cheese** can't be identified. Kindstedt suggests bloomy or washed. Others say Brie or blue. Don't name it.
- **Brevibacterium linens is not the whole story.** It's the textbook name, but the real surface community is mixed and starts with yeasts. Don't lecture; if naming a microbe, say "orange bacteria like *B. linens*."
- **"Smells like feet"** is true but use it carefully on public pieces. Better to describe the paste.
- **Munster vs. Muenster.** Alsace Munster is a washed-rind AOC cheese. American Muenster is a mild, standardized cheese. Don't treat them as one.
- **Port-Salut** today is industrial and its rind is a coating. Don't cite it as a living monastery cheese.
- **Company claims are the company's own:** Jasper Hill's descriptions and name stories, Vermont Creamery's "first washed rind" and its reasons for the shape, Chalet's claim that its cultures date to 1911.
- **Founding dates vary** between farm and cheese dates, and between sources. Check with the maker.
- **Don't call every washed rind "stinky."** Some, like Reading or Oma, are gentle.
