# Sheep's milk cheese: the history, from the first flocks to Vermont

*Background for makers of sheep's-milk and mixed sheep-and-cow cheeses: aged pecorino-style and Pyrenees-style tommes, sheep's-milk blues, feta-style brined cheeses, fresh sheep's ricotta. It covers where these cheeses came from, why they exist, and how the tradition reached Vermont. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012), plus the sources listed at the end.*

---

## The short version

Sheep were domesticated alongside goats about 10,500 years ago. For most of history, cheese in the Mediterranean and medieval England mostly meant sheep's-milk cheese: the Cyclops made it, Roman soldiers were issued it, Anglo-Saxon manors paid taxes in it. Sheep were usually raised mainly for wool, so sheep cheese rose and fell with the wool trade. The great survivors (Pecorino Romano, Roquefort, Manchego, Ossau-Iraty, feta) come from dry hills and mountains where flocks walked to summer grass. Vermont was cleared for sheep in the 1800s, but they were wool sheep and nobody milked them. Vermont sheep dairying began only in the late 1980s, when one Westminster West family started milking because wool and lamb no longer paid.

- Sheep's milk has about twice the fat and nearly twice the protein of cow's milk, so it makes far more cheese per gallon.
- Ewes give little milk, and only part of the year. Sheep cheese has always been seasonal.

---

## Why sheep's milk cheese exists: the problem it solved

**The problem was marginal land.** Kindstedt describes Neolithic Near Eastern farmers, facing worn-out soil around 6500 BC, turning to sheep and goats on land too poor for crops. Every later sheep-cheese region repeats this: the Apennines, Sardinia, La Mancha, the Larzac plateau above Roquefort, the Basque and Béarn mountains. In Roman Italy, erosion and cheap Sicilian grain pushed farmers off wheat and onto sheep grazing deforested hillsides.

**The milk is rich, and there isn't much of it.**
- Sheep's milk runs about 7% fat and 5.5–6% protein. Cow's milk is about 3.3–3.9% fat and 3.3% protein.
- Michigan State Extension puts the yield at about 4 pounds of sheep's milk per pound of cheese, against 8–10 pounds of cow's milk. David Major of Vermont Shepherd has said about 5 pounds.
- A good dairy ewe gives 400–1,100 pounds of milk a lactation; a dairy cow, over 22,000. Sheep's milk sells for several times the price of cow's milk.

**Sheep's milk doesn't cream.** Kindstedt notes it lacks the protein that makes cow's-milk fat rise, so it can't be skimmed for butter. The fat stays in the cheese. On Anglo-Saxon manors butter was a luxury skimmed off the whey; it became common only when England switched to cows.

**The season is short, and it moves.**
- Ewes lamb once a year. In medieval England the milking season ran about late April to late August, roughly a hundred days.
- In Cato's Italy, lambs came in November and December and cheese was made in winter and spring; Varro, a century later, describes cheesemaking from May into summer on the high pastures.
- Fat, protein and minerals rise toward the end of lactation, so the cheese changes through the season.
- Ossau-Iraty's rules still require at least 100 days a year without milking.

Everything that follows (small rinds dried in caves, heavy salt, brine, long aging) is a way of stretching a short, rich season across the year.

**Wool versus milk.** Varro named the trade-off: milking ewes costs wool, and some estates didn't milk at all. That tension runs through the whole history, including Vermont's.

**Flavor as a consequence.** Sheep fat is rich in short-chain fatty acids, which give sheep cheese its sweet, nutty character and, with age, a peppery bite. Kindstedt notes that heavy salt and crude rennet in brined cheeses release enzymes that split fat into feta's piquant flavor.

## Deep history

### The first flocks and the first wool economy (8500–3000 BC)

- Goats and then sheep were domesticated around 8500 BC in the Taurus and Zagros foothills (today's Turkey and Iran), first for meat. Breeding ewes to give more milk than a lamb needed took generations.
- Woolly sheep (earlier sheep had coarse hair) were bred around 4000–3500 BC.
- In Uruk (Iraq), the goddess Inanna chose the shepherd Dumuzi and demanded daily ewe's-milk cheese and butter. Kindstedt argues this gave the temple control of the sheep, and so of wool, the engine of Sumerian wealth. Clay tablets record herders' annual cheese quotas.
- The old Near Eastern sheep method, still used in Turkey: ferment milk to yogurt, churn out butter, boil the buttermilk into a ricotta-like curd.

### Brine, ships and feta (Bronze Age to the Middle Ages)

- Around 1200 BC, tablets from Ugarit record cheese shipped by sea from Ashdod. Kindstedt suggests, without direct evidence, these may have been brined white cheeses of the feta family.
- A salted white cheese makes its own brine: dry-salted curd weeps salty whey and, packed in a jar, pickles itself. That's how a soft cheese survives a Mediterranean summer.
- Cato is the first writer to describe brining cheese. The Byzantine writer Psellos recorded a Cretan brined cheese, *prósphatos* ("fresh"), in the 11th century; a 1400s Italian traveler in Crete saw big cheeses floating in brine two feet deep. Aegean feta traveled in clay jars into the 1800s.
- **Feta became an EU protected name in 2002:** mainland Greece or Lesbos, sheep's milk with up to 30% goat's milk. Denmark and Germany fought it in court and lost in 2005.

### The Cyclops's cave (about 800 BC)

In the *Odyssey*, Odysseus lands to bleating flocks and finds the Cyclops's cave: racks of drying cheeses, penned lambs and kids, whey saved in vessels. His crew wants to steal the cheese and run; Odysseus waits. The Cyclops comes home, sets the milk quickly (rennet or fig sap) and lightly presses the curd in wicker baskets. Kindstedt calls it arguably the first credible description of rennet cheesemaking, and reads the result as small rinded pecorino and caprino rounds dried in the cave. Sicily became famous for hard grating cheese, and by the 7th century BC bronze graters appear in elite Italian tombs.

### Rome: pecorino, estates and legions (200 BC–AD 400)

- **Pecorino becomes the Roman cheese.** In the first millennium BC, Kindstedt traces a shift from fresh ricotta to aged pecorino and caprino for grating, driven by elite demand. Romans sorted cheese into *caseus aridus* (dry) and *caseus mollis* (fresh).
- **Cato's estate math (around 170 BC).** A hundred sheep and one shepherd per 160-acre olive farm; ten pigs to drink the whey; winter pasture rented for 1.5 pounds of cheese per ewe, half of it aged.
- **Transhumance.** Since the Bronze Age, Italian shepherds have walked flocks to summer camps in the Apennines and back in autumn. Varro's own flocks numbered 700.
- **Columella (around AD 60)** wrote down the method: small, uncooked, lightly pressed, surface-salted cheeses dried on shelves, which "can even be exported beyond the sea."
- **The legions.** Kindstedt cites Virgil for a Roman soldier's daily ration that included about an ounce of pecorino. The army carried Roman cheese molds to forts across Europe and kept sheep for wool and milk.

### Medieval England: when English cheese was sheep cheese (800–1450)

- Roman Britain raised sheep to clothe and feed some 45,000 soldiers. Kindstedt says Anglo-Saxon manor cheeses were overwhelmingly sheep's milk, small Roman-style cylinders of 2–6 pounds; cows were kept mostly to breed plow oxen.
- Around 858 a royal manor in Kent paid almost 9,000 pounds of cheese a year in tribute.
- **Wool carried the cheese.** Merchants buying English wool for Flemish looms bought cheese along with it.
- Dairywomen made it. The *Rectitudines* entitled the manor cheesemaker to about one cheese a day through the hundred-day season.
- **The collapse.** Sheep disease from about 1270, the Hundred Years' War, and wet years in the 1430s–40s wrecked the flocks. England moved to cow's-milk cheese, and cheddar descends from that switch.

### Roquefort and the mountain cheeses (1000 onward)

- **Roquefort.** The village sits against cliffs riddled with cool, damp caves (the Combalou), ventilated by natural fissures called *fleurines*. Sheep grazed the Larzac plateau above; cheese was made there, salted heavily with salt hauled up the old Roman salt road, and carried down to ripen in the caves, where *Penicillium roqueforti* thrived. First definite record: 1070, a cave given to the Abbey of Conques. Monks and Templars came to control pastures, salt route and caves. In 1411 the town won sole right to the name; in 1925 Roquefort became France's first protected cheese. It must now be made from Lacaune ewes' milk.
- **The Pyrenees.** Kindstedt notes that Alpine wheel-making spread "beyond to the Pyrenees, where alpine sheep's-milk cheeses flourished." **Ossau-Iraty**, named for the Ossau Valley in Béarn and the Iraty forest in the Basque Country, became an AOC in 1980. Its rules allow only three local breeds (Manech tête noire, Manech tête rousse, Basco-Béarnaise), which milk only in winter, spring and early summer. About three-quarters of flocks still go up to summer pasture, where cheese can be made in the shepherd's hut: *cayolar* or *etxola* in Basque, *cuyala* in Béarn.
- **Spain.** Manchego must come from Manchega ewes in La Mancha (regulations 1984, EU registration 1996). The zigzag rind copies the braided esparto-grass bands once used to mold it. Behind Spanish sheep stood the Mesta, the sheep-owners' guild chartered in 1273, which drove Merino flocks along royal drove roads and guarded Merino wool jealously. That matters for Vermont.

### Pecorino Romano today

Pecorino Romano is protected for Lazio, Sardinia and Grosseto, but about 96% is now made in Sardinia, where producers moved in the late 1800s when Lazio's flocks couldn't keep up. It is made October to July, following the ewes. In 2017–18 more than half was exported to the United States: the Roman soldier's cheese is an Italian-American kitchen staple.

## America

America had almost no sheep dairying until the 1980s; Kindstedt's factory century had no place for a low-volume milk. In 1999 the *Christian Science Monitor* reported the US imported 70 million pounds of sheep's-milk cheese a year, with Wisconsin then leading domestic sheep milk and Vermont second. Most American dairy ewes are imported East Friesian or Lacaune breeds. Sheep's milk can be frozen without hurting the cheese, which helps small producers pool scarce milk.

## Vermont

**The wool sheep came first** (the cheddar history tells this in full). William Jarvis, US consul in Lisbon, brought Merinos out of Spain as Napoleon's invasion broke the Spanish monopoly, and settled in Weathersfield in 1811. By 1840 Vermont had nearly 1.7 million sheep, six per person, descendants of the Mesta's drove flocks. The boom collapsed by the 1850s and cows took the walled pastures. Nobody milked the ewes.

**Vermont Shepherd, Westminster West (sheep since 1965; milking since about 1987).**
- David Major's parents raised sheep for meat and wool. In the late 1980s lamb prices fell and federal wool subsidies ended; David read a book on sheep's-milk cheese and began milking.
- **The hockey puck.** Cindy Major, who started the cheese with David, told the *Los Angeles Times* their first natural-rind cheese was "the worst cheese ever … like eating a hockey puck." She took it to Dean & DeLuca anyway; the buyer spat it out and told her never to come back with cheese like that. She told another paper they "made a zillion mistakes for a couple of years."
- **The next beat.** The Majors spent an intensive two weeks with sheep cheesemakers in the French Pyrenees. The farm says its cheese descends from Pyrenees mountain cheeses.
- First ACS ribbon (a category win) in 1993; ACS Best of Show in 2000.
- **The cave.** Built into the hillside in 1995 under about four feet of earth; wheels ripen on wooden boards.
- **Taught the neighbors.** By 1999 six neighboring sheep dairies made young cheese to the Majors' recipe for them to age and sell. That arrangement is gone, but several Vermont sheep farms started with Major's guidance.
- **Today** David and Yesenia Major milk about 250 ewes, moving them to fresh pasture every 12 hours. **Verano** ("summer") is sheep's milk only, made spring and summer; **Invierno** ("winter") adds a neighbor's cow's milk in late fall as the ewes dry off. The two cheeses split the season.
- **Their claim.** A retailer quotes the farm as "the oldest sheep dairy in the United States"; its own site says "one of the oldest." Treat both as the farm's claim.

**Woodcock Farm, Weston.** Mark and Gari Fischer left arts careers in New York City; Mark was one of David Major's first apprentices. They built a flock of about 100 East Friesian ewes and a cheese house modeled on Major Farm's. Their Weston Wheel, a seasonal sheep's tomme, won its ACS class in 2003; Summer Snow is a bloomy sheep's wheel. A lineage story: teacher, apprentice, copied cheese house. (Its company registration is active in 2026, but it is missing from the Council's current member list; confirm it is still making cheese.)

**Three Shepherds, Warren.** Larry and Linda Faillace imported East Friesian milk sheep from Belgium and the Netherlands in 1996 and made their first official cheese in 1999. In March 2001 the USDA seized their 126 sheep over a suspected TSE (the disease family that includes mad cow), after the family lost in court. Linda wrote a book about it, *Mad Sheep*. They teach cheesemaking and are a current Council member. Any telling of that story should come from them.

**Grafton Village Cheese, Grafton.** A historic cheddar company, not a sheep farm. It buys sheep's milk from an Amish cooperative in central New York (hand-milked, horse-farmed) for Bear Hill and Storyteller (all sheep) and Shepsog (half sheep, half cow). Storyteller, a Pyrenees-style cave-aged cheese, is named for Grafton storyteller Daisy Turner; head cheesemaker Mariano Gonzalez has said his Spanish ancestors' Pyrenees cheeses inspired it. Shepsog and Bear Hill took first and second in their ACS class in 2023. Grafton was sold to Vermont Farmstead Cheese in 2025.

**Newer Council members:** **Shepherd Moon Farm, Corinth**, 222 acres, a self-described "grass-fed, queer-led dairy and creamery" making sheep's cheese and selling whey-fed pork. **Fat Sheep Farm, Hartland/Windsor**, where Suzy Kaplan and Todd Heyman make raw-milk sheep's cheese and pay for it with farm-stay cabins.

**Honest count.** The *Monitor* counted about a dozen Vermont sheep dairies in 1999; several from the 2000s are gone from Council lists. A Vermont sheep maker is one of a few.

## Story seeds

- **The wool hills, milked at last.** Vermont was cleared for Merinos nobody milked. A Vermont sheep dairy puts milk back into that landscape. (The Working Landscape, Second Life)
- **The Pyrenees in Westminster West.** A maker who learned somewhere specific can name the place and the teacher. (Chosen Ancestors)
- **Varro's choice.** Milk the ewe or get the best wool, not both. If a farm started milking when wool stopped paying, that choice is 2,000 years old. (The Working Landscape)
- **Four pounds of milk, one pound of cheese.** Twice the fat, nearly twice the protein, no cream line: a causal link a buyer can taste. (Why It Tastes Like This)
- **Summer cheese, winter cheese.** Ewes milk part of the year, so the calendar is built in. Name the months and what changes. (This Wheel, This Season)
- **The cave and the hut.** The Cyclops's racks, Roquefort's Combalou, the Basque *cayolar*, a Vermont hillside cave. (The Living Thing)
- **The lineage of farms.** Vermont sheep dairying spread farm to farm, apprentice to apprentice. (Nobody Does It Alone)
- **Pigs on the whey.** Cato kept a pig for every ten ewes; a maker who feeds whey to pigs is doing the oldest arithmetic in sheep farming. (The Working Landscape)
- **The hockey puck.** Vermont's first sheep cheese was spat out by a buyer, then won Best of Show. Only in the maker's own words, and only if the maker has one. (The Hockey Puck)

## Careful

- **"Oldest sheep dairy in the US" is Vermont Shepherd's own claim,** worded variously; the *Christian Science Monitor* (1999) said "the state's first modern sheep dairy." Never repeat it as fact or give it to another maker.
- **Vermont Shepherd dates vary:** sheep since 1965, milking since about 1987, Verano since 1993, cave 1995; one source says the couple began cheesemaking in 1990. The address appears as Putney and as Westminster West.
- **The hockey puck is Cindy Major's** (*LA Times*, 2003). Don't adapt it for anyone else.
- **The Cyclops's cheese is disputed.** Kindstedt reads pecorino/caprino rounds; some Greek dairy scientists call it feta's ancestor. Homer mentions neither brine nor salt.
- **The Canaanite brined-cheese shipment is a hypothesis.**
- **"Pecorino fed the legions"** rests on one literary source (Virgil, via Kindstedt) plus molds found at forts. Say "a soldier's ration included pecorino."
- **Ossau-Iraty:** sources disagree on whether it's one of two or three French sheep AOCs. Skip the count.
- **Manchego:** skip retailer lines like "since the Bronze Age" and "milk only April–June"; the council says Manchega sheep graze year-round.
- **Grafton** says its sheep cheeses date from 2016, but the project's Vermont history lists Bear Hill second at ACS Best of Show in 2013. Check before using either date.
- **The Three Shepherds seizure is contested** (USDA tests vs. the family's challenge). Let them tell it.
- **Left out, unconfirmed as current:** Three Owls Sheep Dairy, Hope Farm, Willow Hill Farm, Bonnieview Farm (Craftsbury, sheep and cow cheeses) and Willow Moon Farm. Check before naming.
- **Never imply a maker follows a historical method** (Roman, Basque, Roquefort) unless they say so.
