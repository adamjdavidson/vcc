# Blue cheese: the history, from the caves of Roquefort to Vermont

*Background for makers of blue cheeses: Roquefort-, Gorgonzola-, Stilton- and Fourme-style blues, natural-rind and foil-wrapped blues, soft blues and spirited or cider-soaked blues. It covers where these cheeses came from, why they exist, and how the tradition reached Vermont. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012), plus the sources listed at the end.*

---

## The short version

Blue cheese is what happens when a moist, acidic, salty cheese sits somewhere cool and damp with air getting inside it. A mold that lives in caves, *Penicillium roqueforti*, moves in, grows along every crack and air pocket, and breaks down fat and protein into the sharp, peppery, sometimes sweet flavors of blue. The three great European blues each found that condition in a different way: Roquefort in the limestone caves of southern France, Gorgonzola in the dairies of Lombardy, and Stilton in the soft, unpressed cream cheeses of the English Midlands. Americans spent the 1930s trying to copy Roquefort in Minnesota sandstone caves and Iowa hillsides. Vermont came late. Its first wholesale blue started on a Highgate dairy farm in 1999, and Vermont blues have since won some of the biggest prizes in the cheese world.

- **The mold needs air.** Every blue technique (loose curd, no pressing, piercing with needles) is a way of letting oxygen into the cheese.
- **The cheese has to be built to welcome mold.** Acid, salt, moisture and a cool, wet room make the conditions.
- **The famous origin stories are legends.** The shepherd and the girl at Roquefort, the lovesick cheesemaker at Gorgonzola: nobody knows who made the first blue.

## Why blue cheese exists: the problem it solved

Most cheese history is about keeping mold *out*. Blue cheese is the case where people stopped fighting it and learned to steer it.

- **The cave problem.** People who aged cheese in caves and cellars had a free, steady, cool room, but it was damp, and damp rooms grow mold. Kindstedt describes the caves under Roquefort as holding a near-constant 43–50°F and 95–98% humidity, ventilated by natural fissures in the rock called *fleurines*. Those conditions happen to be ideal for *P. roqueforti*. If you age cheese there, blue mold is going to arrive whether you invite it or not.
- **Salt and acid decide which mold wins.** Kindstedt explains that the Roquefort makers' method was slow rennet setting of sheep's milk, no cooking, and heavy salting of the surface with sea salt. That made cheeses high in acid and salt, which most spoilage organisms dislike and *P. roqueforti* tolerates. In the cave, the blue grew. Over time the makers came to like what it did to flavor and texture, and they refined their methods to encourage it.
- **Salt was the hidden ingredient.** Roquefort and the other heavily salted blues of the southern Massif Central depended on salt hauled up from the Mediterranean salt works on roads the Romans built. The blue cheese of the uplands was, in part, a product of a salt trade.
- **The mold has to breathe.** *P. roqueforti* grows only where it can reach oxygen, so it spreads through gaps, cracks and channels inside the cheese. A tightly pressed cheese stays mostly white. That's why blue cheeses are made with loose, open curd, left unpressed or lightly pressed, and, from early modern times on, pierced with needles to let air in. The veins you see are the mold following those air paths.
- **Stilton shows the same logic in England.** Kindstedt explains that Stilton makers deliberately limited whey drainage, skipped heavy pressing, and made a tall cylinder to slow drying. The result was an open, moist, acidic cheese that grew mold inside and out in a cool, damp room. It was so soft it had to be bound in cloth bandages to keep from slumping. Kindstedt suggests Stilton was perhaps the first cheese wrapped that way.
- **Flavor is the consequence.** Blue mold breaks down fat and protein hard. That produces the peppery "blue" flavors and softens the paste near the veins. Milk, salt, age and how much air the maker lets in decide how far it goes, which is why a blue can be fudgy and sweet or crumbly and fierce.

## Roquefort: caves, monks and the first protected name (Roman times to 1925)

- **The first written record: 1070.** Cheesemaking in the region goes back at least to Roman times, but nobody knows when the caves were first used. Kindstedt and the official French specification both point to a 1070 record at the Abbey of Conques, when a lord donated a "cave" and a manor to the abbey.
- **The monks scaled it up.** Several monasteries came to own caves in Roquefort and worked with their tenant farmers to improve the cheese. The sheep grazed, and the cheeses were made, on the high Larzac plateau. Mule drivers carried salt up to the pastures and brought salted cheeses back down to the caves to ripen.
- **Templars and Cistercians.** In the 1100s the Knights Templar and the Cistercians took control of Larzac grazing land. The Templars also part-owned a Mediterranean salt works and controlled the salt road. For a time, religious orders held enormous sway over the whole Roquefort business.
- **1411: the first protected cheese name.** Kindstedt dates to 1411 the grant from King Charles VI that gave the town alone the right to the name Roquefort. Wikipedia gives the date as June 4, 1411, and describes it as a monopoly on ripening. In 1666 the Parliament of Toulouse confirmed those royal privileges and gave the people of Roquefort-sur-Soulzon sole rights to age the cheese (official specification).
- **"King of cheeses."** Diderot and d'Alembert's *Encyclopédie* called Roquefort the king of cheeses in the 1700s (official specification; INAO).
- **1925: the first AOC.** On July 26, 1925, the French Parliament made Roquefort the first cheese with a legal appellation of origin. Today it must be made from whole raw milk of Lacaune ewes and ripened in the Combalou caves, and the *P. roqueforti* used must come from those caves.
- **Bread as a mold incubator.** Makers traditionally grew the mold on bread left in the caves, then dried and powdered it. The Roquefort strain still produces more spores on bread than other strains do (Dumas et al., 2020).
- **Still a trade-war pawn.** Kindstedt notes that Australia seized and destroyed imported Roquefort under its raw-milk ban in the early 2000s, and that in 2009 the U.S. threatened to triple the duty on Roquefort in a beef dispute.

## Gorgonzola: the "tired" cheese of Lombardy (Middle Ages to 1996)

- **Stracchino.** Gorgonzola was long called *stracchino di Gorgonzola* or *stracchino verde* ("green stracchino"), a name in use until the early 20th century. *Stracchino* comes from the word for "tired." The usual explanation, given by the Gorgonzola consortium and *culture* magazine, is that the cheese was made in autumn from the milk of herds coming down, weary, from the Alpine summer pastures toward the Po Valley.
- **Where it began is disputed.** The town of Gorgonzola, outside Milan, gives the cheese its name. The consortium also notes a rival claim for Pasturo in the Valsassina valley, which has natural caves at a steady 6–12°C. A consortium page says "some claim" a date of 879. Treat that date as a claim.
- **Two curds, and the gaps between them.** The traditional method combined the curd of one milking with the next, *due paste* ("two curds"). The uneven joins left pockets where blue mold could grow. *culture* reports that a few producers still make Gorgonzola this way. The romantic version of this is the legend of a cheesemaker who left his curd out overnight to meet a lover and mixed it into the next morning's batch.
- **Piccante came first, dolce after the war.** The firmer, sharper *piccante* is the older style. The soft, sweet *dolce* that dominates today emerged after World War II, with more milk and refrigeration (*culture*). Piercing with metal needles is now standard.
- **Protection.** Gorgonzola became an Italian designation of origin in 1955, its consortium formed in 1970, and it was registered as an EU PDO in 1996 (consortium).

## Stilton and the 18th-century English market

- **A cream cheese from a coaching road.** Kindstedt places Stilton's rise in the mid-1700s, as better roads and canals linked farm regions to London. The village of Stilton sat on the Great North Road between London and Edinburgh.
- **"Famous for cheese."** Daniel Defoe passed through around 1724 and called Stilton a town famous for cheese (Stilton Cheese Makers' Association history). A recipe was published by Richard Bradley in the early 1720s. Early Stilton was a rich cheese made with added cream; the recipe suggests it was pressed. Whether it was blue at first isn't clear.
- **The innkeeper and the dairywoman.** Cooper Thornhill, landlord of the Bell Inn at Stilton, sold the cheese to travelers and into London. Around 1743 he struck a deal with Frances Pawlett, a well-known cheesemaker from Wymondham, Leicestershire, who organized other makers to produce it to her recipe. The makers' association credits the two of them with commercializing the cheese and developing the recipe that leads to today's Stilton. *Country Life* credits Pawlett with a more open curd, draining pipes, piercing with needles, and a standard size.
- **Never made in Stilton? Disputed.** For a century the official line was that Stilton was only *sold* in the village. Research published by the Stilton village society argues it was made there before 1720. Today the name is restricted to Derbyshire, Leicestershire and Nottinghamshire.
- **Bandaged, unpressed, acidic.** Kindstedt's technical description is above: pre-salted curd, little pressing, minimal drainage, a tall cylinder, and bandages. That is a recipe for a blue.
- **Protection, and the raw-milk loss.** Stilton got a certification trademark in 1966 and EU PDO status in 1996. In 1989 Colston Bassett, the last raw-milk maker, switched to pasteurized milk after a food-poisoning scare that, the *Telegraph* reports, was never tied to its cheese. Raw-milk Stilton can no longer use the name. In 2006 Randolph Hodgson and Joe Schneider began making a raw-milk version called **Stichelton**, after the village's earliest recorded name.

## The mold itself: domesticated twice

- **One species behind all the blues.** Nearly every blue cheese in the world, Roquefort, Gorgonzola, Stilton and Danish blue included, ripens with *P. roqueforti*. Old names like *P. glaucum* are synonyms.
- **Two domestications.** Genetic studies by Jeanne Ropars, Tatiana Giraud and colleagues found the cheese mold was domesticated twice, separately. The Roquefort strain is an old, slower-growing, more varied lineage suited to pre-industrial cheesemaking. Almost every other blue in the world uses a single clonal lineage selected for fast growth and salt tolerance at industrial scale (Dumas et al., 2020). A maker's choice of strain, or reliance on the mold in their own cave, sits on that history.

## America: copying Roquefort (1918–1957)

- **Minnesota's sandstone caves.** In 1933 University of Minnesota professor W. B. Combs began ripening cow's-milk "Roquefort-type" cheese in rented sandstone caves in St. Paul. His first batch failed because the cave had no door. When a French trade official objected to the name, he suggested calling it "blue" cheese, and it became Minnesota Blue. By 1941 Kraft and Land O'Lakes were ripening blue in St. Paul caves.
- **Maytag, 1941.** Iowa State microbiologists Clarence Lane and Bernard Hammer developed a blue cheese process using homogenized cow's milk in 1937. Fred and Robert Maytag built a plant on their Newton, Iowa, dairy farm and made their first wheels on October 11, 1941, selling by mail order (Maytag; Iowa State).
- **Oregon Blue, 1957.** Tom Vella of Rogue Creamery toured the Roquefort caves with his wife and began making Oregon Blue in Central Point in 1957 (Oregon Encyclopedia).
- **Then the revival.** Small makers returned from the 1970s on and founded the American Cheese Society in 1983 (Kindstedt). In Vermont, blue came later than goat cheese, cheddar or Alpine wheels.

## Vermont's blue cheeses

- **Green Mountain Blue Cheese, Highgate (1999).** Dawn Morin-Boucher began making cheese on the Boucher family dairy in 1999, a self-taught maker who developed all her recipes by experiment. The company says that at the time it was the only Vermont blue sold wholesale. The family traces 14 generations of farming, first in Quebec. **Gore-Dawn-Zola** is named for the Gore Road and for Dawn, a ready-made "Name" story. **Boucher Blue** is described by *culture* as based on a Fourme d'Ambert recipe. They also make **Madison Blue**. The company reports that Madison Blue took first and Boucher Blue third in their category at the 2026 American Cheese Society awards. All are raw-milk blues from a mixed herd of Guernsey, Jersey, Brown Swiss and Holstein cows.
- **Jasper Hill Farm, Greensboro (cheese since 2003).** **Bayley Hazen Blue** is a raw-milk, natural-rind blue from the farm's own herd. It's named for a Revolutionary War military road commissioned by George Washington; no major battle happened on it, but it brought Greensboro its first settlers. Wheels are dry-salted and pierced at the Cellars at Jasper Hill. It was served at the White House state dinner for French President François Hollande in February 2014, a nice reversal for a blue. It was named World's Best Unpasteurized Cheese at the 2014 World Cheese Awards and Best American Cheese at the 2024 World Cheese Awards, finishing in the top 14 of 4,786 entries. Jasper Hill calls the recipe an American original. **Withersbrook Blue** is a small cube matured 60 days, then packed in Eden Ice Cider. It's named for a brook that runs through the farm's sugarbush. It took 2nd Best in Show at the 2025 American Cheese Society competition (Vermont Cheese Council).
- **Parish Hill Creamery, Westminster West (2013).** Peter Dixon and Rachel Fritz Schaal make only raw-milk cheese, and only when the cows of Elm Lea Farm, the Putney School's herd, are on pasture. Their starter cultures are clabbered milk from four named cows, Sonia, Helga, Clothilde and Abigail, kept going since the creamery started: a ready "Living Thing." **West West Blue** is described as a traditional two-curd Gorgonzola, the Lombard *due paste* method. It won a Super Gold at the 2025 World Cheese Awards in Bern. **Jack's Blue**, named for their late dog, won a Super Gold in 2024. The blue is aged in a converted refrigerated trailer fitted with wooden shelves (*Brattleboro Reformer*). Dixon has trained and consulted for many Vermont makers over three decades.
- **von Trapp Farmstead, Waitsfield.** **Mad River Blue**, a natural-rind blue from the family's pasteurized organic cow's milk.
- **Blue Ledge Farm, Salisbury.** **Middlebury Blue**, a pasteurized cow's-milk blue aged 60 days, from cows on a neighboring farm. The farm's pun is "town meets gown."
- **Champlain Valley Creamery, Middlebury.** **Bleu de Champlain**, launched in 2023 for the creamery's 20th anniversary, made with organic milk from Severy Farm.

## Story seeds

- **The cheese you build for mold.** Every choice, from salt and acid to loose curd and the needle, is about making a home for one organism. A maker can explain a blue by explaining how they make room for the mold (Why It Tastes Like This; The Living Thing).
- **The needle.** Piercing is a strong Hands image: a specific day, a specific number of holes, and the moment air reaches the inside of the wheel.
- **A cave, or what passes for one.** Roquefort had limestone caves with fissures; Minnesota had sandstone caves; Parish Hill has a converted reefer trailer. Wherever a maker ages their blue is part of the same story.
- **Chosen ancestors.** A two-curd blue points to Lombardy. A Fourme d'Ambert-based blue points to the Auvergne. A maker who chose a tradition on purpose has a Chosen Ancestors story ready, if they say which one and why.
- **The upstart at the White House.** A Vermont blue served to a French president is The Upstart in one line. Award plus a sensory line ("fudgy, with toasted nut and anise") beats either alone.
- **Named for a road, a brook, a dog, a woman and her road.** Vermont blue names are unusually personal (Bayley Hazen, Withersbrook, Jack's Blue, Gore-Dawn-Zola). The Name element is strong here.
- **Blue for people who think they hate blue.** Many Vermont blues are described as subdued, sweet or fudgy rather than fierce. One accurate sensory line can turn a skeptic, and accuracy matters, because the cheese has to meet the description.

## Careful

- **The Roquefort shepherd is a legend.** Even Société, the largest Roquefort brand, tells it with "Or so the legend goes!" The same goes for the Gorgonzola lovers' story. Tell either as a legend, never as history.
- **Don't claim Charlemagne ate Roquefort.** A 9th-century story has Charlemagne eating a bishop's cheese. Some writers say it was Roquefort or a blue; Kindstedt calls that a leap of faith. Claims that Pliny described Roquefort are also disputed.
- **Gorgonzola's 879 date is a claim**, and the town's claim to be the birthplace is contested (Pasturo). Say "dates to the Middle Ages, by tradition."
- **Stilton origins are disputed.** The makers' association and the village society disagree on whether the cheese was ever made in Stilton and who "invented" it. Frances Pawlett was born around 1720, so she couldn't have invented a cheese that was already famous by 1724; she developed and standardized it.
- **"Only Vermont blue for wholesale in 1999"** and the 2026 ACS results for Green Mountain Blue are the company's own claims.
- **Blue Ledge Farm sources disagree** on the breed behind Middlebury Blue (Ayrshire on the product page, Normande on the farm's story page). Ask the maker.
- **Raw vs. pasteurized:** Bayley Hazen and Parish Hill's blues are raw milk; Mad River Blue and Middlebury Blue are pasteurized. Never assume.
