# Alpine cheese: the history, from the high pastures to Vermont

*Background for makers of Alpine-style cheeses: Tarentaise, Gruyère-, Comté-, Beaufort-, Appenzeller- and raclette-style wheels, mountain tommes. It covers where these cheeses came from, why they look and taste the way they do, and how the tradition reached Vermont. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012), plus the archaeology in the existing kit file `alpine-deep-history.md` and the sources listed at the end.*

---

## The short version

Alpine cheese is what happens when a whole village sends its cows up a mountain for the summer and needs the milk to come back down as food that lasts all winter. Everything about these cheeses follows from that one situation:
- the big wheels
- the curd cut small and cooked hot
- the low salt
- the nutty, sweet flavor and the holes

People have been doing some version of it in the Alps for thousands of years. Monasteries, counts and city-states turned it into a trade. Swiss immigrants brought it to America. In the 2000s, Vermont makers chose it on purpose, because Vermont's hills, grass and winters look a lot like the Alps.

---

## Why Alpine cheese exists: the problem it solved

Kindstedt lays out the mountain cheesemaker's dilemma clearly.

- **The setup.** Each lowland family had only two or three cows. Villages pooled them into one herd that a few chosen cowmen drove up to the summer pastures, while everyone else stayed below to make hay for winter. The cowmen milked and made cheese in rough sheds, and later in chalets.
- **Constraint 1: lots of milk at once.** A whole village's milk arrived twice a day. It made sense to turn it into a few big cheeses rather than many small ones, and big cheeses were easier to carry down the mountain.
- **Constraint 2: clean, cold milk that acidifies slowly.** Mountain milk was made right after milking, or cooled overnight in the mountain air. It had few lactic bacteria, so acid built slowly and the curd held on to its whey.
- **Constraint 3: salt was precious.** Every pound had to be packed up the mountain, and the cows needed it too, so it was used sparingly.
- **The catch.** Big wheels, slow acid and little salt are exactly the recipe for a wet cheese that rots.
- **The solution.** Cut the curd very small and cook it hot, which drives out the whey without relying on acid or salt. The result is a large, dense, low-salt, low-acid wheel that keeps for a year or more.
- **The flavor is a side effect of the solution.** Low acid and low salt let propionic bacteria grow. They make the eyes in Emmental and much of the sweet, nutty background flavor of the whole family. The same conditions let orange smear bacteria grow on the rind. Makers either scrub them off (Emmental) or encourage them (Gruyère).
- **Kindstedt's summary:** by the end of the Middle Ages, a whole family of moderate-to-large, wheel-shaped, firm, long-keeping cheeses was being made across the Alps. What united them was seasonal herding up and down the mountain, called transhumance.

## Deep history (about 3500 BC to AD 500)

- **The climate opened the mountains.** Between about 3500 and 2500 BC, colder winters pushed the Alpine tree line down by as much as 1,000 feet, opening new high grassland. Farming and summer herding reached the Swiss plateau and Alps in the fourth millennium BC. Kindstedt dates the beginning of the traditional Alpine model (summer pasture up high, winter stalls and fodder below) to the turn from the fourth to the third millennium BC.
- **Leaves before hay.** Early Alpine farmers wintered their animals on twigs and leaves cut from the forest; there were no hayfields yet. Hay became the main winter feed only around the end of the second millennium BC, after lowland clearing. Swiss farmers were still using leaf fodder as a supplement into the 1900s.
- **Cheese in the high pastures about 3,000 years ago.** Iron Age pottery from stone huts above the tree line in the Swiss Alps carries the chemical trace of heated milk. The huts are much like the ones Alpine dairy managers still use. (University of York/Newcastle, *PLOS ONE*, 2016; see `alpine-deep-history.md`.)
- **The Celts.** Kindstedt traces Alpine cheesemaking through the Celtic peoples of central Europe. They were dairy farmers famous in the ancient world for their cows, and they had centuries of experience making cheese from big herds' milk on the mountain. Their iron and bronze cauldrons could have done the cooking.
- **Rome's taste for mountain cheese.**
  - The Greek geographer Strabo (first century AD) described cheese being made all along the northern slopes of the Alps and brought down to the valley towns.
  - Pliny the Elder wrote that a cheese from the Centronian Alps was a favorite in Rome; he called it Vatusican. The Centrones were the people of the Tarentaise valley in Savoie, which is Beaufort country today. The physician Galen also named Vatusican as Rome's best and most popular cheese.
  - Nobody knows exactly what Vatusican was like. A large cooked Beaufort-type cheese is the best guess, but it is a guess.

## The Middle Ages: monks, counts and the cheese trade

- **Saint Gall's "Alp cheeses."**
  - The Abbey of Saint Gall in Switzerland received tithes of large round cheeses from its farm estates by the 800s. They were recorded as *casei alpini*, "Alp cheeses," about the size of Swiss Alpine wheels of the early 1900s, alongside small "hand cheeses."
  - In the 900s the abbey was given wilderness land in Appenzell. By the 1000s, Appenzell farmers were sending the abbey more than 2,000 cheeses a year in tithes. Appenzeller cheese is still made there.
- **Muri's start-up package.**
  - In the 1000s the Abbey of Muri, near Lake Lucerne, needed settlers to clear its wild land. It offered each family a plough, a wagon, four oxen, a sow, a rooster and two hens, a scythe, an axe and seed.
  - Settlers organized in groups of twelve households, pooling their cows under a master cowman for the summer. A monastic officer inspected their mountain chalets every year.
  - The cheese tithe was due at the abbey on the Feast of Saint Andrew, November 30.
- **The Counts of Gruyère.**
  - In the 1000s the Holy Roman Emperor gave the Counts of Gruyère authority over the highlands. As the cheese trade grew, the counts took control of the high pastures and the traditional summer migration routes.
  - By the 1300s, Gruyère cheese was famous far away. The wheels grew larger so they could travel overland. Eventually they were sized to pack ten to a barrel and ship by boat from Lake Geneva down the Rhône to the Mediterranean.
  - The Gruyère consortium dates cheesemaking in the region to 1115.
- **Bern takes over, and Emmental is born.** The trade was so lucrative that the powerful city-state of Bern took the Gruyère highlands from the counts during the 1400s. Bern then recruited Gruyère's highland cheesemakers to settle in the Emme valley, the Emmental, to improve the cheese there. Those transplanted makers created the big wheels that became Emmental.
- **Comté and the village cooperative.** On the French side of the Jura, villages formed *fruitières*: shared cheesemaking houses where many small farms pooled their milk to make big wheels none could make alone. The first records date from the mid-1200s; official Comté history names 1253 at Déservillers. Records from the 1260s–1280s distinguish *vachelin*, cow's-milk cheese, from *chevrotin*, goat's-milk cheese. About 1,800 fruitières existed in the late 1800s. 134 make Comté today, each drawing milk from an average of 17 farms within about eight miles. It is the same idea Vermont's first cheese factories and creameries ran on: small farms pooling milk.
- **Beaufort.** The Savoie cheese still has a special designation, *chalet d'alpage*, for wheels made in summer mountain chalets from Tarine (Tarentaise) and Abondance cows. Its concave sides come from the beechwood hoop.
- **Alpine know-how went south.** Kindstedt thinks Italy's grana cheeses (Parmigiano-Reggiano, Grana Padano) borrowed the Alpine technique of small curd and high cooking through the monastic network. Monks drained the Po Valley swamps and needed a way to use their large dairy herds.

## Alpine cheese comes to America (1845–1930)

- **New Glarus, Wisconsin.**
  - In 1845 an emigration society in the Swiss canton of Glarus bought 1,200 acres in Green County, Wisconsin. Of the 193 colonists who set out, 131 arrived.
  - Nick Gerber opened a Limburger factory there in 1867–68 and a Swiss (Emmental-style) factory in 1869.
  - By 1925 Green County made about a third of America's Swiss cheese.
- **"Mountain flavor."** In 1927 the Swiss Cheese Association of Bern campaigned that real Swiss cheese could only come from Switzerland because of its "mountain flavor." It was an early round of a fight that is still going.
- **Swiss cheese went industrial.** Kindstedt notes that "Swiss" (Emmental) became a huge American factory cheese because the technology scaled. Most other immigrant cheeses that couldn't scale disappeared by mid-century.
- **The name fight today.** In December 2021 a federal court ruled that "gruyere" is a generic term in the United States, and the Fourth Circuit affirmed on March 3, 2023. The reasons: the FDA has had a standard for "gruyere" since 1977, and plenty of cheese from outside the region had been sold under that name. In Europe, Le Gruyère is a protected name (Swiss AOC since 2001).

## Vermont's Alpine turn (2000s to now)

- **Why the Alps.** John and Janine Putnam of Thistle Hill Farm, North Pomfret, toured Alpine cheesemakers in France, Italy and Switzerland in 2000. They chose the Tarentaise valley style because, as they told NPR, the Alpine climate is the closest match to Vermont's. They made their first Tarentaise on July 5, 2002.
  - Their copper vat, custom-built in Switzerland, was the first in Vermont.
  - Their organic Jerseys graze, and they make cheese only in the grazing months.
  - Their rennet is cultured from the whey of earlier batches.
  - The first cheese won a silver at the American Cheese Society in 2003.
- **Pliny's cheese, reborn?** The Tarentaise valley is the same Centronian Alps whose cheese Pliny praised in Rome 2,000 years ago. A Vermont Tarentaise is named for one of the oldest documented cheese regions in Europe.
- **Spring Brook Farm, Reading.** It licensed the Tarentaise recipe from the Putnams and has made it since 2008 in copper vats from raw Jersey milk. The farm is home to the Farms for City Kids Foundation, which brings city schoolchildren to work on the farm. John Putnam said they knew the cheese would come out different and would celebrate that difference. Spring Brook's Tarentaise Reserve won Best of Show at the American Cheese Society in 2014.
- **Jasper Hill, Greensboro.**
  - Alpha Tolman is an Appenzeller-inspired raw-milk wheel, so the Saint Gall–Appenzell line runs right into the Northeast Kingdom. It is named for a Greensboro dairy farmer who built the town library in 1900.
  - It was named the Highest Scoring Raw Milk Cheese at the 2025 American Cheese Society awards and won a Super Gold at the World Cheese Awards in 2012.
  - Jasper Hill also makes the Alpine-style Whitney and Calderwood.
- **von Trapp Farmstead, Waitsfield.** Werner von Trapp of the Trapp Family Singers bought the farm in 1959. His grandson Sebastian started cheesemaking in 2009. Their Alpine-leaning cheeses include Savage, named for the 1700s settler of the land; Oma, a washed-rind tomme; and Mt. Alice.
- **Others in the family:** Consider Bardwell's Rupert (now made by Woodlawn Creamery), Cobb Hill's Ascutney Mountain (Hartland), and Mt. Mansfield Creamery's Chin Clip (Austrian Alpine recipe) and Halfpipe (French Alpine).
- **A Swiss view of Vermont.** Walter Räss, the Swiss maker of prize-winning Appenzeller and Chällerhocker, has put the difference this way (to Adam Davidson): in Switzerland we follow recipes; Vermont has cheesemakers. Vermont makers in this tradition aren't reenacting a recipe. They are doing what the Alpine makers did: fitting a cheese to their own mountain, grass, animals and weather.

## Story seeds

- **A whole village in one wheel.** Alpine cheese was always communal: pooled cows, chosen cowmen, a shared chalet or fruitière. A Vermont wheel made from one herd, or one valley's milk, sits in that tradition.
- **Summer, carried into winter.** These cheeses turn a short season of mountain grass into food for the long winter. Seasonal Vermont makers who only make cheese on pasture are doing exactly that.
- **Every feature is a solution.** The size, the cooking, the low salt and the holes each answer a mountain problem. A maker can explain the cheese by explaining the problems.
- **Copper and whey.** Copper vats and rennet cultured from yesterday's whey connect a Vermont make room to centuries of Alpine practice.
- **The Romans loved it too.** Rome imported Alpine cheese and sang its praises. A cheese named Tarentaise points back to Pliny.
- **The cows come home.** The *désalpe* or *Alpabzug* is the autumn parade of flower-crowned cows coming down from the summer pastures, usually in September. It's a ready-made seasonal image for any Vermont maker marking the end of grazing.
- **Vermont looks like the Alps.** This is the reason Vermont makers themselves give for choosing the style.

## Careful

- **Say "about 3,000 years" for cheese in the high Alps**, and "more than 7,000 years" for cheese in general. Don't say a specific modern cheese is ancient. The tradition is; the recipe isn't.
- **Vatusican is not known to be Beaufort.** "Possibly a forerunner" is as far as the evidence goes.
- **Fruitière dates vary** (1253 vs. 1263). "Since the mid-1200s" is safe.
- **The Gruyère dates** (1115; AOC 2001; later AOP renaming) come from the consortium and sources vary on the later ones.
- **Spring Brook's cheese is made under license** from Thistle Hill's recipe. Credit both if telling that story.
- **The von Trapp family history is Austrian.** There's no source tying the cheese recipes themselves to Austria.
