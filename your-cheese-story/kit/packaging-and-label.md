# Packaging and Label

**Purpose:** The few words a cheese carries on its own body: a name and one phrase on the front, a short story on the back, and the right small text for a band on a whole wheel or a sticker on a cut wedge.

## Who reads it and how

A shopper holding a wedge for a few seconds, usually after a monger has said something about it; a guest turning the wrapper over at home; a monger opening a wheel. The label's job is narrow. In focus groups, packaging had little influence on what shoppers bought; tasting and the monger did more. In a packaging experiment, a photo of the producer raised perceived quality and authenticity, while a paragraph about the producer lowered them. So the label names the cheese, gives one true phrase, shows a face if there's room, and points to the longer story.

## What it needs from what the maker has gathered

The essential story and outline; the name and why it's called that; milk, rind, age, format; the maker's tasting words; one fact from The Hands or What We Won't Do; any seasonal marker; a photo of the maker at work; and where the fuller story lives (website, QR target). Regulatory facts belong to Check the facts, not here.

## Structure

1. **Front: the name** (as the maker spells it) and the creamery name.
2. **Front: one phrase** (under 12 words). The essential story pressed down to something a shopper could repeat. One concrete thing, not a category claim.
3. **Back panel story** (40 to 70 words). Who makes it, one image of the work, one taste line pairing a broad word with a specific one ("nutty, like toasted hazelnut"). End with where to read more.
4. **Face slot** (optional, strongly suggested). One small photo of the maker doing something. Write a five-to-eight-word caption if any.
5. **Whole-wheel band or paper label.** Name, phrase, creamery, and the wheel's own marker if the maker uses one: make month, pasture or hay season, batch. This is what a monger reads when they open the wheel; it can carry one more technical fact than the wedge.
6. **Cut-wedge sticker** (under 20 words besides the name). Shops usually print their own scale label with weight and price, so the maker's sticker sits next to it. Name, creamery, town, and a shortened phrase. Nothing that needs the back panel to make sense.
7. **Required information panel.** Kept apart from everything above. See the next section.

Total story words across front and back: under 90.

## What a cheese label typically has to carry

**Confirm with your state inspector; this is not legal advice.** In Vermont, that's the Agency of Agriculture, Food and Markets dairy section. These items are listed here so the story pieces stay clear of them. Every one is checked in Check the facts, not written in the story work.

- **Statement of identity.** The common or usual name of the food, on the front (principal display panel). For cheeses with a federal standard, such as cheddar, that's the standard name ("cheddar cheese"). A fanciful name like "Hollow Tomme" is a brand name; ask the inspector whether the label also needs a plain description.
- **Net quantity.** Weight in ounces or pounds and grams, as a separate item in the bottom 30 percent of the front panel. Random-weight wedges are usually weighed and labeled at the shop.
- **Ingredients.** In descending order by weight, for example milk, salt, cultures, enzymes or rennet. Name the kind of milk.
- **Name and place of business** of the maker, packer, or distributor: name, city, state, ZIP (street address too unless it's in a current directory). If someone else makes it, "Made for" or "Distributed by."
- **Allergen.** Milk is a major food allergen. It's declared either in the ingredient list or in a "Contains: milk" statement right after it.
- **Raw milk.** Federal rules require cheese from unpasteurized milk to be aged at least 60 days at 35°F or above. Whether the label must say "raw milk" or "aged over 60 days" depends on the state (Wisconsin requires "aged over 60 days," for example); ask what Vermont expects. A citizen petition dated October 2026 asks FDA to require a warning statement on raw-milk cheese. That's a request, not a rule.
- **Nutrition Facts.** Required on packaged food unless an exemption applies; FDA has a small-business exemption for low-volume products that needs an annual notice.

## Rules

- The phrase and back panel come from the approved story, never a fresh idea invented on the label.
- Public-facing: every fact is maker-confirmed or sourced. Gaps show as `[ask: …]`.
- One phrase, one image of the work, one taste line. Words about the maker stay few; the photo does that job.
- "Made in Vermont" alone earns little outside the state. Put a demonstrated detail next to it.
- Don't rely on a printed certification logo; in one study, printing an eco-label erased the premium the certification had earned.
- Weak words need something specific beside them; banned words stay out.
- Tasting lines must be accurate. A cheese that meets or slightly beats its description pleases most.

## Weak and strong

Weak (fictional Birch Hollow Creamery):
> **Front:** HOLLOW TOMME — A handcrafted artisan cheese from the rolling hills of Vermont.
> **Back:** At Birch Hollow, we make our cheese with passion using time-honored traditional methods. Our family has always believed in quality. Every bite is a unique flavor journey. Enjoy with wine, crackers, and fruit!

It fails because any cheese could wear it; "handcrafted," "artisan," and "unique" claim instead of show; "rolling hills" and "flavor journey" are banned; there's no checkable taste; and the family paragraph does what the research says producer paragraphs do.

Strong:
> **Front:** HOLLOW TOMME · Birch Hollow Creamery
> *Made only while the cows are on pasture.*
> **Back:** Ana Ruiz makes this cheese from May to October, from the milk of her 22 Jerseys on grass, and stops when they come in for winter. Each wheel is turned by hand every week for at least five months. Expect brown butter and cut grass, with a little mushroom near the rind. The full story: `[ask: website or QR link]`
> *[Photo: Ana turning wheels on the aging shelves.]*
> **Wheel band:** Hollow Tomme · raw cow's milk, natural rind · made `[ask: make month and year for this wheel]` from pasture milk.
> **Wedge sticker:** Hollow Tomme · summer milk only · Birch Hollow Creamery, `[ask: town]`, Vermont.

## Self-check

- Could a shopper repeat the front phrase after one look?
- Is every story fact traceable to the maker or a source, with gaps left as `[ask: …]`?
- Is the back panel under 70 words, with a face slot instead of more words about the maker?
- Does the wedge sticker make sense on its own?
- Are all the regulatory items kept off the story work and handed to Check the facts?
