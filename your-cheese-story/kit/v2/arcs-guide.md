# Arcs Guide

*The center of Your Cheese Story. Carried in: next prompts, ready check, door check, ideas, look closer, outline, beat edit. Part 1 is the ten arcs and their elements, each with its key question, unlock question, outline beats and best cases. Part 2 is how to judge coverage and strength. Part 3 is how the arcs steer Gathering and readiness. Part 4 is the rules for three ideas and a pick. Part 5 is the rules for the outline. Part 6 is the leftovers.*

---

# Part 1. Story arcs and elements

*Story arcs are the spines a maker can choose. Elements are the scenes and proofs that make a spine feel true. A maker usually carries one arc, sometimes two braided together, where one raises a question the other answers. We interview for elements, and the elements show which arcs the maker can carry.*

*Eight arcs are about the creamery and the people in it. Two, The Make and The Reinvention, are about one cheese: what was decided in the vat and the cave, and what an old cheese becomes when it's rethought here. A creamery with several cheeses can carry one of these per cheese.*

**Bold** = necessary or highly recommended. Plain = possible.

Each arc below ends with four additions:
- **Key question:** the question the whole arc answers. Use it to test whether an idea built on this arc has a center.
- **Unlock question:** the single best question to put to the maker when the arc is thin. It follows the interviewing rules (one concrete thing, plain, under 18 words, no numbers or dates).
- **Outline beats:** the arc's beats in story order, each with a short plain label and the elements it draws on. Use 5–7. Plain-element beats are optional; bold-element beats are the spine.
- **Best cases:** real cheeses that show the arc done well, from Vermont where possible. They show the AI and the maker what a strong version looks like. **They are models, never material:** no fact from a best case is ever carried into this maker's story. Full sources and longer notes are in `kit/story-arc-best-cases.md`.

## Shared elements (used across many arcs)

**A face.** A real photo of the maker, ideally doing something. In a packaging experiment, a picture of the producer raised perceived quality and authenticity, while a paragraph about the producer had the opposite effect. Show the person; keep the words short.

**What We Won't Do.** The constraint that costs the maker something. It works when the cost is concrete and someone could check it: "when the pasture isn't right, we sell the milk" (Uplands). Weak: "we never cut corners." Strong: a specific refusal and what it costs.

**This Wheel, This Season.** Seasonal variation announced in advance as proof of place. It works with a visible marker and named months: summer Comté is a deeper yellow, Beaufort puts three seasonal tiers on the label, Rush Creek is made only in autumn and sells out. Weak: "flavors vary with the seasons." Strong: what changes, when, and what you'll taste.

**The Hockey Puck.** The failure that taught them. It works in the maker's own words, a little funny, and followed by what they did next. Cindy Major's "worst cheese ever, like eating a hockey puck" works because the next beat is David Major going to the Pyrenees. Without the next beat it's just a joke or a complaint. Never invent one.

**The Hands.** One concrete image of the work, with a time or a number attached: the 4 a.m. milking, turning the wheels by hand every week, the fact that ripening is more than 70% of the labor in a natural-rind cheese. People value results more when they can see the work behind them. One image beats "small-batch" and "handcrafted."

**The Living Thing.** Something alive, named specifically: the cave, the rind, the named cow, the farm's own spruce bark. "We milk cows, but we actually farm microbes" (Jasper Hill). It works as wonder, not a science lecture, and it turns wheel-to-wheel variation into a virtue.

**The Name.** Why the cheese is called what it is. It works when it's short and has a person or place behind it: Harbison is named for Anne Harbison, the "grandmother of Greensboro." Customers ask, so it's built for the counter.

**The Upstart.** An award or recognition placed against the old world or a giant: a Vermont blue served to the president of France, 33 awards won in Bern, a farm founded in 1998 beating every cheese in America. The context makes it a story; a list of medals is just a sticker. Award plus a sensory line outperforms either alone.

**For the Table (the ending).** One line a guest could say while passing the board. Cheese is mostly bought to share, so the buyer is the real teller. It works when it's short, vivid, true, and carries a feeling of care. The "made with love" effect is strongest for gifts to people close to the buyer.

**Shared elements in outlines.** Any of these can become a beat in any arc where it fits. For the Table is the default last beat of every outline. A face is never a written beat; it is a photo, noted as needed.

## Taste of Place
*You can taste where this came from.*

- **What the animals eat, and when.** It works when it's concrete and seasonal: which grasses, which months on pasture, when the hay starts. The buyer can picture it, and ideally see or taste it in the cheese. For cheese, diet and season are the most tasteable parts of place.
- **One specific, named place.** It works at a small scale with a proper name, like the field, the ridge, or the landform (Pleasant Ridge Reserve is named for the ground under the farm). "Vermont" alone is weak in city markets; a name at farm scale carries.
- **One sensory line that links the place to the flavor.** One causal clause, not a lecture. It has to match what the taster actually gets, because the best response comes when the cheese meets or slightly beats the description.
- **This Wheel, This Season.** (Shared.)
- The herd and how it's kept: facts about this herd, not the breed in general.
- The Living Thing. (Shared.)
- What the land was before: works when it's specific and sourced, like cleared for sheep in the 1800s, or a named river or valley.
- What We Won't Do. (Shared.)
- The Name, when it comes from the place. (Shared.)

**Key question:** What does this place do to the cheese? *(from good-story-patterns, "Place as active force")*

**Unlock question:** "What are the animals eating right now, and what changes when they go out on grass?"

**Outline beats:**
1. **The place** — One specific, named place; what the land was before.
2. **What the animals eat** — What the animals eat, and when; the herd and how it's kept.
3. **What lives here** *(optional)* — The Living Thing: the cave, the rind, the named cow.
4. **The season** — This Wheel, This Season.
5. **The taste** — One sensory line that links the place to the flavor.
6. **The ending** — For the Table.

**Best cases:**
- *Pleasant Ridge Reserve (Uplands, Wisconsin).* Made only from milk produced while the cows are on pasture, and only on days the pasture is right, across a rotation of separate paddocks. Diet, season and a named ridge carry the whole story; the golden color is the visible proof.
- *Winnimere (Jasper Hill).* Made only in winter, from rich hay-fed milk, and wrapped in bark from the farm's own woods. Place shows up twice: in what the cows eat that season and in the trees around the barn.
- *Comté.* Summer wheels are a deeper yellow than winter ones, and the trade sells that difference rather than hiding it.

## Chosen Ancestors
*This person went and found an old tradition and made it live here.*

- **The tradition and its deep history.** It works when the history hangs on a practice still in use, like a vessel, a technique, or a kind of rind, rather than "for centuries." The kit's history files supply the background. Never claim the maker follows a historical method unless they say so.
- **The journey of choosing.** It works with names and a moment: the place they went, the mentor who taught them, the first taste that hooked them. A trip, not a sentiment.
- **Why this tradition fits this place.** It works as a physical reason framed as a choice, not a copy. Thistle Hill picked Tarentaise because the Alps' climate matches Vermont's. Uplands: "we weren't trying to copy an old-world cheese."
- **What they changed and made their own.** This is where Walter Räss's line lives: in Switzerland they follow recipes, Vermont has cheesemakers. Without it, the story is a reenactment. With it, the maker is the protagonist. Changes framed as a return to the tradition's roots go over especially well.
- The Hockey Puck: the failure that sent them looking. (Shared.)
- An object carried from the source, like a Swiss copper vat. It works because the object is still in use.
- The mentor's own words: one quoted line, ideally something the mentor said to them.
- The Upstart: beating the old world on its own ground. (Shared.)
- What the style originally solved: the old problem (a mountain winter, a long trip to market) that explains why the cheese is the way it is.

**Key question:** What problem did the original style solve, and what problem or desire does this version answer now? *(from good-story-patterns, "The old solution in a new place")*

**Unlock question:** "Where did you first learn this kind of cheese, and who showed you?"

**Outline beats:**
1. **The problem** — the maker's situation that the cheese answers (often the land), or The Hockey Puck that sent them looking.
2. **The old answer** — The tradition and its deep history; what the style originally solved.
3. **The choice** — The journey of choosing; the mentor's own words; an object carried from the source.
4. **Why it fits here** — Why this tradition fits this place.
5. **Making it yours** — What they changed and made their own.
6. **The taste** — a sensory line, or The Upstart (award plus sensory line).
7. **The ending** — For the Table.

*Worked example (von Trapp, from the flow):* rocky land, not good for lots of cows, so bulk dairy isn't an option (The problem) → Alpine cheese solved the same problem in the mountains, and people have made it for thousands of years (The old answer) → the family went to cheese (The choice) → it fit this land (Why it fits here) → how von Trapp made it their own (Making it yours) → you taste that inventiveness and that land (The taste). The method's history is claimed, not the location's; the place is the new chapter.

**Best cases:**
- *Vermont Shepherd (Westminster West).* A failed first cheese, then a trip to the Pyrenees to learn how it's done there. The journey of choosing, with a mentor and a place, is the spine.
- *Pleasant Ridge Reserve (Uplands).* The founder set out to make a farmstead cheese modeled on the French Alps, learned Beaufort with cheese experts at the University of Wisconsin, and changed the recipe for a wheel about a ninth the size of a traditional Beaufort. Tradition, teacher and the change are all on the record.
- *Thistle Hill Farm Tarentaise (North Pomfret).* A Savoie cheese chosen because the Alpine climate matches Vermont's, made only from the farm's Jersey milk while the cows are on pasture. Why it fits here, stated as a choice.

## The Inheritance
*This family has worked this land for generations, and cheese is the newest chapter.*

- **Dated, specific family history.** It works with dates tied to events and what people did. In a wine experiment, founding dates and production methods raised perceived heritage; naming family members alone didn't.
- **The method or recipe that was handed down.** It works when it's a practice still done today. People feel a product's essence travels through the original process: a grandmother's recipe, a way of cutting curd, an old cellar.
- **The link from past to present.** It works when this generation's addition reads as continuation, an unbroken chain. Without the new chapter, it's a museum.
- Old photos and objects: one strong image beats an archive.
- The Name, after an ancestor. (Shared.)
- The Working Landscape: cheese as how the farm stayed in the family.
- Second Life beats.

**Key question:** What was handed down that is still done today, and what is this generation adding to it?

**Unlock question:** "What does your family still do the same way it was done before you?"

**Outline beats:**
1. **The family on this land** — Dated, specific family history (in Gathering, get the events and what people did; exact dates come at the fact check).
2. **What was handed down** — The method or recipe that was handed down.
3. **The turn to cheese** — The Working Landscape: cheese as how the farm stayed in the family.
4. **The new chapter** — The link from past to present.
5. **The thing you can see** *(optional)* — old photos and objects; The Name after an ancestor.
6. **The ending** — For the Table.

**Best cases:**
- *Oma (von Trapp Farmstead).* Each generation added its own chapter: the grandparents started dairying in the Mad River Valley, the parents went organic, the grandsons added the creamery. The cheese is named for the grandmother, who championed the farm and the cheese and joked about having a stinky cheese named after her. The joke is the image people remember.
- *Crowley Cheese (Healdville).* A factory built in 1881 to turn neighbors' surplus milk into cheese, still making cheese with its original methods and recipes in the original building. The handed-down method is the whole story.

## Why It Tastes Like This
*Every choice we make shows up in your mouth.*

- **One causal link from a process choice to a taste.** One clause the buyer can confirm on the first bite. Uplands: grass-fed sweetness meets the savory washed rind. As one importer put it, it's easy to say a maker is the best; it's far more powerful to explain why.
- **Sensory language.** One broad term a novice already uses, plus one specific term that teaches ("nutty, like toasted hazelnut"). Jasper Hill's lines show the range: "caramel sweetness and savory notes of chicken broth." A sensory shelf talker raised what people would pay more than an award sticker did. It must be accurate.
- **What We Won't Do.** (Shared.)
- How the herd or milk is chosen: works when it's a specific criterion, not "only the best."
- How fast the milk gets from animal to vat: works as a number (minutes, feet from the barn).
- Ripening details: one specific act (brushing, washing, turning) tied to the flavor it creates.
- The Hands. (Shared.)
- The Upstart: an award as outside proof. (Shared.)
- A pairing: specific and a little surprising. It gives the buyer an occasion to open it.

**Key question:** What does knowing this story help someone notice while eating? *(from good-story-patterns, "The table story")*. When the cheese surprises people, use the sensory-reversal form instead: what does the taster expect, and what actually happens?

**Unlock question:** "What's one thing you do in the make room that you can taste in the cheese?"

**Outline beats:**
1. **The first bite** — Sensory language: the broad word and the teaching word.
2. **The choice behind it** — One causal link from a process choice to a taste; how the herd or milk is chosen; how fast the milk gets to the vat.
3. **What we won't do** — What We Won't Do.
4. **The work** — ripening details; The Hands.
5. **The proof** *(optional)* — The Upstart.
6. **The ending** — For the Table, or a pairing.

*Why It Tastes Like This and The Make are close kin. Why It Tastes Like This gives a general buyer one choice and one taste. The Make gives a professional the full set of decisions behind one cheese.*

**Best cases:**
- *Savage (von Trapp Farmstead).* The cows get a varied diet with very little grain and plenty of exercise, so they give less milk with more flavor. One choice, one consequence, told plainly.
- *Pleasant Ridge Reserve (Uplands).* One retailer's tasting note dares the customer to find the pineapple. A specific, surprising flavor word gives the buyer something to notice and say.

## The Calling
*This person gave something up to do this, and keeps going.*

- **The motive in their own words.** It works when the motive is craft, land, animals, or community, and when it's told as a story rather than a list of values. Audiences judge whether the maker acted for good reasons.
- **The disadvantage or struggle.** It works when it's specific and external: cost, scale, weather, a market that wouldn't pay. Hardship alone reads as complaint.
- **The determination.** The act that shows they kept going, like what they did the next morning. Disadvantage and determination together are what make the underdog effect work. It lands most strongly when people buy for themselves.
- **A face.** (Shared.)
- What they left behind: it works as a sacrifice. If it reads as a lifestyle purchase, it turns into a different story.
- The Hockey Puck. (Shared.)
- The Hands. (Shared.)
- A hard year told straight: candor about a bad batch or season builds trust.
- The moment they knew: a scene, with a place and a time.

**Key question:** What pressure shaped this cheese, and what did the maker do the next morning? *(adapted from good-story-patterns, "The maker's impossible choice")*

**Unlock question:** "What's a year that nearly broke the farm, and how did you get through it?"

**Outline beats:**
1. **Before** — What they left behind.
2. **The moment they knew** — the scene; The motive in their own words.
3. **What stood in the way** — The disadvantage or struggle.
4. **The hard year** *(optional)* — The Hockey Puck, or a hard year told straight.
5. **The next morning** — The determination.
6. **The work now** — The Hands (with A face as the photo).
7. **The ending** — For the Table.

**Best cases:**
- *Vermont Shepherd.* "Worst cheese ever, like eating a hockey puck," then the trip to the Pyrenees. The failure is funny, in the maker's words, and followed at once by the next move.
- *Thistle Hill Farm.* A lawyer who left a Boston law firm to make Tarentaise from the farm's own Jerseys. When the restaurant accounts disappeared, the couple went back to the farmers market and leaned into food co-ops. Left behind, struggle and next move are all specific.

## The Working Landscape
*Buying this cheese keeps this land open and working.*

- **This farm's own place on the curve.** It works only as the maker tells it, with a specific local fact (Jasper Hill bought its farm the year about a third of Greensboro's dairies sold their cows). Vermont's statewide numbers are backdrop, never borrowed as this farm's story.
- **What cheese made possible that selling milk alone couldn't.** A concrete before and after: the acres still open, the barn still standing, the people still employed.
- **The forward stake: what this land is becoming.** Future tense, hope rather than elegy. The research points to this as Vermont's most distinctive story, because American terroir earns its credibility through stewardship rather than age.
- **A value the buyer can see.** Fair wages and how the land is treated, shown as facts rather than badges. City buyers pay more for these than for "made in Vermont." A label claiming green credentials once erased the premium the certification earned, so this works best as the reason the cheese tastes the way it does.
- Vermont's farm numbers (2,770 to 439) as backdrop.
- Neighbors and the local economy: named neighbors, a radius (Jasper Hill talks about the economy within 15 miles).
- A published number: what they pay for milk, or what workers earn. Numbers do what adjectives can't.
- Programs that open the farm to others.

**Key question:** What does this cheese keep open and working that selling milk alone couldn't? *(adapted from good-story-patterns, "The regional-renewal story")*

**Unlock question:** "What would this farm look like today if you'd only ever sold the milk?"

**Outline beats:**
1. **The farm on the curve** — This farm's own place on the curve (Vermont's numbers only as backdrop).
2. **Before cheese** — the milk-only side of the before and after.
3. **What cheese made possible** — What cheese made possible that selling milk alone couldn't.
4. **What you can see** — A value the buyer can see; neighbors and the local economy.
5. **What it's becoming** — The forward stake.
6. **The ending** — For the Table.

**Best cases:**
- *Jasper Hill Farm.* The company states its mission as preserving Vermont's working landscape, and it built its cellars partly so smaller neighbors could reach a national market. The local fact (the year a third of Greensboro's dairies sold their cows) anchors it.
- *Von Trapp Farmstead.* The family started making cheese to secure the future of a dairy founded in 1959, and the whey feeds the farm's pigs. A small, concrete loop the buyer can picture.
- *Grafton Village Cheese.* Owned by a nonprofit foundation, so buying the cheese supports Vermont's rural communities. The value is structural, not a badge.

## Nobody Does It Alone
*A whole web of people made this cheese.*

- **Named relationships.** Partner, cellar, co-op, mentor, by name and role.
- **What each one contributes that the maker couldn't do alone.** This makes the collective structural rather than decorative: the cellar's ripening, the co-op's milk, the neighbor's beer that washes the rind.
- **An individual face inside the collective.** A collective story needs a person walking through it. In a wine experiment, an individual name signaled a real craftsperson; a collective label alone didn't.
- The shared cellar: a modern version of the Alpine village herd.
- The Upstart for the group: Cabot Clothbound, a 1919 co-op and a 1998 farm making one cheese together.
- The historical echo: Alpine fruitières, 1800s Vermont cheese co-ops.
- The brewery whose beer washes the rind.
- The Council and festivals.

**Key question:** Whose work is inside this cheese, and what does each one do that the maker couldn't?

**Unlock question:** "Who else has a hand in this cheese, and what do they do that you can't?"

**Outline beats:**
1. **The person** — An individual face inside the collective.
2. **The web** — Named relationships.
3. **What each one adds** — What each one contributes that the maker couldn't do alone; the shared cellar; the brewery.
4. **The old echo** *(optional)* — The historical echo.
5. **The proof** *(optional)* — The Upstart for the group.
6. **The ending** — For the Table.

**Best cases:**
- *Cabot Clothbound.* Made at the Cabot co-op, then wrapped and aged for months at the Cellars at Jasper Hill, where the batches are tasted and sorted. Every hand has a distinct job.
- *Oma and Savage (von Trapp).* Made on the farm and ripened by others: Oma at the Cellars at Jasper Hill, Savage at different times in Jasper Hill's Alpine vaults and in Murray's caves in New York. Same curd, different cave, and the cellar is a named partner.

## Second Life
*Something was lost, came back, and lives here now.*

- **What was lost, and when.** Dated and specific, and the loss is real.
- **What came back, and who carried it.** A named person and an act of recovery.
- **The bridge.** The recipe, name, or building that connects then to now. This is the element everything rests on. Buyers accept a stylized story about a real, continuing practice; they reject one where the chain is broken (invented abbey-beer names failed for exactly this reason).
- The Hockey Puck, or the hard year. (Shared.)
- Overlap with The Inheritance.
- Old documents and photos.
- The Name: often the thing that came back. (Shared.)

**Key question:** What was lost, who carried it back, and what still connects then to now?

**Unlock question:** "Is there something here, a barn, a recipe, a name, that you brought back?"

**Outline beats:**
1. **What was lost** — What was lost, and when.
2. **The hard year** *(optional)* — The Hockey Puck, or the hard year.
3. **Who carried it back** — What came back, and who carried it.
4. **The bridge** — The bridge; old documents and photos.
5. **The name** *(optional)* — The Name.
6. **The ending** — For the Table.

**Best cases:**
- *Grafton Village Cheese.* Founded in 1892 as a farmers' co-op to turn surplus milk into cheese; a fire destroyed the factory; a foundation rebuilt the company in the 1960s. Loss, carrier and bridge (the name, the village, the cheddar) are all on the record.
- *Shepsog (Grafton).* The name recalls the sheep that once covered Vermont's hills in the wool boom, before the forest came back. Sheep's milk returns as a cheese. The lost thing is a landscape.

## The Make
*Here is exactly what we decided in the vat and the cave, and what each decision does.*

*For cheesemongers, buyers and the most obsessive eaters. It is about one cheese. It reads like a spec sheet with a story inside it: the professional wants the parameters, and the story is in why each one was chosen. This is the arc where technical vocabulary is welcome and the maker's make sheet is the best material there is.*

- **The spec.** Milk (species; raw, thermized or pasteurized; which herd), rennet, cultures, format, affinage. It's the header a monger scans first, so it comes first. Jasper Hill opens every cheese page this way: milk type, style, milk source, format, affinage, typical profile.
- **The decisive choices, each tied to what it does.** Two or three choices in the vat or the cave that make this cheese what it is, each with its consequence for texture or flavor. "Cooked and pressed, for a tight, elastic texture" (Alpha Tolman). "Dry salted, like a cheddar" (Savage). A parameter alone is a spec; a parameter with its reason is a story. These come from the maker or a published source about this cheese. Never fill them in from the style's typical recipe.
- **The lineage, named precisely.** The classic it descends from, and exactly where it departs. "For fans of Appenzeller." "A take on Vacherin Mont d'Or or Försterkäse." "Beaufort's recipe, changed for a wheel a ninth the size." The departure is where the maker shows up.
- **The flavor map.** Taste and aroma words a monger can repeat, ideally grouped in families the way the Comté wheel groups 83 descriptors into six. Best of all, notes for this batch: the make date, the profile, what dominates and what sits underneath.
- This batch, this wheel: variation as data rather than a disclaimer. Two batches made a day apart can taste completely different, and saying so in advance is a mark of seriousness.
- Who aged it, and how: the cellar, the washes, the turning, and how long. When a cheese has been aged in more than one place, that's a story in itself.
- Care and service: how to cut it, how it changes once cut, the storage temperature. Winnimere's note that a cut wheel should be sold the same day is the kind of line only a professional needs and every professional appreciates.
- One mechanism, in a sentence: the chemistry behind one flavor or texture, told as wonder. Heat-loving cultures breaking down protein into brothy flavors and crunchy crystals; a cheese that keeps more calcium because it was pressed sweet, so it melts. General chemistry from the style histories is labeled as general, not as this cheese's.
- The Living Thing. (Shared.)
- What We Won't Do. (Shared.)
- The Upstart, with its category: which class it won, against what. (Shared.)

**Key question:** What did the maker decide in the vat and the cave, and what does each decision do in the mouth?

**Unlock question:** "What's one thing you do in the make that most makers of this style don't?"

**Numbers in this arc.** This is the one arc built on numbers: temperatures, times, cultures, acidity. They still never come from interview questions. Ask for the choice and why ("How do you salt it, and why that way?"), and invite the maker to add their make sheet or records as a file. Numbers come from those, or from published sources about this cheese, and are settled at the fact check like everything else.

**Outline beats:**
1. **The spec** — The spec.
2. **The lineage** — The lineage, named precisely.
3. **The decisive choices** — The decisive choices, each tied to what it does; one mechanism, in a sentence.
4. **The cave** *(optional)* — Who aged it, and how; The Living Thing.
5. **The flavor map** — The flavor map; this batch, this wheel.
6. **Care and service** *(optional)* — Care and service.
7. **The counter line** — the monger's version of For the Table: one true sentence to say while slicing.

**Best cases:**
- *Jasper Hill's cheese pages.* Every cheese opens with the same spec header, then story, sensory, and pairing and service. Alpha Tolman's page says it's now made with a traditional rennet built from cultures from previous makes and calf stomachs, and the distributor sheet adds that natural cultures do the acidifying. Technical lines, with a reason attached.
- *Cabot Clothbound Black Label.* After years of batch-by-batch tasting, the Cellars sort every batch into one of four flavor profiles. Retailers sell Black Label with the make date, the profile, the texture, the dominant and subtle flavors, and a spider graph. Variation becomes the selling point.
- *The Comté aroma wheel.* 83 descriptors in six families (lactic, fruity, roasted, vegetal, animal, spicy), handed to retailers as a laminated tasting tool. It gives every monger the same words.
- *Vermont worked example, Savage (von Trapp).* Its cheesemaker calls it a marriage of a cheddar and a traditional Alpine like Comté or Gruyère: hard-cooked and pressed like an Alpine, dry salted like a cheddar, aged most of a year. What a full Make story still needs from the maker: the cultures, the cook, whether the curd is milled before salting, and why they chose to salt it that way.

## The Reinvention
*An old cheese, rethought here, and you can taste the new idea.*

*For the adventurous, curious eater who wants to feel they're eating something exciting. It is about one cheese. It's less about the maker's life than about the cheese's idea, and the journey the eater goes on: what the old cheese was, what's new, and what that does to taste, texture and the experience of eating it. Technical, but at the level of a curious, educated eater: one mechanism in plain words, never a make sheet.*

*Close kin to Chosen Ancestors. In Chosen Ancestors the protagonist is the maker and their journey to a tradition. In The Reinvention the protagonist is the cheese, and the journey is the eater's.*

- **The old form, made vivid.** The classic as a baseline the eater can picture: what it is, where it comes from, what it was for. Bark-wrapped winter cheeses from the Jura; a Gruyère or a Comté; a farmhouse cheddar. The kit's history files supply the background, once the creamery has named the lineage.
- **The new move.** The one thing done differently here, said plainly: two traditions fused, a different milk or a blend of milks, a familiar treatment on an unfamiliar cheese, a new scale, a new place. It comes from the maker or a published source about this cheese. Never infer it.
- **What the move does in the mouth.** Taste, texture, how it eats: what you notice that you wouldn't in the classic. One mechanism in plain words is allowed and often helps ("salted like a cheddar, so it's sharper and more crumbly than you'd expect from an Alpine").
- The invitation: how to eat it so the journey happens. Taste it next to the classic. Peel back the bark and use a spoon. Buy it in its season.
- Why the old form fits here: a short version of Chosen Ancestors' "why it fits this place."
- What the cheese calls back to: a lost landscape, a forgotten practice (Shepsog's sheep).
- The Name. (Shared.)
- This Wheel, This Season. (Shared.)
- The Living Thing: the bark from the farm's own trees, the cave. (Shared.)
- The Upstart, especially when it wins in a category it doesn't seem to belong to. (Shared.)
- A pairing that extends the idea.

**Key question:** What is the old cheese, what's the new idea, and what does the new idea let you taste?

**Unlock question:** "What does your cheese do that the classic version of it doesn't?"

**Outline beats:**
1. **The old form** — The old form, made vivid.
2. **The new move** — The new move.
3. **Why here** *(optional)* — Why the old form fits here; what the cheese calls back to.
4. **In the mouth** — What the move does in the mouth.
5. **The invitation** *(optional)* — The invitation; a pairing; This Wheel, This Season.
6. **The ending** — For the Table.

**Best cases:**
- *Winnimere (Jasper Hill).* A take on the Jura's bark-wrapped winter cheeses, made only in winter from hay-fed raw milk, wrapped in spruce from the farm's own woods and washed in a cultured brine. Bacon, sweet cream, spruce, eaten with a spoon. The old form, the Vermont move and the experience are all there.
- *Harbison (Jasper Hill).* The spruce-bark wrap of those same Jura cheeses, put on a soft bloomy-rind cheese instead. Sweet cream, mushroom, woodsy; scooped when ripe. A familiar treatment on an unfamiliar cheese.
- *Savage (von Trapp).* Cheddar and Alpine married in one wheel: cooked and pressed like a Gruyère, dry salted like a cheddar. Tasters find both parents: cheddar's sharpness, Alpine sweetness and nuttiness, broth and caramelized onion. The obvious invitation is a side-by-side with a Comté and an aged cheddar.
- *Shepsog (Grafton).* Half sheep's milk, half cow's milk, named for the sheep that once covered Vermont's hills. It has won first place in a national class for cheddars made from mixed milks. A new milk, a lost landscape and a category surprise.
- *Pleasant Ridge Reserve (Uplands).* Beaufort's recipe, changed for a wheel a ninth the size and made only from pasture milk. A new scale and a new place.

## Braiding two arcs

When an idea braids two arcs, one **raises the question** and the other **answers it**. Name which is which. Useful braids:
- **Working Landscape raises, Chosen Ancestors answers:** how does a farm on hard land survive? By taking up a tradition built for exactly that land.
- **Chosen Ancestors raises, Taste of Place answers:** why make a European cheese in Vermont? Because this place does to the cheese what that place did.
- **The Calling raises, Why It Tastes Like This answers:** why would anyone work this hard? You taste the reason.
- **Second Life raises, The Inheritance answers:** what came back? Something the family never fully let go.
- **Nobody Does It Alone raises, Working Landscape answers:** why so many hands? Because keeping the land working takes all of them.
- **The Reinvention raises, The Make answers:** what's new about this cheese? Here, decision by decision, is how it was done. The professional's version.
- **Chosen Ancestors raises, The Reinvention answers:** the maker went looking for an old tradition; what did it become here?
- **The Reinvention raises, Taste of Place answers:** why rethink an old cheese here? Because this milk and this land ask for something different.

In a braided outline, the raising arc's beats come first and the answering arc's beats resolve them. Keep the total at 5–7 beats; drop optional beats before bold ones.

# Part 2. Coverage and strength

## Which bold elements "have it"

For every arc, mark each element **have it**, **thin**, or **missing**, and record which material it rests on.

- **Have it:** there is material for this element from the maker's own words (typed, spoken, or files and photos they added), or from the creamery's own website, or from other published sources about **this** creamery (press, interviews, retailer pages, award lists).
- **Thin:** the only support is background research (style histories, Vermont history, region notes, Claude's general knowledge), or an inference, or one weak mention. Background research alone makes an element **thin at most**, never "have it."
- **Missing:** nothing.

How to judge the edge cases:
- **History elements** (The tradition and its deep history; What the style originally solved; the historical echo; The old form, made vivid; The lineage, named precisely). These count as "have it" when a creamery source establishes the link, for example the maker or their website says the cheese is Alpine-style. Then the kit's history fills in the background. Without that link, the history is thin. Never claim the maker follows a historical method unless they say so.
- **Elements that "work as a number" or are "dated"** (Dated family history; How fast the milk gets to the vat; What was lost, and when; The Hands; The spec). The element counts as "have it" once the thing itself is known (the grandparents started milking here; the vat is right beside the barn; the cheese is raw cow's milk, cooked and pressed). Exact numbers and dates are settled at the fact check, never chased in Gathering.
- **A face** counts as "have it" when a photo of the maker exists (added by the maker or on their website). It never blocks story work.
- **Elements that need the maker's account** (The Hockey Puck; The motive in their own words; This farm's own place on the curve; The journey of choosing; What they changed and made their own; The mentor's own words; The decisive choices; The new move). Only maker words, or the maker quoted in a published source, count. Retailer and press descriptions of this cheese count for The decisive choices and The new move when they describe the make, but not when they only describe the taste. **Never invent these,** and never fill The decisive choices from the style's typical recipe. The Hockey Puck is never invented, and neither is anything else the arcs say must come from the maker.
- **Sensory elements** (One sensory line; Sensory language; The flavor map; What the move does in the mouth). Maker tasting notes or published notes about this cheese count. Notes typical of the style are thin.
- Material the maker removed is gone. Material they changed counts in its changed form.
- One piece of material can light up elements in several arcs. Count it everywhere it fits.

## Strength per arc

- **Strong:** several connecting pieces, at least one in the maker's words.
- **Some:** a couple of pieces, or good pieces that still need confirmation.
- **Thin:** one piece, or only background.

"Connecting" matters: three facts that explain one another make a stronger arc than five that sit side by side. A thin arc carries its **unlock question** (Part 1). An arc at "some" may carry the question for its most important missing bold element.

The Make and The Reinvention are judged per cheese. When a maker has several cheeses, show them for the cheese the maker is working on, or for the cheese with the most material.

# Part 3. How the arcs steer Gathering and readiness

## Steering the prompts

After each maker turn:
1. Find the **arcs the material is pointing toward**: the two or three with the most "have it" and thin elements, plus any arc the maker's own framing leans on (what they said they want people to understand).
2. List those arcs' **missing or thin bold elements**.
3. Turn the most important ones into prompts: the arc's unlock question when the arc is thin; otherwise a concrete question aimed at the one missing element, rewritten to fit what's known. Example: Chosen Ancestors has the tradition (website says Alpine-style) but not the choice → "What first drew you to making an Alpine-style cheese?" Example: The Make has the spec and the lineage but not the choices → "How do you salt Savage, and why that way?"
4. These come second in priority, after follow-ups on what the maker just said. (The full order is in the prompt pool.)

Never turn an element into a question that asks for a number, a date, a price, or a feeling. Ask for the thing; the number comes later. For The Make, invite the maker to add their make sheet or records as a file instead of asking for the numbers.

## Readiness

Ready means both:
- there is **material from the maker**, not just the web; and
- **at least two arcs have half or more of their bold elements** at "have it."

When ready, the app says so once, as a suggestion: "I think I have enough to start shaping a story. You can keep adding anytime. Nothing you've told me gets lost." The maker can keep gathering. Readiness is a suggestion, never a gate.

## Doors back to Gathering

Every missing bold element, every "needs you" beat, and every thin arc is a door back to Gathering, carrying its one question.

# Part 4. Three ideas and a pick (Story shape, move 2a)

## Before the ideas

Read everything gathered through the arcs. The governing question: **what facts does this maker have that no one else can claim, and which of them can a taster confirm in the mouth?**

Work out, for yourself and in short form when the task asks for it:
- **What I heard:** the strongest raw material, keeping the maker's vivid phrases word for word and saying where each came from ("you told us," "from your website").
- **Three through-lines:** possible unifying stories, each one a relationship among facts, not a single feature. "A Vermont version of an old mountain solution" is a through-line. "It's special because it's Alpine-style" names a category.
- **What's missing:** big pieces, not facts. A person, a turning point, how the history and the land connect, what the maker changed. Never "how many cows" or "what year."

## The ideas

- Offer **at least three** ideas, side by side.
- Each idea is built on **one arc, or two braided**. For a braid, say which arc raises the question and which answers it.
- Each idea has:
  - **A title:** short, plain, specific to this creamery. Not a slogan.
  - **A short telling:** 3–5 sentences. Story, not copy: something happens, a choice is made, the facts explain one another. Plain words, the maker's phrases where possible, no empty adjectives, no sales voice.
  - **Has / missing, in one line:** "Has the land, the tradition and the taste. Missing: what you changed."
- **Missing pieces appear in the telling in [brackets]**, never invented: "[what you changed from the Alpine way]". Background offered as possibility is phrased as possibility.
- **Mark the pick**, with one line on why. The usual reason is "it's the one only you can tell": the idea whose center another creamery couldn't claim.
- **At least one idea builds on what the maker said they want people to understand.** If the material points elsewhere, that idea sits alongside theirs, never instead of it.
- The ideas should differ in arc, not just in wording. Don't offer three versions of the same spine.
- When the material supports it and the maker hasn't ruled it out, **one of the three is about a single cheese** (The Make or The Reinvention), so the maker sees that a story can belong to a cheese as well as to the creamery.
- Best cases are models for the shape of an idea. Never borrow a best case's facts, names or phrases into an idea.
- Follow every piece of the maker's direction ("not about the family," "warmer").
- **Ideas the maker rejected ("Not us") never return**, in any wording or as part of a braid.
- **"Close, but…" ideas shape the next round:** keep what they liked, change what they named, and let them pull the next three toward it. Starred and "yes" ideas shape everything after.
- **Show me three more:** three new ideas that differ from every idea already shown, using arcs not yet tried where the material supports them.
- **Combine parts of these:** build one idea from the named parts, keeping its sources, and name which arc raises and which answers.

## Example idea (illustrative; brackets show what isn't known yet)

> **Built for hard ground** · Working Landscape raises, Chosen Ancestors answers · *Pick: it's the one only you can tell.*
> The land here is rocky, not made for a big herd, so selling milk by the tankful was never going to work. The family turned to the answer mountain farmers found long ago: make the milk into a cheese that keeps. [What first led the family to Alpine cheese] [What you changed from the Alpine way]. What you taste is that land, and an old mountain answer to it.
> *Has the land and the tradition. Missing: the choice, and what you made your own.*

> **Two cheeses in one wheel** · The Reinvention · *About Savage.*
> Cheddar was a lowland farmhouse cheese, salted by hand and sharp. Gruyère and Comté were mountain wheels, cooked and pressed to last a winter. Savage is made like the mountain cheese and salted like the cheddar, and you can taste both: the sharpness first, then sweetness and broth. [Why you decided to salt it like a cheddar] Try it next to a Comté and an aged cheddar and see which one it reminds you of first.
> *Has the old forms, the new move and the taste. Missing: why you made that choice.*

## Look closer

For any idea: each element of its arc(s) marked **have it / thin / missing** with where it came from; "what would make it stronger" (usually one or two elements, as plain sentences); "what's missing," each item carrying one question for Gathering.

# Part 5. The outline (Story shape, move 2b)

- The chosen idea becomes **5–7 beats in story order**, taken from the arc's outline beats (Part 1) plus any shared elements that fit. For a braid, raising-arc beats first.
- Each beat has: a short plain label, one or two sentences of story, the element(s) it draws on, its source pieces, and **one status**:
  - **from you:** rests on the maker's own words.
  - **from research:** rests on the creamery's website or other published sources about them, or on background history tied to a link the creamery confirmed.
  - **half from you:** part from the maker, part from research or still open.
  - **needs you:** no usable material. The beat text states in [brackets] what's needed and carries **one question** for the maker. Example: "[What you changed from how it's made in the Alps]" with "What's one thing you do differently from Alpine makers?"
  - **thin:** only background, an inference, or one weak mention. Offered as a possibility, with its question.
- **Never fill a needs-you beat.** No plausible guess, no "perhaps the family…" A bracket and a question.
- Keep bold-element beats; drop optional ones first if the outline runs long.
- The last beat is usually The ending (For the Table). For The Make, it's the counter line.
- Beats from non-chosen ideas can be pulled in when the maker asks.
- "Read it as a paragraph": join the beats into plain prose, brackets kept.
- When the maker answers a needs-you question in Gathering, the beat updates and its status changes.

# Part 6. Doesn't fit any shape (yet)

Some material lights up no element in any arc. Keep it. The leftovers are often where a maker's real distinctiveness sits. Walter Räss's line, "in Switzerland we follow recipes; Vermont has cheesemakers," wouldn't have come out of a template.

Worth keeping when it is:
- in the maker's own words, and vivid;
- a belief, a habit, a joke, or a quirk only this maker has;
- surprising, or the opposite of what you'd expect from the style;
- a detail that keeps coming back in what they say.

Not worth keeping: logistics (hours, addresses, prices, ordering), generic claims ("we care about quality"), and anything removed.

What to do with a leftover:
- Show it in the side column word for word, with its source.
- Recheck it each round. It may now fit an element, or it may be the center an idea should be built around. An idea can be built on a leftover when it's the most distinctive thing the maker has.
- Never force it into an arc, and never drop it to make an arc tidier.
