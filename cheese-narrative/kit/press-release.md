# Press Release

**Purpose:** A one-page announcement of real news (a new cheese, an award, a seasonal first release, a milestone, an event) that editors can use with little follow-up, with the creamery's story carried inside the news.

## When to use it, and when to pitch instead

A release is the broadcast version: one text, many recipients. A pitch is personal: under 200 words, to one writer, about why this story fits their beat. The kit's PR research and pitches guide handles pitches, and writers mostly prefer them. In Muck Rack's surveys, 94% of journalists preferred one-to-one pitches (2021), and the top reasons for deleting one were irrelevance to their beat (86%), being overly promotional (71%), and looking like a mass email (50%) (2025). Releases still matter: in Cision's 2025 survey, 72% of journalists called them the most useful thing PR people provide.

Use a release when there is news with a date (first wheels of the season, a new cheese, an award, a 25th year, an open farm day) that must reach many places at once (local papers, event calendars, the Vermont Cheese Council, retailer newsletters), when a writer asks for the full facts, or for the website's news page.

Pitch instead when there's a story but no news. "Ana has made cheese only in summer since 2019" is a pitch. "The first 2026 wheels go on sale October 15" is a release, with the summer-only story inside it. Best practice is both: write the release, then send short personal pitches to a few writers with it attached.

## Who reads it and how

An editor gives the headline and first paragraph a few seconds. If there's something there, they look for facts, a usable quote, and someone to call. Food writers, per the kit's press file, want specific people, real tension, checkable facts, and access.

## What it needs from what the maker has gathered

The news and its date; the approved story and essential sentence; confirmed facts (herd, milk, make, aging, place); tasting language in the maker's words; a real quote; any award with full name, year, and category; where and when to buy or attend; available photos; confirmed access; a contact.

## Structure

1. **Release line.** `FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE`, or `EMBARGOED UNTIL [date, time, time zone]` only if writers agreed in advance to wait.
2. **Headline** (under about 100 characters, title case). Creamery plus news, plain verb, no selling adjectives.
3. **Subheadline** (optional, one sentence). Room for the story hook.
4. **Dateline and lead** (35 to 60 words). AP form: `[TOWN], Vt., Oct. 15, 2026 –`. Who, what, when, where, plus the one fact that makes it interesting rather than routine.
5. **Story paragraph** (60 to 120 words). One or two confirmed story elements: a constraint that costs something, the hockey puck and what came next, the hands with a number. Tension is what separates a story from an announcement.
6. **The maker's quote** (one or two sentences), in their own words, never invented or polished into something they didn't say. If missing: `[ask: in a sentence or two, in your words, why does this matter to you?]`. A second quote (a monger, a chef) only if that person said it and agreed.
7. **Useful facts** (3 to 6 bullets). Milk, style, rind, aging, format; taste in the maker's words; price if public; where and when. Any award named precisely, paired with a sensory line.
8. **Images and access.** Link to photos (ideally from the photo essay) with credit. Access only if confirmed: `[ask: what can you offer a writer: a visit, a call, samples?]`.
9. **Boilerplate** (under 100 words, "About [Creamery]"). Same on every release: who, where, since when, what they make, the one defining specific. Ends with the website.
10. **Media contact.** `[maker's name, role, phone, email]`.
11. **End mark.** `###`, centered.

Total: 300 to 500 words, one page.

## Rules

- **Real news only.** Nothing with a date means no release; offer a pitch.
- **Every fact is public-facing:** maker-confirmed or sourced, gaps where missing. Writers fact-check.
- **Quotes come from the maker.** No invented or composite quotes. A rough real quote beats a smooth fake one.
- **A story, not accolades.** One award, named and placed in context, is fine. "Award-winning" with no award named is not.
- Weak words need something specific behind them; banned words never appear. Prefer a plain verb to "is pleased to announce."
- Paragraphs of two or three sentences. Honor every maker preference.

## Weak and strong

Weak:
> **Award-Winning Birch Hollow Creamery Is Thrilled to Announce Its Exceptional New Season of Artisan Cheese**
> Nestled in the rolling hills of Vermont, Birch Hollow Creamery is pleased to announce its handcrafted, world-class Hollow Tomme, made with passion. "We are so excited to share this unique cheese," said owner Ana Ruiz.

The news is buried, nothing is dated or checkable, no award is named, the banned words pile up, and the quote is invented and empty.

Strong (fictional Birch Hollow Creamery):
> FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE
>
> **Birch Hollow Creamery Releases First Wheels of Its 2026 Summer Tomme**
>
> [TOWN], Vt., Oct. 15, 2026 – The first wheels of Hollow Tomme made from this summer's pasture milk go on sale `[ask: what date, and where?]`. The cheese is made only from May to October, while the farm's 22 Jerseys are on grass.
>
> Ana Ruiz switched to seasonal-only cheesemaking in 2019 and stopped making cheese in winter. That first year she lost two wholesale accounts that wanted year-round supply. Last year both came back. Each 4-lb wheel is turned by hand every week for at least five months.
>
> `[ask: in a sentence or two, in your words, why does this matter to you?]` said Ruiz.
>
> - Raw cow's milk, natural rind, aged 5 to 8 months, 4-lb wheels
> - Taste, in the maker's words: brown butter, cut grass, a little mushroom near the rind
> - Price and stockists: `[ask]`
>
> Photos: `[link, credit]`. Visits and interviews: `[ask: what can you offer a writer?]`
>
> **About Birch Hollow Creamery:** Birch Hollow Creamery in `[town]`, Vermont, makes Hollow Tomme from the milk of 22 Jerseys, only while they're on pasture. `[ask: founding year, website]`
>
> Media contact: `[maker's name, role, phone, email]`
>
> \###

## Self-check

- Is there dated news, in the headline and first sentence?
- Does the story paragraph carry a confirmed element with tension, not praise?
- Is the quote the maker's own words, or a visible gap?
- Does every fact trace to the maker or a source, with any award named in full?
- 300 to 500 words, with boilerplate, contact, and `###`?
