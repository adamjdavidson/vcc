# For the Table

**Purpose:** One line about the cheese, true and easy to remember, that a host can say while passing the board, a gift-giver can say while handing it over, or a maker can say while holding out a sample.

## Who reads it and how

Nobody reads this piece. People say it, and most of the people saying it have never met the maker.

Cheese is mostly bought to share, which makes the buyer the real teller. The story moves along a chain: maker → monger or shelf talker → buyer → guest at the table. At the far end is a host who bought a wedge three days ago, with about five seconds and a glass in hand, talking to a guest who didn't ask for a lecture. The line will get shortened and lose a detail, and it still has to carry something.

It's also said in three other places:
- **The gift.** The line goes on a tag or gets said as the cheese changes hands. The "made with love" effect is strongest here: handmade goods are valued mainly because they seem to contain care, and the effect is biggest for gifts to people close to the buyer.
- **The tasting table.** A farmers' market, a farm store, a demo at a shop. The maker or a staff member has about ten seconds before the customer tastes, asks a question, or walks on. Shoppers won't buy premium cheese without tasting, so the sample does the persuading; the words give the taster something to notice.
- **The restaurant.** A server answering "what is this one?" The same line works.

## What it needs from what the maker has gathered

- The essential story (stage 4), so the line points the same way as everything else.
- One concrete, checkable fact taken from the shared elements: **What We Won't Do**, **The Hands**, **This Wheel, This Season**, **The Name**, or **The Living Thing**. It should be a fact a guest could repeat to someone else.
- The maker's first name, or the name of the farm or place. That is the closest a sentence gets to **A face**.
- One taste note the maker has confirmed, for the tasting version.
- One way to serve it or pair it, for the tasting version.
- For the "if they lean in" layer: a **Hockey Puck** or another hard year, told in the maker's own words.

## Structure

The app offers the core line plus two or three variants. It marks its pick and the maker chooses.

1. **The line** (one sentence, under 20 words; under 15 is better). It holds one concrete fact that shows an act of care, the kind of thing someone did *for* the cheese, the animals, or the people who will eat it. Taste words are optional. A person or a place is strongly preferred.
2. **The host's line** (one or two sentences, under 30 words). This is the line as a guest would actually say it, in the third person and casual: "The woman who makes this…" It's written for a buyer who will pass it on.
3. **The gift line** (one or two sentences, under 30 words). It can go on a tag, a website gift note, or the back of a card. It puts care first and is addressed to the person who receives the cheese.
4. **The tasting line** (three to five short spoken lines, under 60 words). The maker says it at a table. In order:
   - Offer the sample.
   - Name the cheese in plain words ("our raw-milk tomme," not "an artisan farmstead specialty").
   - Give the one fact from the line.
   - Tell the taster what to notice.
   - Suggest one use or pairing.
   The invitation comes first, so the cheese is in their mouth before any explanation.
5. **If they lean in** (optional, one or two sentences). The next layer for the person who asks a follow-up question: the hard year, the price reason, or where the name came from.

## Rules

- **It must be true without qualifiers.** Someone saying it at a party can't carry a footnote. If the fact isn't confirmed, the line shows the gap, for example `[ask: how many cows are in the herd right now?]`, and isn't finished until the maker fills it.
- **Retelling test.** Read the line, look away, and say it from memory with one detail wrong. If it still carries care and something specific, it works. If it falls apart into "it's a local artisan cheese," it doesn't. Round, memorable facts survive retelling better than exact ones ("only in summer," "22 cows").
- **Speakable.** Read it out loud. If it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it. None of the weak words (artisan, handcrafted, small-batch, crafted with care, labor of love) and none of the banned ones.
- **Show care. Don't claim skill.** "She turns every wheel by hand every week" carries love. "Expertly crafted" carries nothing.
- **Don't promise a taste the cheese won't deliver.** The strongest pleasure comes when the cheese matches the description or slightly beats it. "Notice the mushroom near the rind" works. "The best cheese you'll ever eat" sets the cheese up to fail.
- **What must come from the maker:** every fact, every taste note, and every quote. Only the pairing in the tasting line may come from general knowledge of the style, and it is labeled a suggestion until the maker confirms it.

## Weak and strong

Weak (fictional Birch Hollow Creamery):
> This is a handcrafted, small-batch raw-milk tomme from Vermont, made with passion. Rich, nutty, complex.

It fails: no person, no fact a guest could repeat, "nutty" isn't in the maker's notes, and every claim is asserted. Retold, it becomes "some Vermont cheese."

Strong:
> **The line:** Ana only makes this while her cows are out on grass. When winter comes, she stops.
>
> **The host's line:** "The woman who makes this has 22 cows and only makes cheese while they're out on pasture, May to October. So this is basically last summer."
>
> **The gift line:** Made only in summer, from 22 Jerseys on grass, and turned by hand every week for at least five months before it came to you.
>
> **The tasting line:** "Try this one. It's our Hollow Tomme, a raw-milk cheese we only make May to October, while the Jerseys are on pasture. Taste it near the rind. That's where the mushroom is, with brown butter underneath. It's great with a dry cider." *(pairing: maker's suggestion)*
>
> **If they lean in:** The first year she went seasonal, she lost two wholesale accounts that wanted cheese all year. Last year both came back.

## Self-check

- Could a guest repeat the line after hearing it once, without having met the maker?
- Does it hold one concrete fact that shows care, and is that fact confirmed by the maker?
- Does it work out loud, with no brochure words?
- Is every taste promise something the cheese reliably delivers?
- Is any gap visible as `[ask: …]`, not papered over?
