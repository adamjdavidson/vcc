# Who Buys Artisanal Cheese — Research Notes

Reference notes for shaping cheesemaker stories (Vermont Cheese Council work). Each finding has its source and why it matters for the story.

**Overall caveat:** The public research on U.S. artisanal cheese buyers is thin and mostly old. The best Northeast studies use data from 2010–2012, plus consultant reports from 2019–2020. The best current data (SFA, IDDBA, Mintel, Dairy Farmers of Wisconsin) is mostly paywalled. Treat these as directional, not settled.

---

## Who they are

### Two kinds of buyers: quality seekers and price-sensitive buyers
A UVM conjoint survey covered Vermont, Manhattan and Boston (data collected 2012, published 2015). Buyers split into two groups:

- **Quality seekers** would pay 15–25% more for each quality attribute: artisan, farmstead, local, organic, renewable energy. A cheese with all five could sell for roughly twice the price of one with none.
- **Price-sensitive buyers** cared mostly about price. The one other attribute that moved them was "local."

Quality seekers averaged about 46 years old. About 74% were women, versus 56% of the price-sensitive group. They bought about 2.2 lb of artisan or farmstead cheese a month, versus about 1 lb. They shopped much more at co-ops, cheese shops, farmers' markets and restaurants.

The surprise: **income and education did not predict which group someone was in.**

- *Source:* Wang, Thompson & Parsons, "Preferences for Farmstead, Artisan, and Other Cheese Attributes," *International Food and Agribusiness Management Review* 18(2), 2015. https://www.ifama.org/resources/documents/v18i2/wang-thompson-parsons.pdf
- *Why it matters:* The audience is defined by values and attention, not wealth. A story aimed at "rich people" misses the target. The real audience is people who care how food is made and will spend time at a counter. Price-sensitive buyers can still be reached, but only through "local."

### Core national specialty-food shopper: 25–44, $75K+, coastal
The Specialty Food Association's long-running research with Mintel describes the core specialty-food consumer as 25–44, household income $75K+, living on the East or West Coast. Millennials have been the core group, and Gen Z is catching up.

- *Sources:* Food Business News on SFA/Mintel research: https://www.foodbusinessnews.net/articles/7097-specialty-food-industry-continues-surge-upward ; Gifts & Dec on SFA research: https://www.giftsanddec.com/business-news/consumers-spending-more-specialty-foods ; PLMA on SFA's *Today's Specialty Food Consumer*: https://plma.com/article/consumers-buying-record-amounts-specialty-food
- *Why it matters:* This audience is younger than the Vermont survey suggests. SFA also found that Gen Z and Millennials care about a company's values and philosophy, while older shoppers focus more on local and regional origin. The same maker may need to tell the story with different emphasis for different buyers.

### Variety seekers, not brand loyalists
An academic study of artisanal cheese buyers (UK, 1998) found they focus on the cheese's unique character compared with industrial cheese. Price matters less to them. They are "variety seeking," with little loyalty to a brand or even a cheese type.

- *Source:* Kupiec & Revell, "Speciality and artisanal cheeses today: the product and the consumer," *British Food Journal*, 1998. https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/speciality-and-artisanal-cheeses-today-the-product-and-the-consum/
- *Why it matters:* Loyalty to the cheese is weak, so the story has to create loyalty to the *maker*. A buyer who wants to try something new next week can still come back to a person they feel connected to.

## What turns them on

### The individual maker's story, more than the place
In 2020, the consultancy KK&P produced a report for the Vermont legislature. Cheese retailers told them each cheese's individual story, quality and innovation appealed to buyers much more than Vermont origin. A KK&P consultant told lawmakers that a Manhattan shopper at a high-end cheese shop isn't looking for Vermont cheese; they're looking for the story of Jasper Hill.

- *Sources:* KK&P, *Vermont Dairy Marketing Assessment*, Feb 2020: https://agriculture.vermont.gov/sites/agriculture/files/doc_library/KKP_VT%20Dairy%20Marketing%20Assessment0224.pdf ; VTDigger coverage: https://vtdigger.org/2020/03/02/could-speciality-cheeses-save-dairy-farms/
- *Why it matters:* This is the strongest evidence for the core framing: make the maker the hero, with Vermont as the setting.

### In big-city markets, values and distinct flavor beat "made in Vermont"
UVM's Taste of Place survey covered the Philadelphia, Boston and New York metro areas, the three biggest sources of Vermont visitors. The highest premiums went to:

1. environmentally friendly methods
2. fair wages for farmers and workers
3. unique flavors that reflect the region

"Made in Vermont" on its own got the second-lowest premium in the metro areas. In Vermont itself, "family farm" and fair wages ranked highest, followed by "made in Vermont."

- *Source:* UVM Taste of Place research poster (Kolodinsky, Trubek et al.): https://www.uvm.edu/sites/default/files/media/TOP_poster.pdf
- *Why it matters:* For the city buyer, the label "Vermont" means little until the story fills it in: how they treat the land, how they treat the people who work there, what this place tastes like. For buyers inside Vermont, family farm carries more weight.

### "Artisan" outsells "farmstead," probably because people don't know what farmstead means
In the UVM study, quality seekers would pay about 19.7% more for "artisan" but only about 16.1% more for "farmstead." The authors think buyers don't understand farmstead: made on the farm from the farm's own animals, which keeps more value with the farmer.

- *Source:* Wang, Thompson & Parsons 2015 (above).
- *Why it matters:* Jargon labels don't land. "Farmstead" works when the story shows it: these are our cows, this is our barn, we make the cheese a few hundred feet from where we milk. The label alone doesn't.

### Shoppers actively look for information
A Dairy Farmers of Wisconsin study of heavy specialty-cheese buyers found that 92% actively look for information at the specialty cheese case, and 77% will go out of their way to find a cheese they want. Six in ten said the cheese case strongly shapes their view of the whole store.

- *Source:* Dairy Processing on DFW heavy-buyer research: https://dairyprocessing.com/articles/2696-study-looks-at-frequent-specialty-cheese-consumers ; Cheese Market News: https://cheesemarketnews.com/guestcolumn/2024/07jun24_01.html
- *Why it matters:* These shoppers want to be told something. The story has to fit the formats they actually read: a case card, a monger's thirty seconds, a label.

## What turns them off

### Intimidation
IDDBA's education lead said many people feel about cheese the way they feel about wine: they don't know what they like, so they stick with what they know. A brand campaign (Cello, "Cheese Confidently") was built entirely around specialty-cheese intimidation.

- *Sources:* DairyReporter, "Cheese is intimidating": https://dairyreporter.com/Article/2015/11/25/Cheese-is-intimidating-Sell-the-story-experience-of-a-product ; Partners + Napier on Cello: https://www.partnersandnapier.com/news/cello-cuts-through-cheese-aisle-intimidation-with-cheese-confidently
- *Why it matters:* A story full of expert language can make people feel shut out. The story should welcome a non-expert and give them something they can repeat.

### Buying blind
Focus groups for a USDA SARE project found price isn't a major factor, but shoppers won't buy premium-priced cheese without tasting it first. They also rely on store staff for information. Packaging had little influence on what they bought. In Atlantic Corporation's Northeast sheep and goat dairy survey, a third of people who sampled a sheep or goat cheese went on to buy it.

- *Sources:* SARE project FW00-210: https://projects.sare.org/sare_project/fw00-210/ ; Atlantic Corporation / NE-DBIC, Sheep and Goat Dairy highlights report (2023): https://nedairyinnovation.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Highlights-Report-Sheep-and-Goat-Dairy-Market-Conditions-and-Consumer-Research.pdf
- *Why it matters:* The story mostly works in two places, the tasting and the monger's mouth. Write it to be spoken at a counter while someone is tasting.

## Market context

- Circana reports U.S. deli specialty cheese up 8% in both dollars and volume in 2025. Among artisanal styles, goat cheese is favored, while brie, blue and havarti slowed. https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/07/22/us-cheese-trends-2025-specialty-varieties-surge-as-american-cheese-slumps/
- Charcuterie boards spread on social media, especially among younger people. The draw is community and gathering more than formal entertaining. https://www.entrepreneur.com/buying-a-franchise/gen-z-turned-this-food-craze-into-a-tiktok-obsession-franchisors-are-cashing-in
- *Why it matters:* Cheese is increasingly bought to share. That means the buyer will often retell the story to other people (see the story elements file).
