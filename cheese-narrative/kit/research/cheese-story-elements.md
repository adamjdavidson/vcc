# Elements of a Cheesemaker Story That Work — Research Notes

Reference notes for shaping cheesemaker stories (Vermont Cheese Council work). Each element has its research basis, source, and why it matters for the story.

**Caveat:** Only part of this is cheese-specific (UVM, KK&P, Paxson). The rest comes from broader consumer psychology (handmade, labor, underdog, authenticity). Those findings fit the cheese findings well, but they weren't tested on cheese buyers.

**The big idea:** The story's main job may not be persuading the shopper at the counter. Cheese is mostly bought to share, so the shopper takes the story home and retells it. The best story gives them something short and vivid to retell: a face, an act of care, one image of the work, a hardship.

---

## 1. A specific person you can see

**Research:** In UVM taste studies, people told personal stories about the cheesemakers liked the cheese more than people told only about the cheese. This held regardless of how much of a foodie they were. Separately, a German packaging experiment found that a *picture* of the producer raised product attitude, perceived quality and authenticity. Adding *text* about the producer had the opposite effect.

**Sources:**
- Amy Trubek on the UVM research, Eat This Podcast: https://eatthispodcast.com/vermont-and-the-taste-of-place/
- Related paper: Lahne et al., "Consumer sensory perception of cheese depends on context: A study using comment analysis and linear mixed models"
- Gawlik & Hüttl-Maack, "How providing personal information about the producer on food packaging affects consumer responses," EMAC 2020: https://proceedings.emac-online.org/pdfs/A2020-64361.pdf

**Why it matters:** The story changes how the cheese actually *tastes* to people, not just whether they buy it. And a face does more than a paragraph, so a story kit should push makers toward a real photo and a short story rather than a long "about us."

---

## 2. Love, more than effort or skill

**Research:** Handmade products are more attractive mainly because people sense they symbolically "contain love." The researchers ruled out effort, quality, uniqueness, authenticity and pride as the main explanation. The effect is strongest when buying for people the buyer loves, and it disappears for more distant recipients.

**Source:** Fuchs, Schreier & van Osselaer, "The Handmade Effect: What's Love Got to Do with It?" *Journal of Marketing* 79(2), 2015: https://mib.univie.ac.at/en/research/recent-publications/publikationen-detailansicht/pure/b4c66f26-9ce8-4460-a816-7f46a6c4cd0c/show/publ/Pure

**Why it matters:** Cheese is often bought for hosting or for people the buyer cares about, which is exactly where this effect is strongest. The story should show care: for the animals, the cheese, the people who will eat it. Talking about skill is less important than showing care.

---

## 3. The work, made visible

**Research:** People value a result more when they can see the work done on their behalf. Seeing the effort makes them feel they owe something back (reciprocity). In restaurant field experiments, letting customers see the cooks working raised customer-reported quality by 22%.

**Sources:**
- Buell & Norton, "The Labor Illusion: How Operational Transparency Increases Perceived Value," *Management Science*, 2011: https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v57y2011i9p1564-1579.html
- Buell, Kim & Tsay, "Creating Reciprocal Value Through Operational Transparency," *Management Science*, 2017: https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-ddad-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b

**Why it matters:** Specific images of labor carry value: 4 a.m. milking, turning wheels, brushing rinds, a hard winter. One concrete image of the work does more than words like "small-batch" or "handcrafted."

---

## 4. Disadvantage plus determination (the underdog)

**Research:** Underdog brand stories that work have two parts: (1) a disadvantage, such as humble beginnings or being outgunned by bigger competitors, and (2) passion and determination to win anyway. Consumers respond best when both are present. People also identify with underdog brands, partly because they tend to see themselves as underdogs.

**Sources:**
- Paharia, Keinan, Avery & Schor, "The Underdog Effect: The Marketing of Disadvantage and Determination through Brand Biography," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 2011
- Keinan, Avery & Paharia, "Capitalizing on the Underdog Effect," *Harvard Business Review*, Nov 2010 (as summarized in Twin Cities Business: https://tcbmag.com/have-no-fear-the-underdogs-are-here/)

**Why it matters:** Vermont makers have a real version of this. KK&P found Vermont artisan cheese costs about twice as much per pound to make as comparable European cheese, and Vermont lost about a third of its dairy farms from 2007 to 2017. Hardship alone reads as complaint. It has to come with the determination to keep going. (KK&P report: https://agriculture.vermont.gov/sites/agriculture/files/doc_library/KKP_VT%20Dairy%20Marketing%20Assessment0224.pdf)

## 5. Lineage and an original method

**Research:** People believe a product carries its creator's "essence," and that this essence passes through the original *process* the creator developed. In one study, people judged chocolate made with the founder's original recipe in a new factory as more authentic than chocolate from the original factory using a different recipe. Related work finds people pay more for products from a brand's original factory. These effects are much stronger for pleasure products than practical ones.

**Sources:**
- Galoni, Strejcek & Grayson, "The Role of Original Process in Creating Product Essence and Authenticity," *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 2022 (summary: https://tippie.uiowa.edu/node/4991)
- Newman & Dhar, "Authenticity Is Contagious," *Journal of Marketing Research*, 2014 (summary: https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/authenticity-is-contagious ; pleasure vs. practical point: https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/news/cracking-the-authenticity-code/39959)

**Why it matters:** Where a method came from is part of the story: a grandmother's recipe, a technique learned from a Swiss mentor, a family farm in its fifth generation. Cheese is a pleasure product, so this effect applies with full force.

---

## 6. Not seeming to be about the money

**Research:** Beverland's study of luxury wine identified six elements of authenticity: heritage and pedigree, consistent style, commitment to quality, relationship to place, method of production, and downplaying commercial motives. A later study of a mass-market brand found three of these mattered most: consistent style, relationship to place, and downplaying commercial motives.

**Sources:**
- Beverland, "The 'Real Thing': Branding Authenticity in the Luxury Wine Trade," *Journal of Business Research* 59, 2006 (six dimensions as summarized in: https://rujapre.ujaen.es/bitstreams/567d749d-4f2e-436a-b2a3-94b908dc566c/download)
- Alexander, "Brand authentication: creating and maintaining brand auras," *European Journal of Marketing* 43, 2009: https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/03090560910935578/full/html

**Why it matters:** The motive in the story should be the craft, the land, the animals, the community. That fits the KK&P finding that Jasper Hill's stated mission centers on the economy within 15 miles of the farm. The money is real, but the story shouldn't be about it.

---

## 7. A living thing with a cast of characters

**Research:** Anthropologist Heather Paxson describes cheesemakers working with a wild cast: plants, animals, microbes, family, employees, customers. She calls artisan cheese an "unfinished" commodity, a living product whose qualities are never fully settled. Artisan makers adjust to seasonal changes in the milk instead of fighting them. They also use terroir, the idea that a place has its own taste, to set their cheese apart from commodity cheese.

**Sources:**
- Paxson, *The Life of Cheese: Crafting Food and Value in America*, University of California Press, 2013: https://muse.jhu.edu/book/25841
- Paxson, "Locating Value in Artisan Cheese: Reverse Engineering Terroir," *Cultural Anthropology*, 2010: https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/1721.1/61753/1/Paxson_Locating%20value.pdf

**Why it matters:** This is the material a commodity can't claim: a named cow, the June milk versus the January milk, the microbes in this particular cave. Variation becomes a selling point rather than a flaw.

## Supporting findings from the buyer research

These come from the companion file, cheese-buyer-research.md.

- **The maker over the place.** Retailers say individual stories sell Vermont cheese, not Vermont origin (KK&P 2020).
- **Values that make "Vermont" mean something.** In big-city markets, environmentally friendly methods, fair wages and distinct regional flavor command premiums. "Made in Vermont" alone doesn't (UVM Taste of Place).
- **Show "farmstead" instead of labeling it.** Buyers undervalue the label because they don't understand it (Wang, Thompson & Parsons 2015).
- **Welcome non-experts.** Intimidation is a real barrier, so the story should invite people in rather than test them (IDDBA, Cello campaign).
- **Write it to be spoken while someone tastes.** Shoppers won't buy premium cheese without tasting, and they rely on staff for information (SARE).
