# Alpine cheese: the deep history

*Background for stories about Alpine-style, mountain, tomme, and washed-rind cheeses. Tradition and context, not claims about any one maker.*

## The headline

Mountain cheese is one of the oldest continuous food traditions we can trace. People were making cheese in the high Alpine pastures about 3,000 years ago, in stone huts much like the ones Alpine dairy managers still use every summer. And cheese itself is part of how people managed to live in cold, high, hostile places at all.

## What the evidence shows

- **Cheese in the high Alps, about 3,000 years ago.** Archaeologists analyzed pottery from six sites in the Swiss Alps, some above the tree line. Iron Age sherds (first millennium BC) carried the chemical signature of heated milk from cows, sheep, or goats: the step that turns milk into cheese. The pottery came from the ruins of stone buildings similar to those modern Alpine dairy managers use in the summer months. (University of York / Newcastle University, PLOS ONE, 2016.)
- **Why herders went up.** The researchers link high-altitude dairying to growing populations and more farming in the lowlands: pressure on valley pastures pushed herders to higher elevations. Making cheese up there took knowledge of the high pastures and the ability to cope with unpredictable mountain weather. "Even today, producing cheese in a high mountainous environment requires extraordinary effort."
- **Cheese is far older still.** Residues in pottery from the Dalmatian coast show fermented dairy, soft cheeses and yogurts, about 7,200 years ago; sieve-like pots from Poland point to cheesemaking about 7,500 years ago.
- **A technology for living in hard places.** Researchers argue that storable cheese gave early farmers "a food source that could buffer the risk of farming in colder northern climates," helping them expand their territories. Fermenting milk into cheese also lowers its lactose, which made dairy usable for adults who couldn't digest fresh milk. (Penn State, 2018.)

## How it can work in a story

- **Deep time in the hand.** A wheel of mountain cheese is the latest link in a chain going back roughly three thousand years in the Alps: herders carrying summer milk up and carrying cheese down.
- **Survival technology.** These cheeses were how people lived where crops were unreliable and winters were long: summer grass, turned into food that keeps.
- **Carried on, and remade.** A Vermont maker working in this tradition joins something ancient and also changes it, with different land, animals, weather, and choices.

## Careful

- The makers were herders and farmers, not hunter-gatherers: cheese needs domesticated animals.
- Don't say a specific modern cheese (or this maker's cheese) is 3,000 years old. The tradition is; the recipe isn't.
- Say "about 3,000 years" for the Alps and "more than 7,000 years" for cheese in general.
