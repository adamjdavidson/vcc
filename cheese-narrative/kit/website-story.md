# The Longer Website Story

**Purpose:** The creamery's "about" page, told as the approved story in 400 to 800 words, plus a short version for each cheese's own page.

## Who reads it and how

Someone who already cares enough to look: a shopper who tasted the cheese and searched the name, a monger or buyer deciding whether to carry it, a food writer checking before a call. They give it a minute or two and decide in the first two sentences whether to keep reading. They want the person, the place, and why the cheese tastes the way it does, plus something to repeat, since most cheese is bought to share.

## What it needs from what the maker has gathered

The approved outline, essential story, and arc; the maker's own phrases, quoted exactly; the elements the outline uses (The Hands, What We Won't Do, The Hockey Puck, and so on); the cheese list with milk, rind, age, and maker-confirmed taste; available photos; and confirmed facts about visiting, where to buy, and any newsletter. For several cheeses: what they share, which is the flagship, and any that sit outside the main story.

## Structure: the about page

1. **The opening** (40 to 90 words). Start on a person, a moment, or a problem: a decision, a thing that went wrong, a question the maker couldn't drop. Not a founding date, a mission statement, or the landscape.
2. **The turn** (80 to 150 words). What the maker decided or found, and why. The arc shows itself here.
3. **The work** (100 to 200 words). One or two concrete images of the work, with a time or number attached, and the choice that costs something. Tie at least one choice to a taste the reader could confirm: this, so it tastes like that.
4. **The proof** (60 to 150 words). What happened because of the choice: a failure and what came next, an account lost and won back. Awards go here if wanted, placed against something and paired with a taste line.
5. **The cheeses** (60 to 150 words). Each cheese as one answer to the question the story raised, a line or two each, linked to its page. Cheeses that don't fit the story are left to their pages.
6. **The invitation** (20 to 60 words, only if confirmed). Visit days, where to buy, a newsletter. Specific: "Saturdays, June through September," not "come visit anytime." If nothing is confirmed, leave `[ask: can people visit, and when?]` and `[ask: where can people buy it?]`, or end on the story.

Total: 400 to 800 words.

**Photo placements.** Mark two to four in the text as `[Photo: …]`. One near the top shows the maker doing the work; the others show the specific thing the paragraph names (the herd at the gate, the rind up close). No stock landscapes, no posed barn portraits. A face does more than a paragraph about the maker, so let the photos carry "who we are."

## Structure: each cheese's page

1. **Opening line** (under 25 words). What the cheese is and the one thing that makes it this cheese.
2. **Its piece of the story** (60 to 120 words). Why it exists or why it's called what it is, with one choice tied to a taste.
3. **What it is** (one line). Milk, style, rind, age, format.
4. **Taste** (one or two sentences). Maker-confirmed, one broad term next to one specific one.
5. **At the table** (one or two lines). Serving advice and pairings marked as suggestions, and a line a guest could say while passing the board.
6. **What changes** (optional). Seasonal or batch variation, with months named.

Total: 120 to 220 words.

## Rules

- Tell the approved story. A list of dates and milestones is a history, not a story; keep only the dates the story needs.
- Keep the maker's own phrases, quoted where they're the best words available. It should sound like them on a good day, not a brochure.
- Public-facing: every fact is maker-confirmed or sourced. Gaps show as `[ask: …]`, never an invented failure, memory, or quote.
- A style's history is background, never proof that this maker follows the old method unless they said so.
- Demonstrated, not claimed: a refusal with its cost, a named place, a number. Weak words need something specific beside them; banned words stay out.
- Vermont is the setting, not the selling point. City readers respond to the maker, values, and distinct flavor more than "made in Vermont."
- One story per page. Taste lines must be accurate; overpromising costs more than it gains.
- The ending invites only to what the maker has confirmed they can sustain.

## Weak and strong

Weak (fictional Birch Hollow Creamery):
> In the picturesque Vermont countryside, Birch Hollow Creamery was founded in 2014 by Ana Ruiz. In 2016 we added a cave. In 2019 we went seasonal. Today we make artisan cheeses with passion, honoring European traditions while embracing innovation. Come visit us anytime!

It fails because it opens on scenery and a date; it's a timeline; the 2019 change, the one real story, is a bare fact with no cost; it uses banned and unsupported words; there's no checkable taste and no photo; and "come visit anytime" is unconfirmed and says nothing.

Strong (opening and the work, excerpted):
> `[Photo: Ana in the aging room, turning a wheel.]`
>
> In 2019, Ana Ruiz stopped making cheese in winter. From then on, Hollow Tomme would come only from May to October, while her 22 Jerseys were out on grass. `[ask: what made you decide?]` Two wholesale accounts wanted cheese all year. That first year, both left.
>
> The cheese tells you why she did it. `[ask: in your own words, what does pasture milk do to the cheese?]` Each wheel spends five to eight months on the shelves, turned by hand every week for at least five of them. It comes out tasting of brown butter and cut grass, with a little mushroom near the rind.
>
> `[Photo: the herd walking out through the gate in early light.]`
>
> Last year, both accounts came back.

Cheese page, short version:
> **Hollow Tomme.** A raw-milk tomme made only while the cows are on pasture, so every wheel is a slice of one summer. Raw cow's milk, natural rind, aged 5 to 8 months, 4-lb wheels. Brown butter and cut grass, a little mushroom near the rind. Try it with dry cider, or rye bread and honey (suggestions). `[ask: how do early-summer wheels differ from late-summer ones?]`

## Self-check

- Does the about page open on a person, a moment, or a problem, not a date or a view?
- Could a reader say what the story is about in one sentence, and would it match the essential story?
- Does at least one choice connect to a taste the reader could confirm?
- Are the maker's own phrases in it, and is every fact traceable, with gaps left as `[ask: …]`?
- Is there a photo of a face doing the work, and does the invitation appear only if confirmed?
