# Your Cheese Story: The Flow

*Vermont Cheese Council work. How a maker and the AI move from a pile of raw material to a story and the forms it travels in.*

---

## How it moves

**Big stages, each with its own job.** Inside each stage the app does real work of its own and offers lots of ways to interact. There is always a default that guides the maker, so they never have to make decisions, and the default can always be overruled.

**The app says when it's ready to move on.** For example: "I think I have enough to go on to stage 2." That's a suggestion, not a gate.

**Doors go both ways.** At any stage, either the AI or the maker can say "this makes me think we should add X, or explore X, in an earlier stage." You step back, add or explore it, then return or carry on forward.

**It's a loop, and it's never done.** What's missing at any stage is the route back to an earlier one, usually to gathering. Each pass sharpens the story.

---

## Stage 1: Gathering

The app goes out on its own first, then invites the maker to pour in as much as they want or can. This stage is deliberately promiscuous.

**What the app does itself:**
- Scrapes the creamery's website for everything that talks about them.
- Searches for blog posts, articles, write-ups, podcast interviews: anywhere they talk about themselves.

**What it invites from the maker:** downloads of anything at all, including tacit knowledge, feelings, thoughts and technical details. That covers the farm or creamery, the animals, the land, their history, the history of their cheese, the specific cheeses (their qualities and how they're made), cool stories, and things they invented.

**The default rhythm:** the app offers suggested prompts → takes in whatever the maker gives → reviews it → offers new prompts based on what it now knows → repeats.

**Moving on:** when the app thinks it has enough, it says so and offers stage 2.

## Stage 2: Initial story shape

Now we're just beginning to think about the story.

**What the app does:** it reads everything gathered, using the story arcs and elements (`story-arcs.md`) as lenses. Each arc tells it what to look for. Taste of Place looks for the specific land, the feed and season, and the sensory line linking place to flavor. Chosen Ancestors looks for the tradition, the journey of choosing, and what they made their own. And so on. It sorts the particulars by element, because the elements show which arcs the maker can carry. It also flags what fits no arc, since the leftovers are often where a maker's real distinctiveness sits. Walter Räss's line ("in Switzerland we follow recipes; Vermont has cheesemakers") wouldn't have come out of a template.

**The governing question:** what facts does this maker have that no one else can claim, and which of them can a taster confirm in the mouth? The particulars do the selling, not the category.

**The default rhythm:**
- The app offers story hypotheses, usually one arc and sometimes two braided together, and introduces which story elements seem to match.
- It asks the maker what they think. The maker can approve, reject, ask for more approaches, or keep some strands and drop others.
- It tells them what would strengthen the story, what's missing from it, and what the alternatives are.

**Doors back:** missing elements, especially the bold (necessary) ones, become prompts that send the maker back to gathering.

*von Trapp example (Chosen Ancestors / "ancient method, new place"):* rocky Vermont land → not good for lots of cows, so bulk dairy isn't an option → so the family went to cheese → Alpine cheese, because it fit this place and because of Alpine cheese's history → Neolithic people made it thousands of years ago, facing problems much like the von Trapps' → you can taste that inventiveness and that land in the cheese, a deep taste and texture of place → and how von Trapp made it their own. The method's history is claimed, not the location's; the place is the new chapter.

## Stage 3: Audience

**Start from where they buy.** Channel and geography do more of the persona work than demographics.
- **Where:** at the creamery or farm store, in Vermont shops, from cheesemongers in city shops, online.
- **Who, and what they're going for:** connoisseurs looking for a very particular story, Vermont tourists trying to capture that feel, adventurous eaters.
- **What the research says:** income and education don't predict who pays the premium; values and attention do. "Made in Vermont" is strong at home, where family farm leads, and nearly worthless in Boston and New York, where buyers pay for environmentally friendly methods, fair wages and regional flavor. The same wheel may carry different arcs in different places. von Trapp might lead with Inheritance or Working Landscape at the farm store and Why It Tastes Like This at a Manhattan counter.

**The relay.** Anything sold away from the farm reaches the buyer through someone else, and cheese is mostly bought to share, so the buyer retells it. The personas are links in a chain: **maker → monger → buyer → guest at the table.** Each link needs a different-sized piece:
- **Monger:** a profile line, one quotable "why," and a character detail about the maker. Something to say in thirty seconds while someone tastes.
- **Buyer:** one sentence they can say while passing the board (For the Table).
- **Visitor at the farm:** the experience and the relationship, with place as the setting.

**Testing with agentic personas.** Agent versions of the personas show what they respond to and what they don't, grounded in the research where possible:
- **Novelty seeker** (~44% of specialty-cheese buyers in one study): responds to award stickers.
- **Uncertainty-averse buyer** (~47%): responds strongly to sensory shelf talkers.
- **Monger agent:** "What do I say in thirty seconds while they taste?"
- Plus the channel personas above.

**Doors back:** what a persona needs and the story doesn't have goes back to stage 2 or stage 1.

## Stage 4: The essential story

A few key elements of a small, essential nature, such as "ancient history and modern inventiveness that tastes like Vermont."

## Stage 5: Telling it

**Forms:**
- A longer version on the website
- A photo essay
- The Instagram page
- The press release
- The video
- The packaging
- The relay pieces from stage 3: monger card, shelf talker, the For the Table line

**Who else might tell it:** food writers, bloggers and cheesemongers, plus people outside cheese entirely who are fascinated by Alpine history, cultural angles or Vermont tourism. The story gets packaged for each of them.

---

## Open questions travel everywhere

Open questions aren't a stage. They're the doors. Any stage can produce them:
- Questions the AI can research itself.
- Questions to put to the cheesemaker, which send the maker back to gathering.
