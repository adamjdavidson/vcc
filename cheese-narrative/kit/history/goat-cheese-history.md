# Goat cheese: the history, from the first herds to Vermont

*Background for makers of goat's-milk cheeses: fresh chèvre, lactic and bloomy goat cheeses, aged goat tommes, feta-style brined cheeses. It covers where goat cheese came from, why it exists, and how it became one of the signatures of the American and Vermont cheese revival. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012), plus the sources listed at the end.*

---

## The short version

Goat cheese is probably the oldest kind of cheese there is. Goats were among the first animals people domesticated. The earliest cheeses were soft, fresh and soured by acid rather than rennet, much like a basic fresh chèvre. In France, peasant women south of the Loire spent centuries refining small, tangy, slow-set goat cheeses. In America, goat cheese barely existed until about 1980. Then a handful of women, including one in Vermont, built it from nothing, and the goat cheese salad became the emblem of a new American way of eating.

## Why goat cheese exists: the deep story

- **Goats came first.**
  - Goats and sheep were domesticated in the foothills of the Taurus and Zagros Mountains (today's Turkey and Iran) around 8500 BC, soon after the first crops.
  - Kindstedt notes that goats were especially suited to it: they were used to living in confined mountain caves, and they hung around the new farming villages.
  - Ancient DNA from Ganj Dareh in Iran shows goats under human management by about 8200 BC.
  - At first they were kept for meat. Turning them into milk animals took generations of breeding, and people also had to learn how to get a goat to let down her milk for a human.
- **Cheese solved a body problem.**
  - When dairying began (around 7000–6500 BC), almost no adults could digest milk sugar. Drinking milk caused bloating and diarrhea.
  - Letting milk sour and draining off the whey removes most of the lactose. Fresh, acid-set curd was food adults could actually eat.
  - Kindstedt's point is striking: cheese came *before* adult milk-drinking, and cheese is what made dairying worth doing at all. Adult lactose tolerance spread only around 5500 BC, in Europe.
- **The first cheeses were basically fresh chèvre.**
  - The earliest cheeses were almost certainly soft and acid-coagulated, made in pottery and drained in clay sieves or baskets.
  - The oldest direct evidence of cheese comes from milk residues in strainer-shaped pots in Poland (about 7,000–7,500 years ago) and on the Dalmatian coast of Croatia (about 7,200 years ago).
- **Goat milk behaves differently.** Cow's milk separates into cream; goat's and sheep's milk mostly don't. So the ancient goat and sheep method, still used in Turkey, ran in stages: ferment the milk into yogurt, churn it for butter, then heat the leftover buttermilk to get a ricotta-like curd.
- **A myth Kindstedt doubts.** A traveler carrying milk in a bag made from an animal's stomach finds it has turned to cheese. That's the usual story of how rennet was discovered. Kindstedt points out that an adult traveler of the time probably couldn't have drunk milk, so the story doesn't hold up (unless the milk was for a child).

## Goat cheese in the ancient world

- **The Cyclops's cheese cave.** In Homer's *Odyssey*, Odysseus lands on an island full of bleating sheep and goats. He finds the Cyclops's cave racked with drying cheeses and vessels of saved whey. Kindstedt calls the Cyclops's daily routine arguably the first credible written description of rennet cheesemaking. The cheeses were small, lightly pressed sheep and goat cheeses like the caprinos and pecorinos still made in southern Italy.
- **Goat cheese as medicine.** In the *Iliad*, a wounded warrior is revived with wine sprinkled with grated goat cheese and barley. Bronze cheese graters turn up in the graves of Greek warriors from the 800s BC, a sign that grating cheese into wine was a ritual of warrior feasting.
- **Cheese for the gods.** The Greeks offered cheese and cheesecakes, often made with goat's or sheep's cheese and honey, at their temples. They credited a minor god, Aristaeus, with teaching humans to make cheese. In Sparta, schoolboys were encouraged to steal food as soldier training, and competed for the record number of cheeses stolen from the temple of Artemis.
- **The first shipped cheese.** Clay tablets from Ugarit on the Syrian coast record cheese shipped by sea from Ashdod around 1200 BC. That is the first recorded maritime cheese trade. Kindstedt suggests these may have been brined white cheeses of the feta family, packed in sealed clay jars. Brined white cheeses salted in their own whey are a natural fit for hot climates, and Aegean feta was shipped in clay jars into the 1800s. (Feta-style makers: this is your deep line.)
- **Romans flavored their fresh cheese.**
  - Varro said the best rennet came from a kid, or oddly, a hare, rather than a lamb.
  - Pliny noted that smoking improved goat cheese, and Rome loved smoked cheese.
  - Columella, writing around AD 60, told farmers they could give fresh cheese any flavor they liked, naming pine nuts, thyme and applewood smoke.
  - Herbed, smoked and flavored chèvre has a 2,000-year-old pedigree.

## France: the Loire and Poitou

- **Peasant women, little milk, slow curd.**
  - Kindstedt reconstructs how the small French goat cheeses probably arose on medieval peasant farms. Women had a few animals and very little milk at a time, on top of a crushing list of other chores.
  - South of the Loire, peasants traditionally kept goats rather than cows. There, women pooled milk from several milkings, let it set slowly at room temperature with a small dose of rennet, sometimes for 24 hours or more, and drained it.
  - The result was the acidic, dense, "lactic" curd behind the small goat cheeses of central France: Crottin, Sainte-Maure and their cousins. They became recognizable fixtures of the French countryside in the later Middle Ages.
- **The goat cheeses of the Loire today.** Five protected Loire goat cheeses:
  - **Pouligny-Saint-Pierre:** France's first protected goat cheese, 1972. Its pyramid is said to copy the village steeple.
  - **Selles-sur-Cher:** 1975. Ashed, traditionally with burnt vine shoots.
  - **Crottin de Chavignol:** 1976. First written record 1829.
  - **Sainte-Maure de Touraine:** 1990. Balzac mentioned it in 1841. Its rye straw once held the log together and is now engraved against counterfeits.
  - **Valençay:** 1998. Valençay was the first place in France with protected names for both its cheese and its wine.
- **Goats replaced vines.** In Poitou, the phylloxera louse wiped out vineyards around 1876–1880, and farmers turned to dairying. Cooperatives followed: butter from cows first, then goat milk. The first cooperative goat dairy opened at Bougon in 1906. Poitou-Charentes now produces about half of France's goat milk. It's a story about a farming region reinventing itself around goats after a disaster.

## America: from zero to the goat cheese salad (1979–1990s)

- **There was no goat cheese.** *culture* magazine sums up 1980 bluntly: domestic goat cheese production was effectively zero. Kindstedt describes how 20th-century American cheese became almost entirely factory-made. Cheeses that couldn't be scaled up simply disappeared.
- **The "goat ladies."** The revival was led almost entirely by women:
  - **Laura Chenel**, Sonoma, California. She apprenticed with farmstead cheesemakers in France and began making chèvre in 1979. Alice Waters bought her cheese for Chez Panisse around 1980–81, baked it, and served it on garden greens. That dish, the baked goat cheese salad, became a symbol of the new California cooking.
  - **Mary Keehn**, Cypress Grove, California. She founded it in 1983, a single mother of four with a $10,000 family loan. Her line: "In '83, people didn't like goat cheese." She later created Humboldt Fog.
  - **Jennifer Bice**, Redwood Hill Farm, California. A back-to-the-land family goat farm since 1968.
  - **Judy Schad**, Capriole, Indiana. Launched in 1988; her Wabash Cannonball won Best of Show at the American Cheese Society in 1995.
  - **Allison Hooper**, Vermont Butter & Cheese, 1984. See below.
- **What happened next.** Each of these companies eventually sold to a large dairy company: Chenel's to a French family firm, Cypress Grove and Redwood Hill to Switzerland's Emmi, and Vermont Creamery to Land O'Lakes. Goat cheese went from unknown to a supermarket staple in about 30 years.

## Vermont's goat cheese story

- **Vermont Butter & Cheese / Vermont Creamery (1984).**
  - Allison Hooper learned cheesemaking on a farm in France as a college student around 1980.
  - In 1984 Bob Reese, then marketing director at the Vermont Agency of Agriculture, needed a goat cheese for a state "Restaurateur of the Year" dinner at the Topnotch in Stowe, and asked Hooper to make it. The chefs wanted more.
  - The two partnered with a few thousand dollars and started in a Brookfield milk house.
  - Hooper drove cases of chèvre to New York restaurants herself.
  - The company moved to Websterville in 1989 and was renamed Vermont Creamery in 2013. It became a certified B Corp and bought goat milk from family farms across Vermont and Ontario.
  - It sold to Land O'Lakes in 2017.
  - Hooper and Reese also founded the Vermont Cheese Council (1996–97) and the Vermont Cheesemakers Festival (2009).
- **Lazy Lady Farm, Westfield (1987).** Laini Fondiller traveled the world from 1980 to 1984, fell for French cheese, and started making goat cheese off the grid in the Northeast Kingdom. She aged her first cheeses in a plastic-lined closet. Her goats helped stock other Vermont goat farms, including Twig Farm.
- **Does' Leap, East Fairfield (1997).** Organic goat dairy that rotates its herd to fresh pasture every 12 hours.
- **Blue Ledge Farm, Salisbury.** Farm founded in 2000, cheese from about 2002. In 2004 the owners sold the farm's development rights to the Vermont Land Trust to fund their cheese room.
- **Consider Bardwell Farm, West Pawlet (2004).** It revived the name of the man who built Vermont's first cheese factory in 1864. Its first cheese was a goat cheese, Mettowee, from an Oberhasli herd. It closed after a 2019 recall; its cow's-milk recipes live on at Woodlawn Creamery.
- **Twig Farm, West Cornwall (2005).** Founded by a former cheese buyer who apprenticed at a Vermont farm.
- **Boston Post Dairy, Enosburg Falls (creamery 2010).** The Gervais family farm, named for the old Boston Post stagecoach road that crosses the land.
- **Ayers Brook Goat Dairy, Randolph (2012).** Started by the Vermont Creamery founders as a demonstration goat dairy, to show farmers that goat dairying could pay.

## Story seeds

- **The oldest cheese.** A fresh goat cheese is about as close as anyone can get to the first cheese ever made: soured, drained, eaten fresh.
- **Cheese that made milk edible.** Goat cheese begins as a solution for people who couldn't digest milk. That's still part of why some people reach for it.
- **Women's cheese, again and again.** From Loire peasant women setting milk overnight to the American "goat ladies" of the 1980s, goat cheese has been a women's craft and a women's business. The founder of Vermont's best-known goat cheese company is part of that line.
- **Start from nothing.** In 1980 Americans didn't eat goat cheese. A Vermont maker of chèvre is working in a tradition that's thousands of years old in Europe but only about 45 years old here, and Vermont was there at the start.
- **The chef connection.** American goat cheese was built through restaurant kitchens: Chez Panisse in California, and a Stowe dinner for Vermont's restaurateurs.
- **Flavor the fresh cheese.** Herbs, ash, smoke and pine nuts all have roots in Roman farm manuals and Loire tradition.
- **Goats as reinvention.** In Poitou, goats replaced ruined vineyards. In Vermont, goat dairies have been one answer to the long collapse of cow dairying.

## Careful

- **The "Moors left their goats at Poitiers in 732" story is a legend.** The official EU specification for Chabichou du Poitou calls the name origin a legend. Archaeology shows goats in the region for thousands of years before that. If used, tell it as a legend.
- **The Valençay story is also a legend.** Talleyrand (or Napoleon) is said to have cut the top off the pyramid to avoid reminders of Egypt. The official specification credits Talleyrand with the shape and never mentions Napoleon.
- **The Vermont Creamery origin story is often told as "a chef asked for chèvre."** The company's own account is that Bob Reese needed it for the Agency's dinner. Accounts of the start-up money also vary ($2,000–$2,400 of their own, plus a small loan).
- **Say "goats were among the first animals domesticated,"** not "the first." Sheep were domesticated at roughly the same time and place.
- **Founding years for small farms vary** between "farm" and "cheese" dates. Check with the maker.
