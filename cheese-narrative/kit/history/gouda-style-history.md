# Gouda and Dutch-style cheese: the history, from the polders to Vermont

*Background for makers of Gouda, Edam, aged Goudas, goat and sheep Goudas, and other Dutch-style washed-curd cheeses. It covers where these cheeses came from, why they exist, and how the tradition reached Vermont. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012), plus the sources listed at the end.*

---

## The short version

Gouda was invented as a product for export. It came from farmers on drained, sinking bogland who couldn't grow wheat any more, in a country that had to sell abroad to buy its bread. Everything about the cheese answers that situation. The rounded shape survives being thrown into a ship's hold. Washing the curd with hot water lets a big wheel dry enough not to rot inside, and it leaves the cheese sweet instead of sharp. Coloured coatings kept insects off and caught a buyer's eye. The name isn't a recipe or a village where cheese was made. It's the market town where farmers were required to sell. Holland then industrialized Gouda more thoroughly than any country has industrialized a cheese, and the raw-milk farmhouse version nearly disappeared. In America the name is free for anyone to use. In Vermont there have only ever been a handful of serious Dutch-style makers.

- **The core technique:** draining off part of the whey and replacing it with hot water ("washed curd"). Less milk sugar is left, so less acid forms, and the cheese stays sweet and supple and can age for years.
- **The core story:** an engineered cheese made for travel and trade by free farmers who had reclaimed their land from the sea.

## Why Gouda exists: the problem it solved

- **A landscape that stopped growing grain.**
  - Kindstedt explains that Holland's farmland was largely reclaimed peat bog. From the 11th to the 14th century, peasants dug drainage ditches and dikes to dry it out and grew bread grain on it.
  - But drained peat shrinks. Over the centuries the land sank, in places from several yards above sea level to several yards below it. In the mid-1300s the sea also rose a little.
  - Fields became too wet for wheat. Farmers had two choices: quit, or change what they farmed. Many turned to dairy, which can live on wet grass.
- **A country that had to export.**
  - With less homegrown grain, Holland imported grain from France and the Baltic. To pay for it, the Dutch needed high-value exports. Beer from barley and hops was one. Cheese became the big one.
  - So from the start Dutch cheese was made for buyers far away, and for long voyages.
- **Every feature of the cheese answers the voyage:**
  - **Round shape.** Edam was pressed in a rounded wooden mold into a 4-lb ball. A ball has no corners to chip open and let rot in. It packs into barrels and survives rough handling. It also has the least surface for its size, so it holds moisture on hot sea voyages long enough to ripen well.
  - **Washed curd.** Gouda was bigger, 15 to 16 lb. A cheese that size has to leave the press drier, or it rots from the center. South Holland cheesemakers drained off whey after cutting and stirring and scalded the curd with hot water before molding. That pushed out more whey. It also diluted the milk sugar the bacteria feed on, so less acid formed. Kindstedt notes the result was "very sweet or low-acid."
  - **Brine salting.** Edam soaked in brine for nine to twelve days. Kindstedt says this struck the balance between too wet (internal rot) and too dry (no ripening).
  - **Rind armor.** Edam was scalded in hot whey to smooth the rind. Then it was rubbed with turnsole, a plant dye used in medieval manuscripts, which turned violet when exposed to ammonia fumes from urine and dried to a glowing red. Gouda was painted with saffron extracted in vinegar, which dried a bright yellow. The coatings repelled flies and maggots, a real advantage for a cheese with a lot of surface. They also made the cheeses recognizable at a glance. Today's coloured wax and plastic coatings go back to this.
  - **Spice cheese for the skim milk.** Holland also made butter. Instead of skimming the cheese milk the way East Anglia did (which ruined English cheeses' reputation), the Dutch made a separate skim-milk cheese flavoured with caraway and cloves and coated red. That kept the whole milk free for Edam and Gouda.
- **Flavour is the consequence.**
  - Washing out lactose is why a young Gouda is mild, sweet and smooth rather than tangy.
  - A low-acid, well-salted wheel can age a long time. Over a year or more it firms up and turns brittle, and picks up the caramel and butterscotch notes buyers know from aged Gouda.
  - The crunchy white specks in long-aged Gouda are crystals of tyrosine, an amino acid released as proteins break down. Kindstedt himself co-authored the x-ray study that confirmed this in two-year-old Gouda. They are not salt and not a defect.

## Deep history

- **Before the Middle Ages: dune-ridge dairying.** Coastal Holland had small Neolithic settlements on dune ridges above the salt marshes. Cattle grazed the marsh in summer and came up to higher ground in winter. Butter churns and cheese molds have been found at pre-Roman sites. Rome took dairy products as tribute but mostly left Holland alone as a soggy frontier.
- **1000s–1300s: reclamation by free peasants.** The Counts of Holland and the Bishops of Utrecht needed labor to drain the bogs. They paid for it by offering peasants their freedom and near-absolute ownership of the land they reclaimed. Kindstedt traces Holland's later character to this: large family farms owned by free farmers, and towns like Leiden, Haarlem, Delft, Rotterdam and Gouda growing on grain surpluses.
- **1143.** Gouda first appears as a place in official records. According to the town, farms nearby were already making cheese and bringing it to Gouda to trade.
- **1350–1500: the ecological crisis turns farmers to dairy.** The drained land sank, the sea rose, the fields flooded. The Dutch built polders, fields ringed by dikes with the water pumped out. In 1408 a windmill was first hooked to a pump to drain a polder, and by the mid-1400s windmill pumps were common. Even so, much land stayed too wet for wheat. Cheese and butter production rose steadily.
- **1395: Gouda gets the cheese monopoly.** The European Commission's account says Gouda acquired a feudal right to be the place where farmers in the county of Holland had to sell their cheese. Over time the town's name became the cheese's name.
- **1408.** Alkmaar's cheese market is first mentioned.
- **1500–1700: the Golden Age.** Kindstedt calls Holland's economy probably Europe's fastest-growing in this period, with dairy farms leading the way.
  - A typical herd grew from five or six cows in the early 1500s to about fifteen by mid-century and about twenty-five in the 1600s.
  - In the 1600s Gouda's market handled roughly 4 to 6 million lb of cheese a year. Hoorn, near Edam, handled 4 to 5.5 million.
  - Hoorn was called the "cheese provisioner of all of Europe."
  - Cheese was standard ship's food across the Dutch merchant fleet.
  - Kindstedt believes the washed-curd method for both Gouda and Edam was in place by about 1700, before English writers describe scalding. He says it's possible the influence ran the other way, though.
- **1668.** Gouda's weigh house (the Waag), designed by Pieter Post, is completed. It still stands, and cheese is still weighed there on market days.
- **Edam becomes a small Gouda.** The name Edam started with German Rhineland merchants calling imported Dutch cheese "Edamer," after the port it shipped from. Kindstedt says Gouda's washed-curd method sold so well that Edam makers adopted it, turning Edam into a smaller version of Gouda.
- **1803.** Dutch cheese exports reached 18.7 million lb, the first year with reliable national records. Italy and Iberia were the biggest buyers. Germany, England, France, the West Indies and North America also bought it.
- **Late 1800s–1910: the factory wins.** The Dutch government invested heavily in dairy science and factory cheesemaking. By 1910 factory cheese (42.1 million lb) had overtaken farmhouse cheese (41.8 million lb), mostly for export. Kindstedt calls Holland arguably the most industrialized and technology-intensive cheese country on earth. Farmhouse cheesemaking almost vanished until a late-20th-century revival.
- **Today's protections.** "Gouda" on its own is not protected anywhere. In the EU:
  - **Noord-Hollandse Gouda** became a protected designation of origin in 1996.
  - **Gouda Holland** became a protected geographical indication in 2010. It covers cow's-milk Gouda made in the Netherlands.
  - **Boerenkaas** ("farmer's cheese") is a separately protected traditional name for raw-milk Gouda made on the farm. It's the closest living relative of the pre-factory cheese.
  - The Thursday cheese market in front of Gouda's city hall still runs from spring through summer, with prices agreed by clapping hands.

## America

- **Dutch cheese arrived by ship long before Dutch cheesemakers did.** Kindstedt lists North America and the West Indies among the buyers of Dutch cheese by 1803. Durable, coated rounds were exactly the kind of cheese that survived the trip.
- **The name was never protected here.** Because "Gouda" is generic outside the EU, American Gouda covers everything from industrial blocks to raw-milk farmstead wheels. Cabot sells a Gouda under its Vermont brand. Its parent cooperative, Agri-Mark, says it's made at the co-op's plant in Chateaugay, New York.
- **Dutch immigrants brought the farmhouse method.** The clearest modern example is Marieke Penterman. She grew up on a 60-cow dairy farm in the Netherlands, moved to Thorp, Wisconsin, in 2002, and in November 2006 began making raw-milk Gouda because she missed the cheese from home. Her Gouda won a gold medal at the U.S. Championship Cheese Contest in 2007 and was named U.S. Grand Champion in 2013. She learned the recipe at a Dutch boerenkaas farm.
- **A crowded category.** The American Cheese Society runs separate Dutch-style judging classes for Goudas aged up to nine months and for Goudas aged ten months and more. Recent winners' lists are dominated by Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Georgia and California makers. A Vermont Gouda-style maker is competing in a well-stocked field. "Gouda" alone won't set them apart, so the story has to.

## Vermont

Vermont has had very few Dutch-style makers. This is a thin tradition here, and it's better to say so plainly than to pretend otherwise.

- **Taylor Farm, Londonderry (Gouda 1998–2018).** This was Vermont's Gouda.
  - The farm dates to 1840. Jon Wright and his family have run it since 1989.
  - In the 1980s milk-price slump the farm was barely getting by. Wright sold some cows to build a cheese plant and hired the Vermont consultant Peter Dixon.
  - Seven Days (2015) reports that the Wrights first planned to make provolone or Swiss. They found "plenty of cheddars" in Vermont and chose Gouda, which "worked right from the start." At the time it was "Vermont's only version of the Dutch cheese."
  - They milked Holsteins and Jerseys. The Holstein is the black-and-white Dutch breed, so the cows themselves pointed back to Holland. Some molds were Kadova molds imported from Holland. The milk was raw, which is legal because the cheese aged more than 60 days.
  - The LA Times (2003) found Jon Wright in a yellow rubber apron, at work since 4 a.m., having already put 350 lb of Gouda into molds that morning. Production went from about 7,000 lb a year in 2003 to 70,000 lb or more by 2010.
  - Their Maple Smoked Gouda won ribbons at the American Cheese Society, including a first place.
  - The farm stopped making cheese in 2018 and now sells other Vermont makers' cheeses in its farm store.
- **Boston Post Dairy, Enosburg Falls (cheese since 2007).**
  - This is a Gervais family farm near the Canadian border, run by sisters Anne, Theresa and Susan, with about 200 goats and a few cows.
  - **Tres Bonne** is, in the farm's own words, "a goat milk Gouda aged 2–5 months," smooth, mild and nutty.
  - **Eleven Brothers** was their first hard cheese, named because the sisters grew up with eleven brothers ("it's hard growing up with 11 brothers"). The Vermont Agency of Agriculture calls it a goat's-milk Gouda. The farm's own page calls it "tomme style, natural rind." Ask the maker which it is. It took 1st place at ACS in 2026.
- **Cabot Gouda.** Sold under a Vermont brand. Made in New York (see above).
- **The Peter Dixon thread.** Dixon developed the Taylor Farm Gouda. He and Rachel Schaal of Parish Hill Creamery recently consulted for Boston Post Dairy on food-safety systems. Much of Vermont's small-scale cheese runs through a few teachers, and that's a real "Nobody Does It Alone" story.

If a new maker is reviving Gouda in Vermont, they are not joining a crowd. They are picking up a thread that was nearly dropped when Taylor Farm stopped in 2018.

## Story seeds

- **The cheese built for a ship.** Every feature of a Gouda (rounded edges, brined rind, low-acid paste that keeps for years) was designed to survive a voyage. The table line: "This is a cheese engineered to travel." *(Why It Tastes Like This.)*
- **Washed sweet.** One step explains the taste: part of the whey is poured off and replaced with hot water, so there is less sugar to sour. A maker who washes curd can link that choice directly to the sweetness a buyer tastes. *(Why It Tastes Like This.)*
- **Farmers who lost their wheat.** Gouda was born when drained land sank and wheat failed, and farmers switched to cows to survive. A Vermont farm that switched from selling commodity milk to making cheese is living a version of the same move. *(Second Life; The Working Landscape.)*
- **Named for a market, not a recipe.** Gouda is the town where farmers had to sell their cheese. A maker's own cheese name can follow the same logic: named for where it's sold, traded or shared. *(The Name.)*
- **The crystals.** Those crunchy specks in a long-aged wheel are tyrosine, and a UVM cheese scientist helped prove it. It's a wonder-not-lecture detail for a monger card. *(The Living Thing.)*
- **The Dutch cow.** The Holstein came from the Netherlands. A Holstein farm making Gouda is putting Dutch milk genetics back into a Dutch cheese. Use this only if the maker's herd really is Holstein. *(Chosen Ancestors.)*
- **Picking up a dropped thread.** Vermont's only established cow's-milk Gouda stopped in 2018. A new Vermont Gouda-style maker can tell that plainly. *(Chosen Ancestors; The Upstart.)*
- **The coat of colour.** Turnsole red and saffron yellow were insect repellent and branding at the same time. A maker's choice of wax, natural rind, or no coating can carry a short, true sentence about why. *(What We Won't Do.)*
- **Young to old.** Few cheeses change as much with age. A maker selling the same cheese at two ages can turn that into a "This Wheel, This Season" style comparison.

## Careful

- **Don't say "Gouda is from Gouda"** as if the cheese was made in the town. It was traded there and made on farms around it. The name comes from the market right (the EU's account dates it to 1395).
- **Don't call a maker's Gouda "authentic Dutch"** unless they are Dutch-trained or Dutch-born, and even then show it rather than claim it. "Gouda" is unprotected in the U.S., and shoppers punish claimed authenticity.
- **Who invented scalding is disputed.** Kindstedt thinks the Dutch probably had washed or scalded curd before the English, but he says it can't be proven either way.
- **Edam history:** the name came from Rhineland merchants, and Edam later adopted Gouda's washing method. Don't describe modern Edam as an unwashed cheese.
- **Wax isn't original.** The historic coatings were dyes (turnsole, saffron, annatto). Wax and plastic coatings came later. Don't claim a waxed rind is "the traditional Dutch way" without that caveat.
- **Taylor Farm details vary by source.** The farm's site says Gouda from 1998. The LA Times says they first marketed it in 1999. The LA Times names Jon and Kate Wright; Seven Days names siblings Jonathan and Mimi Wright. Production figures come from different years.
- **Boston Post's Eleven Brothers** is called a Gouda by the state Agency of Agriculture and a tomme by the farm itself. Use the maker's own term.
- **Cabot Gouda** is a Vermont-branded cheese made in Chateaugay, New York. Don't list it as a Vermont-made Gouda.
- **Crystals:** they are mostly tyrosine in aged Gouda. Calcium lactate is the usual crystal on cheddar. Some popular sources mix them up.
- **Don't reduce the style to "mild."** It runs from supple to brittle, and from milky to butterscotch.
