# Pasta filata: the history, from the Campanian marshes to Vermont

*Background for makers of stretched-curd cheeses: fresh mozzarella and fior di latte, burrata and stracciatella, scamorza and smoked mozzarella, and aged stretched curds like provolone, caciocavallo and kashar. It covers where these cheeses came from, why they exist, and how the tradition reached Vermont. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012), plus the sources listed at the end.*

---

## The short version

Pasta filata means "spun paste." These cheeses all share one step that no other family has. The curd is soured to just the right point, then dropped into very hot water and kneaded and stretched like dough until it turns glossy, elastic and fibrous. It's then shaped by hand into balls, pears, gourds or braids. The family grew up in southern Italy. Buffalo mozzarella came out of the hot marshes of Campania, and the aged, hanging cheeses like caciocavallo and provolone came from the Apennine hills. Southern Italian immigrants brought the craft to American cities around 1900. Then pizza turned mozzarella into America's best-selling cheese, made almost entirely by machine. Vermont's stretched-curd story is small but vivid: a family mozzarella line that runs back through a New Jersey factory, a failed water-buffalo dairy, and one of the state's most respected cheesemakers hanging caciocavallo-style gourds in a root cellar.

- **The core technique:** acidify the curd until it will stretch (around pH 5.2–5.3), then work it in water at roughly 160–180°F.
- **The core story:** a cheese that happens in the hands, right in front of you, in minutes. And a family of cheeses that ranges from a ball meant to be eaten today to a gourd meant to hang for a year.

## Why pasta filata exists: the problem it solved

Kindstedt doesn't give a single origin story for stretched curd, and nobody can date its invention. What can be explained is what the stretching step does. These are the physical reasons the method suits the places it came from. They are explanations, not documented history.

- **What happens in the hot water.**
  - Ordinary curd is a net of milk protein holding fat and whey.
  - As the curd sours, acid pulls calcium out of that protein net. Food scientists put the right window at about pH 5.2 to 5.3. Too sweet and the curd won't flow. Too sour and it falls apart.
  - In that window, hot water melts the fat and lets the protein soften and flow. Kneading and pulling line the proteins up into long parallel fibers. That's why fresh mozzarella tears in strings, why string cheese peels, and why a good mozzarella is silky instead of rubbery.
  - Cooling it in cold water then sets a thin skin around a soft center.
- **Why that suits a hot place.**
  - Working the curd in near-scalding water heats the whole mass. The cold-water plunge and brine that follow leave a smooth, sealed surface.
  - Fresh versions are made to be eaten within days, close to where they're made. Naples and Salerno were the old markets for buffalo mozzarella.
  - For keeping, the same plastic curd can be molded into a tight, crack-free shape with no seams for mold or insects to get in. It can then be salted, tied with rope and hung in the air to dry and age. That gives you caciocavallo, provolone and scamorza: shapes you can carry, hang, smoke and store.
- **Why buffalo milk.** Kindstedt (quoted in *culture*) says the fat-to-protein balance of water-buffalo milk is "beautifully designed" to hold moisture, which makes an especially juicy, succulent stretched cheese. Buffalo did well in the hot, swampy coastal plains of Campania, where cattle did not.
- **Flavour is the consequence.**
  - Fresh pasta filata tastes of milk: sweet, lactic, a little salty from the brine. Its whole appeal is freshness and texture, which is why the time from milking to eating matters so much.
  - Aged and hung versions turn buttery, tangy, sharp and even peppery. In provolone piccante, the "piccante" sharpness comes from adding lipase enzymes.
- **A curd that can wait.** Kindstedt (2019) describes the old way: partly soured curd was bagged, iced overnight, carried into the city, then warmed and stretched at the shop. Curd-making and stretching could happen in different places, which shapes the American story.

## Deep history

- **Romans heating curd.** Writing around AD 60, Columella described a "hand-pressed" cheese he called the "best known of all," made by adding hot water to the curd soon after it set. Kindstedt says the hot water might have been an early form of cooking, or it might have been used "to plasticize the curd" as in mozzarella. Either way, Roman cheesemakers were experimenting with heat. Rome also loved smoked cheese: Martial ranked the cheeses smoked at the Velabrum in Rome as the best of their kind.
- **Buffalo reach Italy.** The European Commission's account says water buffalo were in Italy by the 7th century, valued for pulling ploughs through marshland without sinking. Genetic work points the same way. The Mozzarella di Bufala Campana consortium favors a route through Sicily with the Arab conquests of the late 900s, spreading to the mainland under the Normans.
- **12th century: the monks of Capua.** The best-known origin tale: the monks of San Lorenzo in Capua gave pilgrims a cheese called *mozza* (or *provatura* when smoked) with a slice of bread. It traces to a 1915 church history and is retold by the consortium. Treat it as tradition, not proof.
- **1570: the word in print.** "Mozzarella" first appears in the cookbook of Bartolomeo Scappi, cook to the papal court. The name comes from *mozzare*, "to cut off." The maker pinches and tears each piece from the stretched mass by hand.
- **1700s: the Bourbon dairy.** Ferdinand IV of Bourbon set up an experimental buffalo farm and dairy on the royal Carditello estate. A period journal quoted by the consortium calls buffalo provola, mozzarellas and scamorze "unknown to the rest of Italy."
- **1861–1900s: collapse and recovery.** After unification the buffalo barns emptied, but by the early 1900s Campania had about 20,000 buffalo again.
- **The hanging cheeses.**
  - **Caciocavallo** means "horse cheese." The usual explanation is that two cheeses are tied together by a rope and hung *a cavallo*, straddling a pole, like saddlebags. Caciocavallo Silano, a cow's-milk cheese from the southern Apennines, became a protected name in the 1990s.
  - **Provolone** means "big provola." The name appeared around the end of the 1800s, when the cheese grew to its present large sizes. Today most of it is made in the north, around Cremona. Provolone Valpadana has been protected since 1996.
  - **Scamorza** is a mozzarella-type ball tied a third of the way down and hung to dry into a pear shape. It's often smoked.
- **Burrata: a 20th-century invention.** Burrata comes from Andria in Puglia. Records credit Lorenzo Bianchino of the Piana Padura farm with creating it in 1956, though some accounts push it back to around 1900. It's a pouch of stretched curd filled with stracciatella, shreds of curd soaked in cream. Kindstedt doesn't cover it. Burrata di Andria became a protected name in 2016.
- **Today's protections.** Mozzarella di Bufala Campana has been protected since 1996. Its rules require fresh buffalo milk, delivered to the dairy within 60 hours of milking. "Mozzarella" by itself is not a protected name.

## America

- **Immigrant cheese.** Kindstedt (2019) describes waves of southern Italian immigrants settling in New York, Boston, Philadelphia and San Francisco around 1900. Italian cheese was at first imported to feed them. By the end of the 1920s, immigrant cheesemakers were making Italian cheeses in New York State, California and Wisconsin. Mozzarella stayed local, made near the big Italian neighborhoods. Until about 1950 much of it in the East was still made the old way, from raw milk without starter, and stretched by hand.
- **Cow's milk, not buffalo.** America had no buffalo herds, so immigrant makers stretched cow's-milk curd (what Italians call *fior di latte*) and called it mozzarella.
- **The 1949 rule.** Kindstedt notes that the 1949 federal Standards of Identity name mozzarella among ten cheeses that must be made from pasteurized milk. Fresh mozzarella in the U.S. cannot legally be a raw-milk cheese. Aged stretched curds, held 60 days or more, can be.
- **Pizza changes everything.** After World War II, cars, highways and suburbs carried the neighborhood pizzeria to the rest of the country.
  - U.S. mozzarella consumption tripled in the 1960s, then nearly tripled again in the 1970s with the pizza chains.
  - Factories got bigger and automated the stretching, and raised yields by leaving more water in the cheese.
  - Kindstedt says some pizza cheese changed so much that it no longer met the legal definition of mozzarella and had to be sold as "pizza cheese."
- **Mozzarella passes cheddar.** Kindstedt writes that cheddar led American cheese "until 2001, when mozzarella moved into the number one position." By 2017 U.S. per-capita mozzarella consumption was nearly ten times the 1970 level.
- **Fresh mozzarella comes back.** Hand-stretched mozzarella, burrata and imported buffalo mozzarella became specialty items again, sold on their difference from pizza cheese.

## Vermont

Vermont has only a handful of stretched-curd makers. Two are active and well documented, and one important attempt failed.

- **Maplebrook Farm, North Bennington (2003).**
  - Mike Scheps is a third-generation mozzarella maker. His family's New Jersey operation (Scheps Cheese) was, according to a Brattleboro Food Co-op profile, once the country's largest mozzarella producer. He started stretching curd there at 14.
  - When that business ended, his father Al moved the family to Vermont and opened Al Ducci's, an Italian food shop in Manchester (1991). Mike made fresh mozzarella in the shop.
  - In 2003 Johann Englert, then in her late sixties, tasted it on a visit. It took her back to her college years in Italy. She bought 20 balls and carried them back to Boston, and the two went into business. Englert died in 2020, and her grandson Alex now runs sales.
  - Maplebrook makes hand-stretched mozzarella, smoked mozzarella, burrata, stracciatella and scamorza smoked over cherrywood. It also makes ricotta, feta and cheddar curds.
  - Since 2010 its burrata and scamorza have been made by Domenico Marchitelli, from Puglia, burrata's home region.
  - The company says it makes about 40,000 lb of cheese a week with about 60 employees, produces some 8,000 burrata a day in season, and has used milk from a single Vermont dairy farm for several years.
  - Al Ducci's, under new owners, still gets Maplebrook deliveries daily.
  - Story notes: it's a family recipe that went from city factory to village shop to Vermont creamery. It's also large and hand-stretched at the same time.
- **Parish Hill Creamery, Westminster West (2013).**
  - Run by Peter Dixon, Rachel Fritz Schaal and Alex Schaal. Dixon is the consultant behind many of Vermont's best-known cheeses.
  - They make raw-milk cheese only when the cows of Elm Lea Farm at the Putney School are on pasture. Their starter cultures are clabbered milk from four named cows (Sonia, Helga, Clothilde and Abigail), kept going at the creamery since they began.
  - **Suffolk Punch** is a caciocavallo-style gourd, stretched by hand, tied and hung "like saddlebags thrown over the back of a horse." It's aged at least three months, weighs 1½ to 2½ lb, and turns buttery, tangy and peppery past six months. It won a World Cheese Awards silver in 2022 and placed at ACS in 2015 and 2019.
  - **Kashar** is made from the same curd, molded into baskets instead of hung. Its rind is rubbed with olive oil. The creamery describes it as Balkan-style, inspired by the basket cheeses of Turkey and southeastern Europe. It won 1st place at ACS in 2016 and 2019.
  - Seven Days (2015) said these cheeses "have no parallels in Vermont."
- **Woodstock Water Buffalo Company, South Woodstock (c. 2002–2009).**
  - Founder David Muller brought in a whole herd at once ("a sensible person probably would have brought two or three," he told the LA Times in 2003). He had a $1 million state loan and a plan for as many as 50 buffalo farms, at a time when South Woodstock had gone from 28 dairies to two.
  - The company made buffalo mozzarella, ricotta and yogurt. *The Nibble* reports its fresh mozzarella won 1st place at the American Cheese Society in 2007, and calls it the only water-buffalo dairy in the U.S. at the time.
  - It closed in February 2008 and was restarted as Bufala di Vermont. In 2009 the herd went to Quebec, where it became the base of Élevages Buffalo Maciocia.
  - Neighbors then bought the farm and founded Vermont Farmstead Cheese Company on the site.
  - Story notes: this is Vermont's own "Hockey Puck" story at the scale of a whole industry. It's useful as context, and a reminder that copying the old-world animal isn't the same as making it work here.

Some Vermont cheeses borrow mozzarella's name or texture (Bridport Creamery's "Swisserella"). Whether the curd is actually stretched is a question for the maker.

## Story seeds

- **The cheese you can watch being made.** Stretching takes minutes, happens in plain view, and is done by hand. A short video or photo sequence of the pull is the most natural "The Hands" image in all of cheese. *(The Hands; photo essay; video.)*
- **Hours, not months.** Fresh mozzarella's quality is about time since milking. A maker who can name the hours from milking to stretching to shelf has a checkable, specific claim. *(What We Won't Do.)*
- **Hand-stretched vs. pizza cheese.** America's most-eaten cheese is mostly made by machine, and some of it no longer legally counts as mozzarella. A hand-stretched ball can be set against that plainly and without sneering. *(The Upstart.)*
- **Family recipe, carried north.** Grandfather to father to son, from a New Jersey factory to a Manchester shop to a Bennington creamery. Most American pasta filata lines run back through an immigrant family. Use it if the maker's line does. *(The Inheritance.)*
- **Cheese on horseback.** Caciocavallo's name and shape are a story in themselves: two gourds tied by a rope and hung over a pole. A Vermont maker hanging gourds in a cellar is choosing those ancestors. *(Chosen Ancestors; The Name.)*
- **Fresh and aged from the same curd.** One curd can become a ball eaten tonight, a smoked pear, or a gourd aged half a year. That's a "This Wheel, This Season" for stretched curd. *(Why It Tastes Like This.)*
- **For the table.** Burrata is made to be cut open in front of guests. "Cut it at the table and let the center run" is the kind of line a buyer repeats. *(For the Table.)*
- **The buffalo that left.** Vermont tried water buffalo and the herd went to Quebec. A maker choosing cow's milk on purpose can say why, and own it. *(Taste of Place; The Working Landscape.)*

## Careful

- **The Capua monks story is a tradition.** It traces to a 1915 church history. The first sure written use of "mozzarella" is Scappi, 1570.
- **When buffalo arrived is disputed.** Sources say anything from the 7th century to the Norman period, with arrival via Arab Sicily, the Lombards, or other routes. Say "by the early Middle Ages."
- **"Hippocrates mentioned caciocavallo"** comes from the Caciocavallo Silano consortium and is repeated on Wikipedia. Kindstedt doesn't say it. Columella's hot-water cheese *might* have been stretched curd, and Kindstedt only offers that as a possibility. Don't say "the Romans made mozzarella."
- **Burrata's origin date** is given as about 1900, the 1920s, or 1956, depending on the source. 1956 (Lorenzo Bianchino, Andria) is the best-documented.
- **When mozzarella passed cheddar:** Kindstedt says 2001. A USDA Economic Research Service article says 2002 (per-capita consumption). Other industry sources say 2009. The figures measure different things. Say "around the turn of the century" unless you cite one.
- **Fresh mozzarella can't be raw-milk** under U.S. standards. Don't let a story suggest otherwise. Aged stretched curds (60 days or more) can be raw-milk, as Parish Hill's are.
- **"Old world," "authentic," "made exactly as in Puglia"** are company claims. Maplebrook uses them about its own cheese. Show the hands, the person and the hours instead.
- **Maplebrook figures are the company's own** (volume, burrata per day, single-farm milk). So is "largest mozzarella producer in the country" about the Scheps family's New Jersey business, which comes from a co-op profile. Sources spell the founder's surname both Englert and Engler.
- **Woodstock Water Buffalo dates** (c. 2002, 2008, 2009) come from trade and press sources and aren't fully consistent.
- **Kashar** is Parish Hill's Balkan-style cheese. Use their wording, not "provolone."
