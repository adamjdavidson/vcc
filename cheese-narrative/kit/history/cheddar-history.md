# Cheddar: the history, from Somerset to Vermont

*Background for makers of cheddar and cheddar-family cheeses (clothbound, block, granular-curd, washed-curd). It covers where cheddar came from, why it exists, how it reached New England, and what it became in Vermont. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012). Kindstedt is a UVM cheese scientist, and the book was published in White River Junction. Vermont details come from the sources listed at the end.*

---

## The short version

Cheddar is a solution to a problem: how to make a very big cheese that won't rot from the inside. English farm women in Somerset worked out that solution over about three centuries. Puritans from England's dairy country carried it to New England, and their descendants carried it north into Vermont after the Revolution. Then factories took the name and turned it into the most-produced cheese on earth. Over that time "Cheddar" stopped being the name of a village and became the name of a method.

Every Vermont cheddar sits somewhere on that line. A clothbound wheel goes back to an early-1800s American answer to hot summers and maggots. A plastic-wrapped block is the factory endpoint of the same line. Some cheddars, like Crowley's washed curd and Plymouth's granular curd, are survivors of the farmhouse styles that came before the factory standard.

---

## Why cheddar exists: the problem it solved

- **The big-cheese problem.** A small cheese can dry out from the surface before it spoils. A big one can't: its center stays wet and under-salted for weeks, and it rots from the inside. Any region that wanted to sell large, long-keeping cheeses had to find a way to squeeze far more whey out of the curd.
- **Two old answers.** Kindstedt says there were only two ways to do it:
  - **Cooking the curd hot.** This is the Alpine answer.
  - **Breaking pressed curd into pieces, salting it heavily, and pressing it again.** This "milled curd" method probably began in the Massif Central of France, where it gave rise to Cantal and possibly goes back to Celtic cheesemakers of Roman times.
- **Cheddar uses both.** Kindstedt names cheddar as the cheese that combines the two. That is the technical core of what a cheddar is.
- **Why the cheeses got big.** The demand came from the market. London's cheesemongers built their warehouses, ships and buying system around big wheels. Regions that wanted into London had to make cheeses that could survive months in transit and storage.

## The English story (1500s–1860s)

- **Cheddar was famous early.** William Camden noted in 1586 that cheesemakers around the Somerset village of Cheddar were known for very large cheeses. At that point they were a local specialty, too far from London to sell there.
- **London picked winners.** In the 1500s and early 1600s East Anglia (Suffolk and Essex) supplied London. Then its farmers chased butter profits by skimming more and more cream from the cheese milk. The resulting skim "flett" cheese got so poor that in 1690 Suffolk cheesemakers complained to Parliament that it had become "only fit for Slaves." Meanwhile Cheshire cheese, regularly shipped to London from 1650, took the market. By 1725 about 13.8 million pounds a year were arriving.
- **Cheshire's tools.** Cheshire makers developed:
  - giant stone-weighted presses, some weighing more than a ton and a half
  - mixing salt into broken curd before pressing, borrowed from the Cantal method
  - rubbing the finished cheese with butter
- **Somerset's answer: scalding.** Somerset and its neighbors took a different route. They drained the whey, heated it, and poured it back over the curd. Later they heated the curd while it was still in small pieces. By the early 1800s, Somerset cheddar makers had combined this with Cheshire-style pre-salting. That combination is cheddar as a method.
- **Joseph Harding, mid-1800s.** Harding, a Somerset cheesemaker, turned cheddar into a written system of set times, temperatures and acidity targets. For the first time a cheese could be made by recipe anywhere in the world. He moved cheddar from women's handed-down craft knowledge to published science.
- **Women's work, men's books.** For centuries English cheese was made by dairywomen, with skills passed from mother to daughter and mistress to maid. From the late 1700s, gentlemen improvers and cheese buyers studied their methods and published them. Some also wrote of the women as backward. Kindstedt treats Harding's system as the end point of that shift.

## New England: the Puritan cheddar line (1630–1850)

- **Dairy people, not just religious refugees.** Most early Massachusetts Bay settlers came from the dairy counties of Suffolk, Essex and Norfolk, and some from the West Country, home of Somerset and Gloucester cheese. John Winthrop's wife Margaret had supervised the dairymaids at the family's Suffolk manor. The 1630 fleet carried 240 cows. In 1631–33 cows arrived on almost every ship.
- **The first market was a dark one.**
  - In 1647 a plague hit Barbados, and Boston merchants shipped emergency provisions.
  - By 1650 New England cheese and butter were going to the West Indies sugar plantations, mostly to feed enslaved Africans.
  - The cycle ran like this: cheese and provisions went south, molasses came back, New England distilled it into rum, and the rum bought more enslaved people.
  - The Puritans had in effect taken the Caribbean cheese market that East Anglia, their grandparents' home, had once supplied.
- **Rhode Island cheese, and who made it.**
  - The finest colonial cheese was "Narragansett" or Rhode Island cheese. Benjamin Franklin sold it in his Philadelphia shop.
  - Family accounts say Richard Smith's wife brought a Cheshire recipe from Gloucestershire around 1637.
  - In the 1700s much of it was made by enslaved Black women on the Narragansett plantations. One dairy reportedly had 24 enslaved dairymaids making 12 to 24 bushel-sized cheeses a day.
  - Kindstedt says plainly that the story of American cheese cannot be separated from this history. The same expansion also brought Puritan cattle into Native hunting grounds and cornfields, one of the early sparks of the violence that followed.
- **Cheshire came first.** Through the 1700s and into the mid-1800s, the most common New England cheese was Cheshire-style, uncooked and pressed. Cheddar-style cooked cheese was on the rise by midcentury. American writers began borrowing the English names (Cheshire, Gloucester, Cheddar) only around the 1790s.
- **The first American cheesemaking manuals were written right next door.** George Hough's *The Art of Cheese-Making* was printed in Concord, New Hampshire, in 1793. Others included Deane (1790) and Johnson (1801). Men wrote them; the women who did the work did not.
- **The American innovation: the bandage.** New England summers are hotter than English ones, and cheeses cracked, dried and got maggots.
  - Makers first "dressed" cheeses by smearing them with melted whey butter.
  - In the early 1800s they began wrapping them in cotton bandages soaked in butter, and later lard.
  - This was enabled by suddenly cheap cotton, which came from Southern slave plantations and New England mills.
  - Kindstedt thinks the greased-bandage wrap may be an American invention that then spread to England. In 1842 English writers noticed that American cheeses came wrapped in pasted cloth.
  - Later steps in the same line: paraffin wax replaced lard, and in the 1900s plastic film replaced wax. **Today's clothbound cheddar and today's plastic block are two ends of the same American story.**
- **The exodus north.** By the mid-1700s southern New England was running out of farmland. Families from Connecticut and Massachusetts moved into Vermont and New Hampshire. Kindstedt says the movement was a trickle during the wars and "surged to a torrent" after the Revolution. They brought their cows and their wives' cheesemaking with them.

## Vermont: from sheep to cheese (1763–1900)

- **Settlement.** Settlers began bringing livestock into Vermont after the French and Indian War, around 1763. The population grew from about 1,000 in 1764 to about 85,000 at the 1791 census and 292,000 by 1840.
- **An early export.** In 1787 a Quebec auctioneer wrote to Levi Allen (Ethan Allen's brother) that cheese was scarce in Quebec and that shipping it up from Vermont would pay. The letter is in UVM's Special Collections.
- **Merino mania first.**
  - In 1811 William Jarvis brought Spanish Merino sheep to his farm in Weathersfield.
  - By 1840 Vermont had about 1.7 million sheep and fewer than 300,000 people.
  - Wool prices then collapsed. Cheaper Western wool arrived by canal and rail, and tariff protection was cut.
  - Roughly two-thirds of the state's sheep were slaughtered between 1846 and 1850.
  - The cleared hillsides stayed, and cows took them over.
- **Vermont as a top cheese state.**
  - The 1850 census counted about 8.7 million pounds of cheese and 12.1 million pounds of butter made in Vermont.
  - Kindstedt ranks Vermont third in the nation for cheese that year, behind New York and Ohio and ahead of Massachusetts and Connecticut.
  - Per cow, Vermont made more cheese than every state except Connecticut.
- **The railroad changed everything.**
  - The Boston–Burlington line opened in 1849.
  - Shipments of cheese from the St. Albans depot rose from 500,000 pounds in 1851 to 2 million in 1860.
  - Vermont historian T. D. Seymour Bassett wrote that the railroad sped the switch from wool to dairy.
  - On Tuesday market days in St. Albans, up to 300 wagons of butter and cheese came into town.
- **The factory arrives.**
  - America's first cheese factory opened in 1851 in Rome, New York, run by Jesse Williams and his son. It made more than 100,000 pounds of cheddar in its first season, about five times a big farm's output.
  - Vermont's first factory was built in 1864 south of West Pawlet by Consider Bardwell. His name lives on in the modern Consider Bardwell Farm.
  - Factories followed in town after town. Hinesburg's Valley Cheese Factory opened in 1866 with managers brought in from New York. Charlotte had two by about 1870.
  - The 1900 census counted 71 Vermont cheese factories.
- **The Civil War pushed cheese off the farm.**
  - Nearly 35,000 Vermont men went to war. Running the state's farms became largely women's work.
  - Kindstedt argues that the new local factories gave exhausted farm women relief from seven-day-a-week cheesemaking. That made it easier to give up a craft women had owned for centuries.
  - Wartime England paid in gold, and US cheese exports to England jumped from 5 million pounds in 1859 to 50 million in 1863.
- **Boom, fraud, bust.**
  - American cheddar exports to England peaked at 148 million pounds in 1881.
  - Then some factories skimmed the cream for butter. Some made "filled cheese," with the milkfat replaced by lard; New York began making it in 1871.
  - The reputation of American cheddar collapsed in England, and Canada, New Zealand and Australia took the market. **America never got it back.**
- **Butter and fluid milk win.**
  - By the 1890s the Franklin County Creamery in St. Albans was rated the largest butter factory in the world.
  - The first milk train left Bellows Falls in 1890. Once refrigerated rail could get fluid milk to Boston, most small Vermont cheese factories closed.
  - Crowley Cheese's own history calls refrigeration "cataclysmic" for small Northeastern cheesemaking.

## Vermont's surviving historic cheddar makers

These are the threads that connect today's makers to the 1800s.

- **Crowley Cheese, Healdville (Mount Holly).**
  - The family says it was making cheese in the farm kitchen by 1824. Winfield Crowley built the factory in 1881 or 1882; sources differ.
  - It shipped from the Healdville rail depot. The factory is on the National Register.
  - Its cheese is a washed-curd style, moister and milder than cheddar, legally classed as Colby. The Crowley method may be older than Colby itself, which was named in Wisconsin in 1885.
  - Crowley calls itself America's oldest remaining cheese factory. Treat that as the company's claim.
- **Plymouth Cheese, Plymouth Notch.**
  - Built in 1890 by local farmers led by Col. John Coolidge, Calvin Coolidge's father, to give them a market for their milk. In 1923 the same man swore his son in as president by kerosene lamp in the farmhouse nearby.
  - The factory closed in 1934 during the Depression. The president's son John reopened it in the 1960s and in 1998 sold it to the State of Vermont on condition that cheesemaking continue.
  - Since 2009, Plymouth Artisan Cheese has made a raw-milk granular-curd cheese from an 1890s-style recipe.
- **Grafton Village Cheese.**
  - Founded in 1892 as a farmers' cooperative to use surplus milk. Fire destroyed it in 1912.
  - The nonprofit Windham Foundation revived it in the mid-1960s.
  - Sold in 2025 to Vermont Farmstead Cheese.
- **Cabot.**
  - On April 1, 1919, 94 farm families bought the Cabot creamery. Each paid $5 per cow plus a cord of wood for the boiler. Some members owned a single cow.
  - They made "Rosedale" butter for Boston and started making cheddar around 1930. That year Vermont's cows outnumbered its people, 421,000 to 359,000.
  - Nearly bankrupt, Cabot merged with the Agri-Mark cooperative in 1992.
  - Cabot cheddar won first in its class at the 1989 U.S. Championship Cheese Contest. Cabot's "World's Best Cheddar" line comes from its 2006 win at the World Championship Cheese Contest.
- **Shelburne Farms.** The 1886 Webb–Vanderbilt estate became a nonprofit in 1972 and began making raw-milk farmhouse cheddar from its own Brown Swiss herd in 1980 (some sources say 1981). It is one of the bridges between the old cheddar world and the artisan revival.
- **Cabot Clothbound, aged at the Cellars at Jasper Hill.** Cabot's cheddar is wrapped in cloth and aged with a natural rind at Jasper Hill. It goes back to the 1800s bandaged wheel, and it won Best of Show at the American Cheese Society in 2006. (See the Vermont 1985–2026 file.)

## What happened to cheddar in the 1900s, and why it matters for a Vermont maker

- **Bigger, wetter, younger, blander.** Kindstedt traces a century of price pressure on factory cheddar. Plants got bigger and more automated. Pasteurization and standardized milk cut defects but flattened flavor. Factories found they could sell more water by making moister cheese, which meant shorter aging. The result was more consistent cheddar with less character, and Kindstedt notes that for the most part the public didn't notice.
- **Cheddar lost its crown.** Cheddar was America's top cheese until 2001, when mozzarella passed it on the strength of pizza.
- **What that leaves.** It leaves a big opening for anyone making cheddar the slow way: aged long, from one farm's milk, sometimes raw, sometimes clothbound. That cheese is a deliberate step back toward what the factory squeezed out.
- **The name fight.** The EU's protected-name system (PDO) has made some formerly generic names, like feta, exclusive to their home regions. Kindstedt, a Vermont scientist who admires European cheese, writes that he gets riled at the thought of "Vermont Cheddar" being forced into some silly substitute name, given its long and proud history. He uses "Vermont Delight" as his example. It's a good, human illustration of why the cheddar name means something here.

## Story seeds

- **"A cheese built to travel."** Cheddar exists because cheese had to survive the trip to London, then to the Caribbean, then to Boston. The size, the dryness and the rind or cloth all exist for the journey.
- **The method, not the place.** Cheddar is a village in Somerset, but the word now means a technique. A Vermont maker is practicing that technique, and adding a place of their own.
- **The women's craft.** For most of its history cheddar was women's knowledge, handed down and then written up by men and moved into factories. That makes a strong frame for any woman making cheddar today, or any maker who learned from a mother or grandmother.
- **The cloth.** The greased cotton bandage may be an American invention, born of New England heat, maggots and suddenly cheap cotton. A clothbound wheel carries all of that.
- **Sheep to cows.** Vermont's open hillsides were cleared for Merino sheep. When wool crashed, cows and cheese inherited the land.
- **The railroad and the milk train.** The railroad first made Vermont a cheese exporter. Then it made fluid milk to Boston more profitable than cheese and nearly ended Vermont cheesemaking. Today's makers are bringing back something the trains took away.
- **The Coolidge factory.** The farmer who built Plymouth's cheese factory also swore in a president by lamplight.
- **The lost reputation.** America once supplied England's cheddar and lost the market through fraud: skim milk and lard. Quality is how you build that kind of trust back.

## Careful

- **The slavery and land history is real and well documented.** It shouldn't be smoothed over if a maker invokes the Puritan or colonial lineage. Kindstedt's framing works well: it is part of the story and can't be separated from it.
- **Crowley's "oldest" claim and 1824 date** are the company's own. The factory date is 1881 or 1882.
- **Vermont was never the nation's top cheese state**, despite one state brochure saying so. Third in 1850 is the well-supported claim.
- **The often-cited "best year for cheese, 1869, 4.8 million lb" figure counts only farm-made cheese.** It is not Vermont's peak.
- **Don't claim a specific modern Vermont cheddar uses an old recipe** unless the maker says so. The lineage belongs to the method.
- **"Vermont cheddar" as a style.** There's no well-sourced definition of a distinct Vermont style (white, sharp, aged) from before the 1980s. It's a reputation, not a standard.
