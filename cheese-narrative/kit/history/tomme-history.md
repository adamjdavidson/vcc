# Tomme: the history, from Savoie and the Pyrenees to Vermont

*Background for makers of tommes: Tomme de Savoie- and Tome des Bauges-style wheels, Pyrenees sheep's-milk tommes like Ossau-Iraty, Italian tomas, and the many farmhouse cheeses that American makers call "tomme." It covers what the word means, where these cheeses came from, and how they reached Vermont. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012), plus the sources listed at the end. Kindstedt rarely uses the word "tomme," so this file leans on his history of mountain and peasant cheese, with the French and Vermont details from the web.*

---

## The short version

"Tomme" isn't a recipe. It's an old mountain word for a farm cheese. In the Savoyard dialect *toma* meant a cheese made in the alpine pastures. In practice, a tomme was the everyday cheese of a family farm in the French Alps or Pyrenees: a modest wheel, pressed but not cooked, with a natural rind, made from whatever milk the farm had. It was often made from milk that had been skimmed so the cream could go into butter. The big cooked wheels like Beaufort and Comté were made to sell. The tomme was mostly made to eat at home. In America the word has become a loose label for a natural-rind farmstead wheel, and Vermont has dozens of them. Vermont's best-known tomme-family cheese, Vermont Shepherd's Verano, was modeled on the sheep's-milk cheeses of the Basque Pyrenees.

- **What "tomme" means:** a smallish-to-medium farm wheel, usually pressed and uncooked, natural rind, mountain or farmhouse origin.
- **What it doesn't mean:** a fixed milk, size, age, texture or method. Outside a few protected French names, anyone can call anything a tomme.
- **Where its prestige comes from:** Savoie and the Pyrenees, where named tommes are protected.

## Why tomme exists: the problem it solved

Tomme answers a different problem from Alpine cheese. Alpine cheese is the answer to *too much* milk on the mountain at once. Tomme is the answer to *not much* milk, on one farm, with butter competing for the cream.

- **A small farm's milk.** Kindstedt describes medieval peasant households with one or two cows (about a gallon of milk a day each, in the early Middle Ages) and a long list of other chores. Women made the cheese with whatever milk there was. A small wheel suited a small amount of milk.
- **The cream went to butter.** Butter sold for more than cheese. Kindstedt shows that by the 1200s English estate manuals expected dairymaids to skim cream for butter before making cheese, aiming for a fixed butter-to-cheese ratio. In Savoie, too, tommes were traditionally made from skimmed milk, the cream kept back for butter (Slow Food; Tomme de Savoie makers). Kindstedt's account of the mountain chalets describes the same habit: evening milk left to cool overnight, some cream skimmed off in the morning, the rest mixed with the fresh milk. That is why old tommes were lean, and why fat content varied from farm to farm.
- **The leftover cheese of the cooperative age.** When villages in Savoie pooled their milk in shared dairies (*fruitières*) in the 1800s, the milk went into big, valuable wheels: Emmental de Savoie, Gruyère, Beaufort. The Tomme de Savoie makers' own history says tomme survived only when there wasn't enough milk for a big wheel, or on farms that didn't send their milk to the dairy.
- **Pressed, not cooked, keeps well enough.** A tomme is pressed to push out whey but usually not cooked like an Alpine wheel. Combined with modest size, that gives a cheese that keeps for weeks or a few months in a cellar, not years. It was food for the household through the season, not a cheese built to travel to Rome or Paris.
- **A natural rind, because nobody had time for more.** The official description of Tomme des Pyrénées puts it plainly: a shepherd could keep these cheeses while doing farm work, because the shape and rind made them easy to ripen. A cave, some planks and a brush were enough.
- **Flavor is the consequence.** A small, lean, natural-rind wheel in a damp cellar grows a gray, mottled, "mousy" rind of wild molds. The flavor is earthy, milky, mushroomy near the rind, and changes with the season's grass. Savoie makers say the cheese tastes different on summer grass than on winter hay.
- **The peasant side of the manor.** Kindstedt's big frame is the medieval manor. The lord's side made larger cheeses for sale and rent; the peasant side made small cheeses for the household, bartered in the village. Most of the soft cheeses of northern France come from the peasant side. The tommes of the mountains belong to the same peasant tradition. In Savoie, the Slow Food account notes, aristocrats preferred richer cheeses, which may be why gastronomes said little about the lean tome while ordinary Savoyards loved it.

## Deep roots: mountain cheese in Roman Gaul

- **Rome ate cheese from Savoie.** Kindstedt cites Pliny the Elder on a cheese called Vatusican from the Centronian Alps, which is the Savoie of today. Galen called it the best and most popular cheese in Rome. Nobody knows what it was like; Kindstedt says a large Beaufort-type cheese is a guess. The point for tomme makers is that Savoie was a cheese country 2,000 years ago (see the Alpine file).
- **Salt roads into the uplands.** Kindstedt describes salt hauled on Roman roads from the Mediterranean into the Massif Central. That made possible the salty upland cheeses of Auvergne: Cantal, Salers and Laguiole, made in mountain huts called *burons*. The fresh, unripened curd of that tradition, *tome fraîche*, is still the base of the Aubrac potato dish aligot (Wikipedia).
- **Alpine know-how reached the Pyrenees.** Kindstedt says the Alpine family of cheesemaking spread "beyond to the Pyrenees, where alpine sheep's-milk cheeses flourished." The Pyrenees tradition is Alpine in its seasonal herding but built on sheep rather than cows.

## Savoie: the oldest everyday cheese

- **The word.** Merriam-Webster traces *tomme* to the French dialects of Savoy and Dauphiné. The official dairy-appellation site and Slow Food both say *toma* meant "cheese made in the mountain pastures" in Savoyard. The single-*m* spelling in Tome des Bauges is the old Savoie spelling. Italy's *toma* and *tuma* come from the same Alpine world, from the Aosta Valley down to the Ligurian coast.
- **Tomme de Savoie.** The French official summary calls it the oldest of the Savoie cheeses and says it was long a cheese for home use, an essential source of protein for peasants. Fat content varied with how much the milk was skimmed, and size varied by farm. The makers' syndicate traces it to the 1500s in the Duchy of Savoy, in the Maurienne and Tarentaise valleys and the Bauges and Chablais massifs. That date is their own.
  - Nearly lost in the 1800s, when the fruitières sent milk to bigger cheeses.
  - In the 1960s French officials resisted protecting the name because so many tommes were made outside Savoie. Farmers won a regional label in 1978 and the EU protected geographical indication in June 1996.
  - Every wheel carries a casein disk under the rind: **green for farm-made, red for dairy-made**. Farm milk must come mostly from the Savoie breeds Abondance, Tarentaise and Montbéliarde.
- **Tome des Bauges.** A raw-milk farm tome from the Bauges mountains, protected as an AOC in 2002 and an EU PDO in 2007. The rules require raw milk, forbid fermented feed and require summer pasture. Milk from the earlier of two milkings may still be partly skimmed, an echo of the butter economy. Slow Food notes a 1793 mention of the cheese in local markets, and an 1807 report that Savoyards loved it.
- **"As many tommes as villages."** The Tomme de Savoie makers quote the saying that France has as many tommes as it has villages. That's the clearest definition there is: a tomme is a village's own cheese.

## The Pyrenees: shepherds' cheese

- **Ossau-Iraty.** The best-known Pyrenees tomme takes its name from the Ossau valley in Béarn and the Iraty forest in the French Basque Country.
  - Made from the whole milk of three local breeds: Manech Tête Noire, Manech Tête Rousse and Basco-Béarnaise.
  - Pressed and uncooked.
  - AOC in 1980; EU PDO in 1996.
  - The official French dairy-appellation site says that in the 1300s the cheese was recognized as a trading currency in rental and purchase contracts, and was the shepherds' main source of income.
- **Summer in the huts.** In summer some shepherds take their flocks to the high pastures (transhumance) and make cheese in mountain huts, called *cayolars* in the Basque Country. Summer-pasture farmstead wheels carry an edelweiss stamp. Farmstead wheels carry a front-facing sheep's head; dairy wheels carry a sheep in profile (Taste France). In the Ossau valley the summer cheese is called *fromage d'estive*. Shepherds there still milk morning and evening in the summer pastures.
- **Tomme des Pyrénées.** A broader protected name (EU, 1996) covering cow's, goat's and mixed-milk tommes from the French side of the range. The black-waxed dairy version dominates; farm versions are growing. A 2020 rule change let raw milk and more milk types in. The official description says it was a keeping cheese made in summer by shepherds.
- **What the Pyrenees add to the tomme story.** In Savoie, tomme was the lean leftover of the butter trade. In the Pyrenees, the sheep's-milk tomme was the main product and the shepherd's income: rich, made from whole milk, and valuable enough to pay rent with.

## America: "tomme" as a farmstead word

- **No tommes arrived with immigrants.** Kindstedt notes that the cheeses immigrants brought to America either scaled up into factory products or disappeared. Small, lean, natural-rind farm wheels had no place in the factory system.
- **The revival brought the word.** From the 1970s on, small American makers went to Europe, worked on farms, and came home interested in exactly the natural-rind cheeses the factories had abandoned (Kindstedt). The French farm tomme was an obvious model: a cheese one family could make from one small herd, with a cellar and a brush.
- **The word loosened.** In American use "tomme" now covers almost any natural-rind farmstead wheel, and sometimes cheeses that are nothing like a tomme. Vermont's Lazy Lady Farm sells La Petite Tomme, a creamy, Brie-style disc that *Bon Appétit* named one of the most important cheeses in America in 2018. That's not a mistake to correct. It shows how far the word has traveled.

## Vermont's tommes

Vermont has more tommes than any other style name except cheddar. Some makers below call their cheese a tomme; others make cheeses in the tomme family under another name.

- **Vermont Shepherd, Westminster West.** David Major's family raised sheep for meat and wool; around 1990 they began milking them and making cheese. Cindy Major told the *Los Angeles Times* their first natural-rind cheese was "the worst cheese ever." In the winter of 1993 the Majors went to the French Pyrenees to find out what they were doing wrong. French farmers tasted the cheese and shook their heads, and told them to stop freezing their milk and stop fighting the cheese with mold inhibitors. Janet Fletcher reported in 2003 that the Majors went farm to farm in the Basque Pyrenees learning to make Ossau-Iraty, and came home using a culture designed for Pyrenees cheeses. Murray's Cheese says Verano and Invierno are based on the traditional cheese of the Basque Country.
  - **Verano** ("summer") is all sheep's milk, made only while the ewes are on pasture.
  - **Invierno** ("winter") blends sheep's milk with a neighbor's cow's milk.
  - The farm built its own cave into a hillside, where wheels ripen on wooden boards.
  - Press and the farm's distributors say Vermont Shepherd has won Best of Show at the American Cheese Society since its first win in 1993.
  - Verdict on the brief's question: **verified.** Vermont Shepherd was modeled on Pyrenees sheep's-milk cheese, specifically Ossau-Iraty.
- **Twig Farm, West Cornwall (2005).** Michael Lee, a former Boston cheese buyer, apprenticed at Peaked Mountain Farm and largely taught himself. His raw goat's-milk **Goat Tomme** has a gray-brown natural rind and is aged at least 80 days, with notes of grass, hay and nuts (*culture*). Since 2019 Twig Farm's goats have lived at Ice House Farm in Goshen, and Lee makes the cheese from their milk (Eat Vermont). Lee told WCAX in 2024 that he makes about 100 pounds a week, by himself.
- **Stony Pond Farm, Fairfield.** Melanie Webb makes **Swallow Tail Tomme** from raw Jersey milk "still warm" from the farm's cows, in 4–5 lb wheels aged 3–6 months. The farm calls it inspired by the French farmhouse style and names it for the barn swallows that return each spring as the cows go out to pasture.
- **Parish Hill Creamery, Westminster West.** **Humble** is a tomme washed in the creamery's own hard cider (*Edible Vermont*). The creamery makes raw-milk cheese only when the cows are on pasture.
- **von Trapp Farmstead, Waitsfield.** **Oma** is a washed-rind tomme. It debuted in early 2009 as one of the first cheeses ripened in the Cellars at Jasper Hill. As of September 2024 it moved back to the von Trapps, who now have their own ripening space (Jasper Hill).
- **Woodlawn Creamery, West Pawlet.** **Pawlet**, an "Italian-style toma" with a washed rind, from raw milk of the Leach family's Holsteins. The Leaches, seventh-generation Pawlet dairy farmers, licensed the recipe from Consider Bardwell Farm in 2024.
- **Lazy Lady Farm, Westfield.** Laini Fondiller, who worked on French farms in the early 1980s, has made off-grid goat cheese since 1987. Her tongue-in-cheek names have included **Tomme Delay**, a tomme named for the Texas congressman.
- **Crawford Family Farm, Whiting.** **Vermont Ayr**, a raw-milk, natural-rind, Alpine-style tomme from the family's registered Ayrshire herd, first made in 2006 (Vermont Fresh Network; Vermont Cheese Council 2008). Current production not confirmed.
- **Others.** Green Mountain Blue Cheese's **Tomme Collins**, named for a friend who helps make their cheese, is listed by the farm as on hiatus.

## Story seeds

- **A village's own cheese.** "As many tommes as villages." A tomme is the cheese of one place and one herd. The Taste of Place arc fits, if the maker can name the place and one thing about it that reaches the cheese.
- **The cheese made from what's left.** Tomme was what the farm made with the milk it had, after the cream went to butter or the big wheels took the rest. A maker who makes a tomme from the milk of a few animals, or between other jobs, is in that line (The Working Landscape; The Hands).
- **Planks, a cellar, a brush.** The official Pyrenees description is a gift for The Hands: a tomme needs a cave, boards and someone to turn and brush it. Turning every wheel by hand every week is that tradition.
- **Summer in the huts.** Pyrenees shepherds made the summer cheese on the mountain and stamped it with an edelweiss. A seasonal Vermont tomme made only on pasture is This Wheel, This Season with a ready marker.
- **The shepherds who said "stop."** Vermont Shepherd's story has a Hockey Puck (the worst cheese ever), a trip to the Pyrenees, and old shepherds telling them what they were doing wrong. It's the clearest Chosen Ancestors story in the Vermont tomme family, and a model for any maker who learned from a specific place.
- **The rind is wild.** A tomme rind is a mottle of whatever molds live in the maker's cellar. That's The Living Thing at its simplest.
- **Cheese as money.** In the Pyrenees, shepherds once paid rent in cheese. One line about that can carry a lot.
- **Say what *your* tomme means.** Because the word means so little to most buyers, the maker's own definition ("our tomme is …") is a chance to be specific. Pair it with one reference cheese a buyer knows.

## Careful

- **Don't let "tomme" stand in for a description.** Most shoppers don't know the word. Say what the cheese is (milk, rind, age, texture, taste) first.
- **Don't claim a protected name.** Tomme de Savoie, Tome des Bauges, Tomme des Pyrénées and Ossau-Iraty are protected in Europe. "In the style of" or "inspired by" is the honest framing, and only if the maker says so.
- **Skimmed milk is history, not a rule.** Old tommes were often lean, but today's Tomme de Savoie rules and most Vermont tommes use whole milk. Don't imply a maker skims unless they do.
- **Dates from makers' groups are their own.** The 1500s origin of Tomme de Savoie (syndicate), the 12th-century mention of Pyrenees tomme (unsourced on Wikipedia), and the 14th-century cheese-as-currency claim (official appellation site, repeated in trade press) should be told as "according to."
- **Ossau-Iraty numbers vary by source and year** (nearly 1,800 shepherds in one official source; 1,141 milk producers plus 173 farmhouse makers for 2020 in another).
- **Vermont Shepherd dates vary.** Sources give the start of milking as 1987 and of cheesemaking as around 1990, and the farm is variously placed in Putney and Westminster West. The "Best of Show since 1993" record is the press's and distributors' phrasing; the farm's own pages don't spell out the year. The farm's current owners are David Major and the Ielpi family (Yesenia); older press names Cindy Major. Check with the maker.
- **Status changes.** Oma moved from Jasper Hill to von Trapp in 2024; Twig Farm's goats moved in 2019; several older Vermont tommes (Tomme Collins; possibly Vermont Ayr) are on hiatus or unconfirmed. Don't list a cheese as current without checking.
- **Pyrenees ≠ Spain only.** Verano and Invierno have Spanish names, but the documented model is the French Basque cheese.
