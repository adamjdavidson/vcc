# Bloomy rind: the history, from the peasant farms of northern France to Vermont

*Background for makers of bloomy-rind cheeses: Brie- and Camembert-style wheels, Coulommiers, Chaource, double- and triple-crèmes, and the soft white-rinded cheeses of Vermont. It covers where these cheeses came from, why they exist, and how the tradition reached Vermont. The main source is Paul Kindstedt's* Cheese and Culture *(Chelsea Green, 2012), plus the sources listed at the end.*

---

## The short version

Bloomy-rind cheeses were born on small peasant farms in the damp north of France, in households with one or two cows where the woman who made the cheese had a dozen other jobs. She pooled a few milkings, set them fast with rennet, ladled the curd into small forms and put the cheeses in a cool cellar. They were too wet to keep long, which suited a region of village markets and, later, Paris. For most of their history these cheeses weren't white: their rinds were gray, blue-green, spotted brown. The snow-white rind comes from a single mold strain picked out around 1900. Then came the railway, the poplar-wood box and industrial dairies. French immigrants brought Camembert and Brie to America, where they mostly vanished into factories. Vermont makers brought back the small, handmade version in the last twenty-odd years.

- **The problem:** too little milk, too little time, a cheese that spoiled fast.
- **The answer:** a small, wet cheese that a mold ripens from the outside in, sold near home within weeks.

## Why bloomy rinds exist: the problem they solved

Kindstedt rebuilds the origin from the constraints of the medieval manor, and says plainly that it's a reconstruction.

- **Very little milk.** Tenant households could graze a cow or two on common land. Early medieval cows gave about a gallon a day, roughly a pound of soft cheese. A single milking came to about half a pound of cheese, hardly worth the work of making it.
- **Very little time.** Milking and cheesemaking fell to the women, who also kept the poultry and pigs, baked, brewed, spun, wove and raised the children. Kindstedt's point: they would choose the simplest method that fit a crowded day.
- **A cool climate helped.** In the Mediterranean, milk soured within hours. In northern France it could sit overnight. So a woman could pool two or three milkings and make one batch.
- **Pooling changes the cheese.** Milk that sits unrefrigerated fills up with lactic acid bacteria. Warmed to about 85°F and set fast with active rennet (around an hour), it makes a curd that is **high in moisture and fairly high in acid**. Ladled into small baskets or forms and drained without pressing, it becomes a small, soft cheese.
- **The cellar did the rest.** Stored somewhere cool and humid, like a root cellar, the surface picked up yeasts and molds from the air. If the cheese and the cellar weren't too wet, **gray and white molds won out over black and blue ones.** As the yeasts and molds ate the acid on the surface, orange bacteria sometimes moved in later. Kindstedt says cheeses like this would have looked something like a traditional Brie de Meaux.

How every feature answers the problem:

- **Small and flat.** Little milk makes a little cheese. A thin shape also lets the mold work all the way through. The rind ripens the cheese from the outside in.
- **High moisture and a short life.** It can't travel far or keep long, so it stays close to market. The Abbey of Corbie's rules from 822 required that cheese tithes from outlying farms be brought in every month so they wouldn't spoil from aging too long. Kindstedt reads that as evidence of soft cheeses with a life of weeks, not days or months, already by the 800s.
- **The rind is a ripening engine.** Science now explains what the peasant women found by trial and error. The surface mold eats the lactic acid and breaks down protein, releasing ammonia. That raises the pH at the surface. The paste then softens from the rind inward, which is why a young Brie has a chalky core and a ripe one runs.
- **Flavor is a result.** Mushroom, cream, cabbage, ammonia and earth come from a mold working through protein and fat. "Ripe" versus "over" is the whole drama of the style.

Kindstedt's broader framing: the same simple recipe split three ways depending on milk age, rennet and temperature. Fast-set pooled milk gave **bloomy rinds**. Very slow, cool, acid-heavy sets gave the **lactic** goat cheeses south of the Loire. Fresh, sweet milk set immediately gave **washed rinds**. He calls the whole family "controlled rotting."

## Deep history

**The early Middle Ages (c. 600–1000).** Few records survive, though manorial records show peasant cheeses paid as rent. A ninth-century monk named Notker tells of Charlemagne, on a meatless Friday, being served "rich and creamy" cheese by a bishop. The emperor cut off the rind. The bishop told him it was the best part. Charlemagne tried it and ordered two cartloads a year. That story is often cited as the birth of Brie. Kindstedt says it proves no such thing (see Careful).

**The manors break up (from about 900).** In northern France, the great estates broke into small peasant farms much earlier than in England, during the Viking-era wars, before a market economy took hold. The resulting landscape of small farms and village markets became, in Kindstedt's word, the "bastion" of soft-ripened peasant cheese. In England, by contrast, peasant cheesemaking died out, and the hard, pressed cheeses of the old estates took over. (Cheddar makers: that's the other branch of the same tree.)

**Brie becomes a Paris cheese (1200s–1800s).** Brie, Neufchâtel, Livarot and Pont-l'Évêque emerged as recognizable cheeses in the later Middle Ages. The Brie region lies just east of Paris. A cheese that had to be eaten within weeks did well with the biggest city in France a short cart ride away. The Paris cheese house Androuet records gifts of Brie to French royalty from 1217 onward.

**The Congress of Vienna (1815).** The best-known Brie story: at a diplomats' dinner, Brie de Meaux was proclaimed king of cheeses. Taste France (the French government's food promotion agency) credits Talleyrand and says Brie beat 52 European cheeses. Androuet credits Talleyrand in one place and Metternich in another, and notes the story rests partly on tradition.

**Camembert (late 1700s).** Normandy had its own soft cheeses. A 1708 French geographical dictionary already names "the excellent cheeses Livarot and Camembert" at the Vimoutiers market. The famous story credits a farm woman, Marie Harel, with inventing Camembert in 1791 after a priest fleeing the Revolution taught her the Brie method. That is a legend (see Careful).

**Rail, boxes and Pasteur (1850–1916).**
- **The railway.** A line from Paris to Lisieux and Caen put Normandy within hours of Paris by the mid-1800s.
- **Napoleon III.** In 1863 he tasted a Camembert from Victor Paynel, husband of Marie Harel's daughter, and ordered deliveries to Paris.
- **Pasteur.** After his discoveries, Camembert moved into industrial production around 1880.
- **The poplar box.** In 1890 an engineer named Ridel introduced the thin wooden box. It protected a fragile cheese on long journeys, including across the Atlantic, and it is still the Camembert's signature.
- **The trenches.** In 1916 Camembert went into French soldiers' rations, which made it a national cheese.

**The white mold (around 1900 to the 1970s).** This is the part of the story most people don't know.
- Before the 20th century, Camembert and Brie rinds were **gray-green or bluish**, often with brown spots. What grew depended on the cellar.
- Around 1900, a **white mutant** was selected for its color from a gray-green mold, *Penicillium commune*. That white mold, *Penicillium camemberti* (also sold as *P. candidum*), has been grown as a clone ever since. A 2020 genomic study from French researchers called it a domesticated fungus, comparing the process to dogs bred from wolves. It is whiter and faster-growing than its wild relatives, makes little or no toxin, and crowds out unwanted molds.
- Genetic tests suggest one key gene arrived in a single strain sometime after 1905. That strain is now used almost everywhere, and the researchers warn that relying on one clone tends toward degeneration.
- According to Wikipedia's history of Camembert, rinds became mostly white in the early 1900s, and **pure white became standard only in the mid-1970s.**
- Many soft cheeses also carry *Geotrichum candidum*, a yeast-like mold that gives a thin, wrinkled rind.

**Protection and the raw-milk fight (1970–today).**
- Chaource got its AOC in 1970 (the official French protected-name status). Its rules require a mostly lactic, slow set of at least 12 hours, so it sits between Brie and the goat cheeses.
- Brie de Meaux got its AOC in 1980. Camembert de Normandie got its AOC in 1983 and must use raw milk.
- In 2008 the big producers, Lactalis and Isigny, pushed to allow heat-treated milk. The appellation held to raw milk, and the large brands left it.
- **Triple-crèmes are a 20th-century idea.** Cream is added to the milk until the cheese is very high in fat (a minimum of 72% fat in dry matter, by one count; many sources say 75%). Brillat-Savarin was created in the 1930s by Pierre and Henry Androuët, the Paris cheese merchants, who based it on an 1890s cheese called Excelsior.

## America

- **Immigrant cheese, then factory cheese.** Kindstedt lists Camembert, Brie, Neufchâtel and cream cheese among the cheeses French immigrants brought. Cream cheese became a giant industry; cheeses that couldn't be scaled up "largely disappeared from the American scene" by the late 20th century.
- **The raw-milk wall.** Since 1949, US rules have required raw-milk cheese to age at least 60 days. A true young Brie or Camembert can't be made from raw milk here and sold, which is why American bloomy rinds are almost always pasteurized. Kindstedt notes that when the artisan revival came in the 1970s and 80s (the American Cheese Society was founded in 1983), the new makers were especially drawn to bloomy, washed and natural rinds. Those are the cheeses the big industry saw as the riskiest.
- **The goat-cheese route.** Much of the early revival in soft-ripened cheese came through goat cheese. (See the goat cheese history file.)

## Vermont

Bloomy-rind cheese in Vermont is mostly a 21st-century story, and it's closely tied to one aging facility: the Cellars at Jasper Hill in Greensboro (operational in 2008; 22,000 square feet built into a hillside).

- **Jasper Hill Farm, Greensboro: Moses Sleeper.** A pasteurized cow's-milk bloomy rind the farm says was inspired by classic French Brie. It is named for Moses Sleeper, one of two Revolutionary War scouts killed defending a blockhouse on the Bayley Hazen Military Road in the Northeast Kingdom.
- **Jasper Hill: Harbison.** A soft bloomy rind wrapped in strips of spruce cambium (the inner bark) harvested from the farm's own woods. According to *Cheese Connoisseur*, it began in 2011 as a rescue: a batch of Moses Sleeper came out too firm, and Mateo Kehler wrapped it in bark to save it. It is named for Anne Harbison, known as the "grandmother of Greensboro." It won Best of Show at the American Cheese Society in 2018. This is the clearest Vermont example of a Hockey Puck turned into a signature.
- **Jasper Hill: Little Hosmer.** A small bloomy rind the farm calls its "mini version of a new world-style brie," named for a pond in Craftsbury. It won a Super Gold at the 2017 World Cheese Awards.
- **von Trapp Farmstead, Waitsfield: Mt. Alice.** A Camembert-style bloomy rind from the farm's certified-organic cows, named for a peak southeast of the farm. The family herd is a mixed "rainbow" of Jersey, Normande, Ayrshire and Montbéliarde cows.
- **Champlain Valley Creamery, Middlebury: Champlain Triple.** Carleton Yoder founded the creamery in 2003 in a rented former deli in Vergennes. *Seven Days* describes Champlain Triple as "supple, mushroomy, bloomy-rinded." It is made from the organic milk of one grass-fed Jersey farm, Severy Farm in Cornwall. Yoder: "We like the single source."
- **Woodcock Farm, Weston: Summer Snow.** Mark and Gari Fischer's sheep's-milk, Camembert-style bloomy rind. The Vermont Cheese Council lists it as available only from spring through summer. A ready-made This Wheel, This Season story.
- **Vermont Creamery, Websterville: Bonne Bouche and Cremont.** These are *Geotrichum*-rinded cheeses, the wrinkled cousin of the white bloom. Bonne Bouche is an ash-dusted, hand-ladled goat cheese. Cremont is a double-cream blend of cow's and goat's milk with added cream. Technically they're closer to the French lactic goat cheeses than to Brie.
- **Blue Ledge Farm, Salisbury: Camembrie.** A cow's-milk cheese whose name nods to both Camembert and Brie. The milk comes from twenty Normande cows on a seasonal farm two miles away. (The farm's page doesn't state the rind type; confirm.)
- **A cheese that was lost: Ploughgate Creamery's Hartwell.** Marisa Mauro's organic Ayrshire Camembert-style cheese won a blue ribbon at the American Cheese Society. A fire in 2011 stopped production. (Her washed-rind Willoughby was revived by Jasper Hill; see the washed-rind file.)

Nearly all are pasteurized, small and young, and many are named for a local person, peak or pond.

## Story seeds

- **The cheese of the busy woman.** The bloomy rind began as the answer to a woman with a cow, a dozen jobs and half a pound of cheese per milking. A maker with a crowded day can stand in that line without claiming any historical method. (Chosen Ancestors.)
- **It used to be gray.** For most of history these rinds were gray-green and blue-spotted. The pure white rind is a single mold strain picked around 1900. A maker who lets other molds in, or uses a different culture, has a real story about what "natural" looked like. (The Living Thing; Why It Tastes Like This.)
- **Outside in.** The rind ripens the cheese from the edge toward the middle. Showing a cut wheel at two ages (chalky core vs. runny line) is the most demonstrable sensory proof this style offers. (Why It Tastes Like This; The Hands.)
- **When to eat it.** Bloomy rinds have a peak window. Telling the buyer exactly when, and what changes, is accuracy the research rewards: the strongest pleasure comes when the cheese matches or slightly beats the description. (For the Table.)
- **The near-market cheese.** Brie lived because Paris was close, and soft cheese can't travel far or wait long. A Vermont maker selling to Boston or New York has a modern version of that geography. (The Working Landscape.)
- **The rescue.** Harbison came from a batch that was too firm. Bloomy rinds are fragile, and every maker has lost batches. A real failure in the maker's own words is worth more than polish. (The Hockey Puck; never invented.)
- **The box.** The poplar box was a fix for moving a fragile cheese by rail. If the maker uses a box, bark or wrap, ask why.
- **Name it for someone.** Vermont bloomy rinds are often named for a local scout, a grandmother, a peak or a pond. (The Name.)
- **Against the giant.** French Brie and Camembert are global industrial products now. A small Vermont bloomy rind sits against Lactalis, not against a French farmhouse. (The Upstart; pair any award with a sensory line.)

## Careful

- **The Marie Harel story is a legend.** The 1791 tale of a priest teaching Harel to make Camembert is the "official legend" told at the Camembert museum, as *Saveur* puts it. Camembert is named in print by 1708. *Saveur* also notes that Harel came from the neighboring village of Crouttes. Kindstedt doesn't mention Harel. If used, tell it as a legend. A true story about the legend: her statue in Vimoutiers was started with an American admirer's donation in 1926 and, after the 1944 bombing, replaced with money from Borden employees in Van Wert, Ohio.
- **Charlemagne did not prove Brie is ancient.** Kindstedt says the Notker story names neither the place nor the cheese clearly enough to call it Brie. Others have claimed it was a blue cheese or even Roquefort. Androuet says local abbey origins in the 600s can't be proven from the archives. Say "a soft cheese," not "Brie."
- **The Congress of Vienna story** has Talleyrand or Metternich in the lead and varying details. Treat it as tradition.
- **Kindstedt's peasant-farm origin is a reconstruction** he calls conjectural. Say "probably" or "Kindstedt argues."
- **"Penicillium candidum" and "P. camemberti"** are the same mold in trade use. "Around 1900" is the researchers' approximation, not a documented event.
- **Triple-crème fat thresholds vary by source** (72% vs. 75% fat in dry matter). Don't print a number unless the maker uses one.
- **Rind types are often blurred.** *culture* notes that Moses Sleeper picks up some orange *B. linens* bacteria as it ripens; Vermont Creamery's cheeses are *Geotrichum*, not *P. camemberti*. Use the maker's own description.
- **Company claims are the company's own,** including Jasper Hill's name stories and "inspired by Brie."
- **Don't imply a raw-milk young bloomy rind** unless the maker confirms it and how it's legal. US rules require 60 days of aging for raw-milk cheese.
- **Don't say "Brie-like" by reflex,** and don't use "creamy" without saying what ripeness and texture you mean.
